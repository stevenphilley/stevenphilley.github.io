Letting the planet light the way

Educational essay · DarkSky-cited · Not medical advice · Updated Sep 2026

Artificial light at night is useful — and it can also be a pollutant. Skyglow, glare, and trespass

DarkSky defines light pollution as the human-made alteration of outdoor light levels from those occurring naturally. The ordinary vocabulary breaks into a few components — not scare terms, just ways to name what your eyes already notice on a clear suburban night.

Skyglow

Brightening of the night sky over inhabited areas — the orange or white haze that washes out stars. Upward and poorly aimed light scatters in the troposphere (aerosols, humidity, haze) and returns as a glow.

Glare

Excessive brightness that causes visual discomfort. Unshielded fixtures and blue-rich LEDs punch the eye; older drivers often feel this first. More lumens aimed at your face is not the same as safer seeing.

Trespass

Light falling where it is not intended or needed — a neighbor’s window, a bedroom, a creek corridor. Often fixable with a shield, aim, or timer rather than a brighter bulb.

DarkSky also lists clutter — bright, confusing groups of sources. The 2016 World Atlas of Artificial Night Sky Brightness (Falchi et al.) estimated that about 80% of the world population, and 99% in the U.S. and Europe, live under light-polluted skies. That is a measurement story, not a vibe.

Wildlife

Night is not empty habitat. Migratory birds use stars and polarized skylight; many insects navigate by the Moon; bats, amphibians, and coastal species time feeding and nesting to darkness. Artificial light at night has no natural analog at city scale — it is a novel cue layered on top of the day/night cycle that evolution expected.

Documented patterns (agency and review literature, not street folklore): birds drawn off course by urban skyglow and brightly lit towers; insects trapped at lamps (with knock-on effects for pollination and food webs); sea-turtle hatchlings disoriented by beachfront lighting. Cities are often stopover points for migrants, so warm, shielded, and timed light matters even far from “wilderness.”

Human circadian & sleep literacy

Educational · Not medical advice · Not a diagnosis

Humans keep a roughly 24-hour circadian clock. Short-wavelength (“blue-rich”) light is especially effective at shifting that clock and suppressing melatonin in experimental settings — which is why DarkSky and many lighting standards push warmer outdoor spectra at night. Indoor screens and bedroom spill from outdoor fixtures are part of the same physical story: light is a biological signal, not only a visibility tool.

Public-health agencies and medical organizations have discussed artificial light at night in the context of sleep disruption and circadian misalignment. Treat that as literacy: reduce unnecessary night exposure where you can, prefer warmer and dimmer outdoor spectra, and ask a clinician about personal sleep or health concerns. This page does not diagnose, treat, or prescribe.

Honest limit. Population studies and lab melatonin data are not the same as “this streetlight caused my insomnia.” Dose, timing, spectrum, and individual factors all matter. Cite the sources; don’t invent clinical certainty.

Practical fixes

DarkSky’s solutions page and Approved Luminaires program converge on a short, actionable list. Useful light can stay. Waste light can go.

  • Shield / full cutoff. Fixtures that hide the lamp from the side and send light down — zero (or near-zero) uplight. Retrofit shields and better aim often beat buying a brighter bulb.
  • ≤3000 K CCT. DarkSky Approved outdoor luminaires cap correlated color temperature at nominal 3000 K (warmer / amber preferred in sensitive habitat). Cool “daylight” LEDs scatter farther and glare harder at night.
  • Timers, dimmers, sensors. Light only when and where needed. Motion sensors for paths; curfews for facade washes and sports fields; dimming after midnight when traffic drops.
  • Right amount. Task lux, not parking-lot noon. Overlighting wastes energy and worsens skyglow without automatically improving safety.
  • Look for DarkSky Approved. Third-party photometric review for shielding and spectrum — stronger than a retail “dark sky friendly” sticker. DarkSky International is the current name of the former IDA.

Five words that travel well: useful, targeted, low, controlled, warm — the same hierarchy DarkSky teaches homeowners and cities.

A light Bay Area angle

From Campbell and the South Bay, the Milky Way is usually a rumor. Valley haze, marine-layer moisture, and dense street / commercial lighting build a persistent skyglow dome over San Jose and the wider metro — the same physics that softens stars from any mid-latitude suburb, just denser. Hills and parks toward the Santa Cruz Mountains can be darker; coastal fog can either mute or redistribute glow depending on the night.

This is literacy, not a zoning rant. Practical local moves look ordinary: shield a porch flood, swap a cool LED for ≤3000 K, put holiday lights on a timer, close curtains so indoor light does not become outdoor trespass, and notice whether a fixture lights the sidewalk or the bedroom across the street. For the sky you are trying to see, start at the local sky desk; for the air the light scatters in, see atmosphere.
