Layer by layer

Educational essay · Cited ranges · Not a buying guide · September 2026

Additive manufacturing builds a solid by adding material, usually one thin layer at a time, from a digital model. The public name “3D printing” covers that whole family. The machines do not share one plastic, one nozzle, or one idea of accuracy. A desktop that melts a spool of polylactic acid and a factory cell that melts metal powder are both additive, and they are barely the same tool. This page is a map of the standard process families and of the plastics and resins people actually feed them, with temperatures only where a public source states a range.

Adding, not cutting

A mill starts with a block and throws away what is not the part. Additive manufacturing starts with almost nothing and puts material only where the geometry says it should be. The model is sliced into layers. The machine then deposits, fuses, jets, or cures one layer, shifts by the layer thickness, and repeats. Supports, leftover powder, or a vat of unused liquid hold up overhangs that have nothing under them yet. When the job ends, that extra material comes off, and many processes still want a cure, a sinter, or a wash.

The origin people mean is stereolithography in the 1980s. Chuck Hull coined the term in 1984 in a patent application for curing liquid photopolymer with light; US 4,575,330 was granted in 1986. In the same decade Carl Deckard and Joseph Beaman developed selective laser sintering, with defense-research sponsorship. R. F. Housholder had patented a related powder process in 1979 that was not commercialized. Filament extrusion of thermoplastic arrived in the late 1980s and was sold commercially in 1992. After those patents expired, desktop machines became ordinary. The generic name is fused filament fabrication (FFF). Standards writers use the wider label material extrusion, which also covers pellets and pastes.

Seven families, one vocabulary. ISO/ASTM 52900, as summarized in the public process survey, groups additive manufacturing into binder jetting, directed energy deposition, material extrusion, material jetting, powder bed fusion, sheet lamination, and vat photopolymerization. Everything below is one of those seven. Trade names for individual machines come and go. The categories do not.

How the seven processes differ

The useful question is where the material is, and what makes it solid. In extrusion it is a filament or pellet, melted in a hot nozzle. In a vat it is a liquid that light turns into a solid. In a powder bed it is a dry grain, sintered or melted in place, or glued and fired later. Jetting throws either the build material itself or a binder. Directed energy deposition feeds metal into a moving melt pool, often onto a part that already exists. Sheet lamination cuts and stacks sheets. Resolution, strength, and whether the machine belongs on a desk or in a shop follow from that choice.

Material extrusion, mostly filament

Fused filament fabrication is the machine most people have stood next to. A spool feeds thermoplastic into a hot nozzle, a stepper meters the melt, and the head draws a bead that welds, imperfectly, to the bead below. Software plans that path as G-code. As of 2023 this was described as the dominant printing method: the usual home machine, and a factory tool once the polymer gets demanding. The surface you see is the bead, so a finer nozzle looks smoother and takes longer. Layers split before the plastic itself snaps. Steep overhangs need a support, in the same plastic or in a second one that can be dissolved. An open desk handles polylactic acid. ABS wants an enclosure; the compilation suggests a chamber around 40–60 °C. Polycarbonate and the high-temperature polymers want heat a hobby frame may not hold. Pellet extruders, for large parts and recycled flake, are the same family with a different feed.

How the head moves

Four motion systems cover almost every filament printer. None of them changes the polymer. They change speed, size, and how much mass has to reverse.

CartesianThe common “bed-slinger”: the head travels in X, and often Z, while the bed slides in Y. Simple, and the whole part is along for that ride.
CoreXYTwo stationary motors and a crossed belt move the head in the plane. Less moving mass, which is why these frames are associated with higher speed.
DeltaThree arms hang from a triangular top and carry the nozzle. The volume is tall and open. A heavy head on thin arms can ring, and the filament path length changes as the head moves.
BeltThe bed is a conveyor. Parts can be longer than the frame in one direction, or a row of small parts can walk off the end. Less common than the other three.

A bed-moving Cartesian machine is enough for fixtures and anything you will drill later. CoreXY can reverse faster because the motors stay put, if the belt routing is right. A delta buys a tall volume and pays in calibration and vibration. A belt machine is for long sections and queues, not for a small precise part.

Vat photopolymerization

The feedstock is liquid resin. Light crosslinks monomers, often acrylates, and that spot becomes solid. The platform steps one layer, leftover resin drains, and most formulations still need a wash and a final cure. Stereolithography scans a laser. A projector, commonly abbreviated DLP, flashes a whole layer. Masked machines, often called MSLA, put a liquid-crystal panel between a UV backlight and the vat. That last arrangement is what made resin cheap enough for a desk.

A spot or a pixel sets the surface, so fine features are the reason to choose a vat. Standard resin is brittle next to a decent filament. Tough, flexible, castable, and dental grades are different liquids. Castable grades are patterns that are invested and burned out. A biocompatible label belongs to one tested formulation and one kind of contact. Supports still leave marks. Desks run miniatures, jewelry patterns, and dental models. Industrial laser vats chase process control and size. Multiphoton lithography, a research cousin, cures only at a laser focus and has made features under 100 nanometers. It is not a home printer.

Powder bed fusion

A powder bed spreads a thin layer, fuses the cross-section, drops, and spreads again. Loose powder holds overhangs, so the printed supports can almost disappear. The public survey splits the family by how the grains lock.

Polymer laser sintering

Selective laser sintering scans a laser across powder, often PA12, sometimes glass-filled, sometimes PEEK. Deckard’s mid-1980s work is the commercial root. The surface stays slightly sandy. Hinges, ducts, and nested parts in one cake of powder are the usual work: prototypes, and a widening slice of low-volume production. Preheat, the laser, and the loose powder keep it in a shop more often than a spare room.

Fusing agents, no laser

A cousin skips the laser. An inkjet lays a fusing agent where the part should absorb energy, and often a detailing agent at the edge. A heater sweeps the bed and sinters only the treated grains. Loose powder still supports the shape. It is an industrial route: fast layers, nylon-like powders, then a step to recover the cake. Chemistry and heat, not a spool and not a vat.

Metal, melted rather than glued

Laser powder bed fusion melts metal grains into dense parts whose behavior is described as comparable to conventionally processed metal. Electron-beam machines do it in vacuum, often with titanium alloys. The powder is fine and the energy is serious. This is the wrong weekend bracket, and the right part when milling would throw away most of the billet.

Jetting, binders, deposited metal, sheets

Material jetting

Material jetting deposits the build material as droplets, thin enough to leave a nozzle and thick enough to stack. Early work used hot-melt wax; later machines jet photopolymer and cure each pass with ultraviolet light. A second droplet material can form a support that washes or peels away. Layers are thin, and color or flexibility can change inside one part. The machines sit in labs and production cells. Unlike a vat, the head places every drop.

Binder jetting

Binder jetting prints adhesive into powder: metal, ceramic, sand, or polymer, including plaster-like powders for full-color models. The fresh part is a weak green body. Loose powder supported every overhang. An oven burns out binder and sinters the powder, or the pores are filled with wax, a thermoset, or bronze. Sand printers make foundry molds. Sintered metal shrinks. Home use is rare. The powder and the furnace are the real equipment.

Directed energy deposition

Directed energy deposition feeds metal into a melt pool on the part. Powder can be blown into a laser, or wire can be melted by a laser, an electron beam, or a welding arc. Inert gas, or a sealed chamber, keeps air out of the alloy. A tilting head on a multi-axis arm can coat a worn surface or add a boss to a forging. Resolution is coarser than a powder bed. When it works, the result is metal. It is a repair and large-part process, not a desk.

Sheet lamination

Sheet lamination cuts each outline from paper, plastic, or metal and bonds the stack. Paper, cut by blade or laser, can be cheap and wood-like, and it is not a structural polymer. Ultrasonic consolidation bonds metal foil without a full melt. The family is standardized, and it is much less common than extrusion or a powder bed.

Plastics and resins

Filament melts and freezes again. Resin, once light crosslinks it, generally does not. Nozzle and bed figures in the table are typical ranges from the public filament compilation, not a setpoint for one spool. Heat figures are a glass transition or a melting point where a polymer article states one. A dash means the column does not apply, or these sources do not give a single number.

PLA and PETG

Polylactic acid is the default teaching plastic: low difficulty, a glass transition of 60–65 °C, and brittle, with elongation at break under 10 percent for the plain polymer. A hot car can slump it. Some heat-resistant grades are described as surviving 110 °C. It comes from feedstocks such as corn and biodegrades only in specific industrial conditions. Lactide shows up in its emissions. It is the lower-emission everyday choice, and it still emits. PETG is listed as more flexible than PLA or ABS, durable, and medium difficulty. These sources give it no single heat-deflection figure. Reach for it when PLA snaps. The melt is sticky and it strings.

ABS and ASA

ABS is rubber-toughened, with a glass transition near 105 °C and medium difficulty. It shrinks as it cools, so a draft splits layers, which is why the compilation pairs it with a warm chamber. Styrene has been measured from ABS and from high-impact polystyrene. ASA was developed as the weatherable, ultraviolet-resistant outdoor alternative. It is still a styrene copolymer, and it still wants an enclosure and a vent.

Flexibles, nylon, polycarbonate

TPU and TPE print rubber-like gaskets and wearables, both at high difficulty, and both slowly. Soft filament buckles in a loose extruder. TPU is noted for abrasion and oil resistance. Neither replaces a stiff part. Nylon is a family, medium difficulty, and the spool has to stay sealed. PA12, the usual powder-bed nylon, melts at 178–180 °C. PA11 melts at 180–190 °C, with a glass transition near 42 °C, and it holds size better after taking on water. The nozzle in the table sits above those melting points so the melt can pass a small hole. Wet nylon prints as bubbles. Polycarbonate is tougher and sometimes clear, glass transition near 147 °C, medium difficulty. Open frames usually fail it. Some grades can carry residual bisphenol A.

Fibers, supports, and the hot polymers

Short carbon or glass fiber mostly buys stiffness, and it wears a brass nozzle, so the compilation says to use a hardened one. The heat limit is still the base polymer. PLA-filled grades are the easy row. ABS-filled grades are medium. High-impact polystyrene is the dissolvable support beside ABS, and one of the styrene emitters. PVA is water-soluble and as thirsty as nylon. It washes away as support for PLA or ABS. Humidity turns it to gel.

PEEK, PEKK, and PEI need another kind of machine. PEEK’s glass transition is about 143 °C and it melts near 343 °C; some grades are described as useful up to 250 °C. PEKK’s glass transition is 162 °C, with chemical resistance beside the heat. PEI’s glass transition is 215 °C. The table’s “medium” on PEI only makes sense on a machine that can hold a 220 °C chamber. Ducts, sterilizable fixtures, and chemical duty are why the three exist.

Photopolymer resins

Standard resin favors detail and chips if you drop it. Tough grades add impact resistance. Flexible grades are not TPU. Castable grades are patterns that get invested and burned out. A dental or biocompatible label applies only to a formulation tested for a stated contact. None of them has a nozzle temperature. All of them are a skin hazard while liquid. A NIOSH-authored survey of light-curing resins for vats and for material jetting found irritants and sensitizers that the safety data sheets only partly declared.

Air, skin, and powder

A hot spool is a small chemical plant. The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency reports volatile organic compounds, some hazardous to inhale, and ultrafine particles about 1–100 nanometers, small enough to deposit deep in the lung. PLA and ABS are the common cases, and filaments do not emit equally. Specialty filaments with metal or flame-retardant additives can add exposure. Printers now sit in classrooms; published respiratory studies have focused on adults. The practical summary is: lower-emitting materials, an enclosure, ventilation that captures the plume, and less time beside a running machine.

In a chamber study of desktop filament printers, Azimi and colleagues (2016) found median ultrafine-particle rates from about 108 to about 1011 particles per minute, set mostly by the filament and less by bed temperature. The largest organic rates they quantified were caprolactam from nylon-based and from wood-like or mineral-like filled filaments (about 2 to 180 micrograms per minute), styrene from ABS and high-impact polystyrene (about 10 to 110), and lactide from PLA (about 4 to 5). Many of those combinations do not belong in a poorly ventilated room, or behind a filter that catches only particles or only gases. NIOSH puts the same warning in plainer words: particles under 100 nanometers are on the order of two thousand times narrower than a hair, and invisible. PLA often emits fewer particles than ABS and still emits. Prefer fresh air, a hood, or a garage over a bedroom. Smell is a late alarm.

A two-lab pilot (Felici and colleagues, 2023) found higher nanoparticle counts beside a stereolithography printer running resin (about 4,091 per cubic centimeter) than beside a filament printer running PLA (about 2,203). That is one comparison, and it is enough to treat resin as an air problem too.

Uncured resin is the skin problem. Methacrylates in printing resins have caused allergic contact dermatitis in case reports. A NIOSH survey of 39 light-curing products, for vats and for material jetting, found many more irritants and sensitizers than the safety data sheets listed. Gloves, eye protection, and a bench that is not the kitchen table are the minimum. Do not drive a splash into the skin with a random solvent. Cure waste before it is trash. A rash that returns is a reason to stop and get medical advice. The next exposure is worse, not easier.

Powder-bed jobs end as a cake of fine loose particles. Sweeping that cake into the room turns a solid into something you can inhale. Polymer and metal powder work belongs in an enclosed machine, with a shop procedure for spills and filters. It is not a dustpan hobby.

Choosing without a catalog

Match the shape to a process, the duty to a plastic, and both to the room. Brackets, jigs, knobs, and drafts are filament jobs. PLA if the part stays cool. PETG for a little toughness. ASA in the sun. ABS only with an enclosure, a vent, and a reason to want a glass transition near 105 °C. TPU when it must bend, slowly. Nylon if you will dry the spool. Fiber-filled filament when you want stiffness and you own a hardened nozzle. PEEK, PEKK, and PEI only on a machine that can hold the table’s temperatures.

Use a vat when the surface and the small letters matter more than impact: a pattern, a dental model, a fine housing. Budget gloves, a wash, a cure lamp, and air, not just the printer. Use polymer powder bed fusion, usually nylon, when the shape is full of channels and a shop can run the cake. The sandy skin is part of the deal. Use metal laser powder bed fusion for dense complex metal, binder jetting plus a furnace when sintering and shrinkage are acceptable, and directed energy deposition to add metal onto a part you already have. Sheet lamination is for paper models or foil, not the default strong part.

Motion is the second choice. A Cartesian bed-slinger is enough to learn. CoreXY is the upgrade when speed and a quiet bed matter. A delta is a tall volume with a calibration habit. A belt is for long parts and queues. No motion system makes ABS safe in a closed bedroom, and none of them makes uncured resin safe on bare skin.
