White dwarfs,
neutron stars

A white dwarf is held up by electron degeneracy pressure. The electrons are packed so tightly that the Pauli principle, not the heat, sets how fast they move. Add mass and the star gets smaller, because more weight needs more crowding. Nauenberg’s 1972 zero-temperature relation makes that explicit. With the magnetic energy set to zero, R/R☉ = (0.0225 / μe) √[1 − (M/MCh)4/3] / (M/MCh)1/3 and MCh/M☉ = 5.82 / μe². Helium, carbon, and oxygen have μe = 2, so the limit on this formula is 1.455 M☉. That is the Chandrasekhar limit here. Iron-56 has μe = 56/26, and the limit drops. A neutron star is the next remnant, if the core collapses and protons and electrons become neutrons. Neutron degeneracy, and the strong force, hold up a city-sized ball. This page does not pick an equation of state for that interior. The shaded band is the Miller et al. 2021 ±1σ span at 1.4 solar masses, across three frameworks: 11.79–13.11 km. c = 299,792,458 m/s. One nominal solar mass uses GM☉ = 1.3271244×10²⁰ m³ s⁻². Density divides by the 2022 CODATA G = 6.67430×10⁻¹¹. The spin of one of these stars is the pulsar desk. If the pressure fails, the horizon is the black-hole desk.

Zero-temperature white dwarfs, and a neutron-star radius that is a measured span rather than an equation of state. Nauenberg’s 1972 relation is equations (54)–(56) with the magnetic energy set to zero. It is not a cooling model. Coulomb corrections and general relativity are left out, and both move the maximum mass. Escape speed is the Newtonian square root of 2GM/R, reported as a fraction of c. It is not a general-relativistic result. The neutron-star band is Miller et al. 2021’s ±1σ span at 1.4 solar masses, 11.79–13.11 km, across three frameworks. It is not a curve of radius against mass. Named medians are the papers’. The teaspoon mass is the density on the card times one US teaspoon. Not a stellar model, not a dense-matter inference, and not a pulsar timer.

Nauenberg’s 1972 zero-temperature relation, μ_e = 2, the value for helium, carbon, and oxygen, gives a radius of 0.007800 R☉ · 5,427 km · 0.852 R⊕. The Chandrasekhar mass on this formula is 1.455 M☉. Mean density is 3.024×10⁶ g cm⁻³. One US teaspoon of that density is 14,905 kg (14.91 t). Surface gravity is 4.588×10⁶ m s⁻² · 4.678×10⁵ g. Escape speed is 0.024 c. Compactness GM/(Rc²) is 2.77×10⁻⁴. A black hole of this mass has compactness 0.5 and a Schwarzschild radius of 3.006 km, so this radius is 1,805 times that horizon. The horizon itself is the black-hole desk. The same paper’s STIS radius is 0.008098 R☉ · 5,634 km, internal uncertainty ±0.000046 R☉. This page’s log g from that published mass and radius is 8.629. Bond et al. print 8.629. The model radius is not that STIS radius. The curve is colder and simpler than the star.

Everyday comparison

One teaspoon

One US teaspoon is 4.92892159375 cm³, from a gallon of 231 cubic inches, 128 fluid ounces to the gallon, and six teaspoons to the fluid ounce, with the inch at 0.0254 m. At the density on this card, that volume has a mass of 14,905 kg (14.91 t).

The mass changes when the density changes. A canned “billion tons” is not used. The number on this card is density times that volume.

What the words mean

Degenerate matter
Matter whose pressure comes from the Pauli principle. Two fermions cannot share a state. Pack them tighter and they must take higher momentum. That momentum is a pressure even when the temperature is zero.
Electron degeneracy
The pressure that holds a white dwarf up. At low mass the electrons are non-relativistic and the radius falls about as M to the power −1/3. Near the limit the electrons move near c, and the pressure does not stiffen fast enough to hold more mass.
Chandrasekhar limit
The greatest mass this zero-temperature electron gas can support. On Nauenberg’s printed coefficients it is 5.82 / μe² solar masses. μe = 2 gives 1.455. Textbooks often round that familiar limit to about 1.4. More nucleons per electron means fewer electrons for the same mass, so iron’s limit is lower. Real stars also feel Coulomb forces and general relativity, which this curve leaves out.
μe
Nucleons per electron in a fully ionized gas, A/Z. Helium-4, carbon-12, and oxygen-16 all have A/Z = 2. Iron-56 has 56/26. The button switches only that number. It does not load a cooling track.
Compactness
GM/(Rc²). A Schwarzschild black hole, with R = 2GM/c², has compactness 1/2. Radius divided by that horizon is 1/(2 × compactness). The black-hole desk keeps the photon sphere and the ISCO. This page only borrows the horizon as a ruler.
Neutron-star radius
The nuclear equation of state is not settled, so this page does not draw one curve. Miller et al. 2021, after folding in NICER radii, high pulsar masses, and two gravitational-wave tidal measurements, find that the ±1σ radii at 1.4 solar masses from three different frameworks all sit inside 11.79–13.11 km. Presets replace that band with a paper’s median radius. PSR J0030+0451 and PSR J0740+6620 are those medians. Their pulses, as clocks, are the pulsar desk.

Sources

Where the numbers come from

  • Nauenberg 1972, ApJ 175, 417 — analytic mass–radius relation for a zero-temperature star. Equations (54)–(56). This page sets the magnetic energy to zero and uses the printed coefficients 0.0225 and 5.82. The relation is compared in that paper with Chandrasekhar’s numerical radii. It is not a hot-envelope model.
  • IAU 2015 Resolution B3 (Prša et al. 2016, AJ 152, 41) — nominal solar mass parameter GM☉ = 1.3271244 × 10²⁰ m³ s⁻², and the nominal solar radius 6.957 × 10⁸ m. A conversion constant, not a new measurement of the Sun. The same GM is the black-hole and lensing desks.
  • NIST — Newtonian constant of gravitation — 2022 CODATA G = 6.67430 × 10⁻¹¹ m³ kg⁻¹ s⁻². Used only to turn GM into kilograms for the density. Radii, gravity, escape speed, and compactness use GM and do not divide by G. c = 299,792,458 m/s is exact. Standard gravity 9.80665 m/s² is the conventional value from the 3rd CGPM (1901), for the “g” comparison only.
  • NASA NSSDCA — Earth Fact Sheet — volumetric mean radius 6,371 km. Used only to quote a white-dwarf radius in Earth radii. The same length is on the vis-viva desk.
  • Bond et al. 2017, ApJ 840, 70 — Sirius B dynamical mass 1.018 ± 0.011 M☉. STIS radius 0.008098 ± 0.000046 R☉, internal errors. log g from that mass and radius, 8.629 ± 0.007. Also arXiv:1703.10625. This page recomputes log g from those two numbers and the constants above. The model radius is Nauenberg’s, not the STIS radius.
  • Bond et al. 2015, ApJ 813, 106 — Procyon B dynamical mass 0.592 ± 0.006 M☉. Radius adjusted from Provencal et al. 2002 to their parallax, 0.01232 ± 0.00032 R☉. log g = 8.028 ± 0.023. Also arXiv:1510.00485. This page’s log g from that mass and radius is 8.029.
  • Riley et al. 2019, ApJL 887, L21 — PSR J0030+0451, NICER. For the favored ST+PST model, the 68% credible interval about the median is M = 1.34 (−0.16, +0.15) M☉ and Req = 12.71 (−1.19, +1.14) km. They print compactness GM/(Req c²) = 0.156 (−0.010, +0.008). This page uses the two medians and recomputes 0.156.
  • Miller et al. 2019, ApJL 887, L24 — the companion NICER analysis of the same pulsar. Their three-oval model gives R = 13.02 (−1.06, +1.24) km and M = 1.44 (−0.14, +0.15) M☉ at 68%. Those medians are not a second preset. They are the other reduction of the same data.
  • Fonseca et al. 2021, ApJL — PSR J0740+6620. Model-averaged pulsar mass 2.08 (+0.07, −0.07) M☉ at 68.3% credibility, from the Shapiro delay. arXiv:2104.00880.
  • Riley et al. 2021, ApJL 918, L27 — NICER and XMM-Newton radius of PSR J0740+6620, conditioned on that radio mass. Equatorial radius 12.39 (−0.98, +1.30) km. The inferred mass is dominated by the 2.08 ± 0.07 M☉ prior. This page uses 2.08 and 12.39.
  • Miller et al. 2021, ApJL 918, L28 — the parallel NICER and XMM-Newton analysis, and the equation-of-state summary. Table 4 note: the final ±1σ radius range for a 1.4 M☉ star, spanning all three EOS frameworks, is 11.79–13.11 km. That span is the band. It is not one of the three curves.

How this desk works

A white dwarf shrinks as you add mass. A neutron star’s radius, on this page, does not. The band is a measurement at 1.4 solar masses, not an equation of state.
