Angular size
why the Moon and Sun match

A disc’s apparent size is an angle. For a sphere of physical diameter d at distance D from the observer to the center, that angle is δ = 2·arctan(d / 2D). The small-angle line beside it is d/D in radians. The Moon’s volumetric mean diameter, twice the Moon Fact Sheet radius 1,737.4 km, is 3,474.8 km. At mean perigee, 0.3633 × 106 km, the disc is 32.880′ (0.5480°). The Sun’s volumetric mean diameter, twice the Sun Fact Sheet radius 695,700 km, is 1,391,400 km. At Earth’s aphelion, 152.100 × 106 km on the Earth Fact Sheet, the solar disc is 31.448′ (0.5241°). That Moon is wider: ratio 1.0455, so a perfect lineup can be total. At mean apogee, 0.4055 × 106 km, the Moon is 29.458′ and a perihelion Sun is 32.518′. Ratio 0.9059. The Moon no longer covers it, and the lineup is annular. The tilt that makes most new Moons miss is the eclipses desk. The phase that night is the Moon desk. The peek past the limb is libration.

Classroom schematic. One angle, drawn as a disc scaled to δ. A dashed ring, when the angle is small enough to share the frame, is 0.5°. The discs are the angle, not a photograph and not a map. Fact-sheet presets use the published radii and distances named above. Thumb, coin, and the half-degree mark are approximate examples, not measurements. Not an ephemeris. Not a prediction of the next eclipse.

Same Moon, two distances

Supermoon and micromoon

A full Moon near perigee is sometimes called a supermoon, and one near apogee a micromoon. Those are popular names, not fact-sheet terms. The size difference here is computed from the Moon Fact Sheet, not quoted from memory. Mean perigee is 0.3633 × 106 km and the disc is 32.880′. Mean apogee is 0.4055 × 106 km and the disc is 29.458′. The angular-diameter ratio is 1.1162, so the perigee disc is 11.616% wider. Area scales with the square of that ratio, so the perigee disc covers 24.580% more of the sky.

The sheet’s footnote says the distance roughly ranges from 357,000 km to 407,000 km over the year, wider than the table’s mean perigee and mean apogee. The same diameter at those two footnote distances makes the nearer disc 14.005% wider. That is a different pair of numbers. This page’s 11.616% uses the table.

The formula

δ = 2·arctan(d / 2D). d is the physical diameter. D is the distance from the observer to the center. The result is in radians, then converted to degrees, arcminutes, and arcseconds. This is the exact angular diameter of a sphere seen from outside.

δ ≈ d/D. The small-angle line, in radians. For the Moon at mean perigee it sits 0.0008% above the exact angle. It is the right classroom shortcut near half a degree. It drifts when the object fills more of the view: if d = D, the exact angle is 53.1301° and d/D is 1 radian, 57.2958°, several percent high.

The inputs. Moon volumetric mean radius 1,737.4 km, so the diameter used here is 3,474.8 km. Equatorial radius 1,738.1 km is on the same sheet and is not the diameter in the presets. Mean perigee 0.3633 × 106 km. Mean apogee 0.4055 × 106 km. Semimajor axis 0.3844 × 106 km. Sun volumetric mean radius 695,700 km, so the diameter is 1,391,400 km. Earth perihelion 147.095 × 106 km. Aphelion 152.100 × 106 km. Semimajor axis 149.598 × 106 km. At the two semimajor axes the Moon is 31.075′ and the Sun is 31.974′. Ratio 0.9719, slightly under 1. They look the same size, and a mean lineup is annular.

Ratio greater than 1, and less than 1. Divide the Moon’s angular diameter by the Sun’s. Above 1, the Moon can cover the photosphere: a total solar eclipse along the center of a perfect lineup. Below 1, a ring of Sun remains: an annular eclipse. With these mean perigee and apogee distances, every perigee pairing on the card is total and every apogee pairing is annular. The discs match at 367,346 km when the Sun is at perihelion, and at 379,846 km when the Sun is at aphelion. Angular size does not say whether the new Moon is on the line. The orbit is tilted about 5°. That question is the eclipses desk.

Approximate examples. A thumb about 2 cm wide at about 57 cm is 2.0102°. A coin about 2.4 cm across at about 70 cm is 1.9642°. A 5 mm mark at about 57 cm is 0.5026°, the half-degree rule of thumb. 57 cm is the convenient length where 1 cm subtends about 1°, because 1° is about 1/57 of a radian. None of these is a measured hand or a mint specification. They are classroom marks, so a 2° thumb reads as about four times the Moon.

The sheet’s own apparent diameters. The Moon sheet prints 1,896″ at 378,000 km. This diameter gives 1,896.09″, which rounds to that line. The Sun sheet prints a maximum of 1,952″, a minimum of 1,887″, and 1,919″ at 1 AU. This radius at Earth’s perihelion, aphelion, and semimajor axis gives 1,951.08″, 1,886.88″, and 1,918.44″. The minimum rounds to the sheet’s 1,887″. The other two sit under the sheet’s integers by less than 1″. The presets use the radii and the distances. They do not adjust a radius to force the integer.

Literacy · an angle, not a forecast

Angular diameter
The angle from one limb to the other. δ = 2·arctan(d / 2D). A bigger world farther away can match a smaller world nearby. That is the Moon and the Sun.
Small angle
d/D in radians. Near half a degree it matches the exact angle to a thousandth of a percent. The gap shows up when d is a large fraction of D.
Total
Moon / Sun angular diameter greater than 1. The Moon’s disc can cover the Sun. Mean perigee does that against both perihelion and aphelion, on these fact-sheet distances.
Annular
Ratio less than 1. The Moon is too small and a ring of Sun remains. Mean apogee does that against both ends of Earth’s orbit. The semimajor-axis pair, ratio 0.9719, is annular too.
Supermoon
A popular name for a full Moon near perigee. From the table’s mean perigee and mean apogee, the perigee disc is 11.616% wider and 24.580% larger in area. The footnote’s 357,000 km to 407,000 km span is 14.005% in angular diameter. Different pair.
What the discs leave out
The 5° tilt, the nodes, the shadow on Earth, and the exact distance of a given month. A ratio above 1 is permission for a total eclipse, not a date. Everyday sizes are approximate on purpose. Not an ephemeris. Not a photograph. Earthrise preview: NASA / Bill Anders (public domain).
