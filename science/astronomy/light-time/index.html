Light time
across the system

Pick a date and two bodies. The distance between them is divided by the speed of light. One way is that quotient. The round trip, the wait for a radio answer, is twice it. c = 299,792,458 m/s exactly, the SI defining constant. One astronomical unit is 149,597,870,700 m exactly, the IAU 2012 value, so 1 AU of light time is 499.005 seconds, 8.317 minutes, about 8.3 minutes. Planet places are the JPL approximate Keplerian elements of Standish and Williams, Table 1, valid 1800–2050. The Moon is held at the fact-sheet mean, 384,400 km, and the perigee 363,300 km and apogee 405,500 km are labeled, not solved. A Mars rover cannot be joysticked live: Earth–Mars runs roughly 3 to 22 light-minutes. The Moon’s mean round trip is about 2.5 seconds. The year of a circular solar orbit is the Kepler desk. The milliseconds of a station overhead are the satellites desk.

The delay is the straight-line distance at that instant divided by c. The bodies are not moved forward by the flight time, and there is no Shapiro delay. Published heliocentric distance tolerances for 1800–2050 run from 1 × 1000 km (Mercury) to 1,500 × 1000 km (Saturn). Mars is 25 × 1000 km. Jupiter is 600 × 1000 km. Classroom geometry · not a Horizons state

A line and a constant

Nothing here is a launch window, a station schedule, or a spacecraft range. The positions are a Keplerian fit, adjusted for a best match over 1800–2050, and JPL says the elements are not valid outside that interval. The light time is distance over c at one instant. A real ephemeris iterates, because the target moves while the signal is on the way. Horizons is that ephemeris. This page is the size of the delay.

What this means

You cannot joystick a Mars rover. Earth–Mars on this model runs roughly 3 to 22 light-minutes, one way, across 1800–2050. The round trip is twice that. A stick moved now would be old news when it arrived, and the picture of what happened would be older still. Commands are sequences, written for the geometry they will meet. The card’s closest and farthest are one synodic period around the date you picked, about 780 days, not every opposition in the table.

The Moon is a pause, not a blackout. At 384,400 km the round trip is 2.564 seconds, about 2.5 seconds. A sentence can be answered, and the gap before the reply starts is that long. Perigee, 363,300 km, and apogee, 405,500 km, move it by a fraction of a second. The Moon Fact Sheet also says the distance roughly ranges from 357,000 km to 407,000 km over a year. This page does not solve which of those today is. The circle is the mean.

One astronomical unit is a clock. 149,597,870,700 m divided by 299,792,458 m/s is 499.005 seconds, 8 min 19.0 s, 8.317 minutes. Sunlight is that old when Earth is exactly 1 AU out. The barycenter wanders from about 8.2 to about 8.5 minutes because the orbit is eccentric. Farther out, the same constant turns a distance into a wait of hours. Jupiter’s published tolerance, 600 × 1000 km, is about 2 seconds of light beside a delay of tens of minutes.

Spacecraft past the planets are not given a number here. Voyager 1 and Voyager 2 have a live range on the Voyager status page and on NASA’s Voyager mission page. This desk does not copy those figures. When a distance has to be exact, the tool is Horizons.

Literacy · c, the au, and the fit

Speed of light
c = 299,792,458 m/s exactly. The metre is defined by fixing that number. A radio signal in vacuum and a pulse of light take the same time. The satellites desk uses the same c for a hop straight down to the ground, which is milliseconds.
Astronomical unit
1 au = 149,597,870,700 m exactly, IAU 2012 Resolution B2. It is a defined length, not this year’s measured Earth–Sun distance. Light crosses it in 499.005 seconds, 8.317 minutes. The Kepler desk measures orbits in this unit.
One way and round trip
One way is the geometric distance divided by c. The round trip is twice that, the wait if a signal goes out and the answer comes straight back along the same line. A tracking pass is not always that straight line. This page draws the straight line.
Table 1
Six Keplerian elements and their rates per century, for 1800–2050, from Standish and Williams via JPL. Mean anomaly, then Kepler’s equation, then a rotation into the J2000 ecliptic. Table 2, and the extra terms that belong to it, are not used. Outside 1800–2050 the slider stops.
The Moon’s circle
Semimajor axis 384,400 km, sidereal period 27.3217 days, inclination 5.145° to the ecliptic. The node is held fixed, as on the solar map. That is a direction for the diagram. The Earth–Moon distance on the card does not change with the date. Perigee and apogee are the fact sheet.
What the number leaves out
Light-time iteration, aberration, the Shapiro delay, and the few thousand kilometres between Earth’s center and the Earth–Moon barycenter. The barycenter is what Table 1 calls Earth. Pluto is the historical row the solar map already uses, semimajor axis 39.482 AU, and the current JPL page publishes no Pluto error. A spacecraft distance is a link, not a stored figure.
