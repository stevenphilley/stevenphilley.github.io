Proper motion
& space velocity

A nearby star is not painted on a sphere. Proper motion μ is how far it drifts across the sky in a year. The tangential speed is vt = 4.74047 · μ[″/yr] · d[pc], in kilometres per second. The 4.74047 is one astronomical unit per Julian year: 149,597,870.7 km divided by 365.25 × 86,400 s is 4.74047046 km/s. That quotient rounds to 4.74047 at five decimals. The calculator uses the quotient. Distance is a parallax. The third component is the radial velocity, positive when the star recedes. The space velocity is √(vt² + vr²). Edmond Halley in 1718 compared declinations Ptolemy had recorded and found that Sirius, Arcturus, and Aldebaran had moved. E. E. Barnard in 1916 published a star at about 10.3″ a year. Gaia DR3 gives that star a total of 10.393349″/yr. At 1.828234 pc the tangential speed is 90.076 km/s, and with a radial velocity of −110.468 km/s the space velocity is 142.537 km/s.

Educational schematic. Not a catalog reduction and not an orbit. Proper motion, parallax, and radial velocity are combined as a straight line at constant speed relative to the Sun. Galactic acceleration, perspective acceleration, and the Sun’s motion are left out. The closest-approach time is that linear approximation. Formal errors are not folded in.

Barnard's Star’s Gaia DR3 motion is 10.393349″/yr, about 10″ a year. Over the 100 yr span that is 1,039.3″, a bit more than half of a 0.5° full-Moon width (1,800″). E. E. Barnard’s 1916 comparisons were about 10.30″ and 10.36″ a year. They are not substituted for the Gaia total. The Big Dipper’s outline changes on a timescale of order 100,000 years. Approaching, so the straight-line minimum is in the future. 9,719.8 yr from now the distance is 1.155347 pc. Linear approximation: t = − d vr / (vt² + vr²), and rmin = d vt / √(vt² + vr²). One parsec at 1 km/s is 977,792.22 Julian years.

vt = 4.74047046 × 10.393349 × 1.828234 = 90.076 km/s. The five-decimal factor 4.74047 times those same printed μ and distance also rounds to 90.076 km/s.

What the three numbers are

Proper motion
μ is an angle per year, not a speed. Catalogs list μα* and μδ. The star is already μα cos δ in the first number. The total is the root sum square. A large μ usually means the star is nearby, or fast, or both. Barnard's Star is both.
The 4.74047
One AU per year, in km/s. The IAU astronomical unit is 149,597,870.7 km. A Julian year is 365.25 × 86,400 s. Divide. The quotient is 4.74047046 km/s. Five-decimal rounding is 4.74047. The calculator uses the quotient. 4.74 is the same constant with fewer decimals. It is not substituted.
Three components
Parallax gives the distance, so μ becomes vt across the line of sight. Radial velocity is the component along it. The space velocity is the hypotenuse. The angle from the outward line of sight is atan2(vt, vr). 0° is straight away. 90° is across the sky. 180° is straight toward the Sun.
Closest approach
Hold the Sun still and let the star coast in a straight line. Then t = − d vr / (vt² + vr²) and rmin = d vt / √(vt² + vr²). A negative vr puts t in the future. The conversion is 977,792.22 Julian years for one parsec at 1 km/s. This is a linear approximation. It is not an orbit, and the Sun is not actually fixed.
A full-Moon width
Half a degree is 1,800″. This page uses that round width for “one full-Moon width.” The angular-size desk has the fact-sheet lunar diameters, which are not 1,800″ and are not substituted here. One degree is 3,600″. Years equal that angle divided by μ.
