Solar system
map

A clickable schematic of our neighborhood — Sun through Neptune, with Pluto labeled as a dwarf planet. It opens in 3D (drag to orbit; the flat map is the 2D control). Set a day and local time (defaults to now) and the planets move to approximate positions for that moment. Play walks the date forward; set the speed (0.5×–10×) to go slower or faster. Also see the Moon phases dial, why that same face still shows past the limb (libration — a locked spin, eccentricity 0.0549, and a lunar equator tilted 6.68° to its orbit), why eclipses are rare (the Moon’s orbit is tilted about 5°, so most new and full Moons miss), why most shores on a smooth-Earth cartoon see about two high tides a day (spring when the Sun lines up, neap at the quarters), and why the year has seasons (axial tilt, not distance), why that axis slowly walks a cone so the pole star changes (precession — about 26,000 years, and the tropical year is shorter than the sidereal year), why the noon Sun traces a figure-8 (analemma — declination and the equation of time), why a sidereal day is shorter than the mean solar day (sidereal — the stars gain about four minutes a night), why new Moon to new Moon outlasts one orbit of the Moon (synodic month — the Moon is back among the stars and the Sun has moved on), why Mars can seem to back up (retrograde — Earth overtaking on a faster inner orbit, not a reversed path), why an outer planet stands opposite the Sun at opposition (Earth between the Sun and Mars — the bright geometry, not a reversed orbit), why a larger orbit takes longer (Kepler — for a circular orbit around the Sun, P² = a³ when P is in years and a is in AU), why a circular orbit is slower than escape by √2 (escape — orbital speed and escape speed at one altitude, Newton’s cannon as a schematic), why the cheap path between two circular orbits is half an ellipse (Hohmann — two impulsive burns, fact-sheet distances, Earth to Mars in 258.88 days), why five points keep a fixed pattern in the rotating frame (Lagrange — L1 through L5, a Hill approximation beside the numerical collinear root, Sun–Earth L2 at 1,501,538 km from Earth), why a moon inside a critical distance cannot hold together (Roche — a fluid limit and a rigid one, Rhea outside, the Moon far outside, Phobos between them), why Venus shows phases (Venus — an orbit inside Earth’s, crescent through gibbous, not a moon), why the other planets do not all do that (planet phases — Mercury’s crescent, Mars’s gibbous disk, Jupiter from Earth and from a spacecraft), then continue with the local sky (city or ZIP, default Campbell), a stars types & lives color lesson, nearest stars, the parallax yardstick that turns Earth’s orbital shift into a parsec, exoplanets, and a Milky Way starter. Light itself — radio through gamma — has a literacy desk at EM spectrum. The force that schedules this map has its own literacy desk: Gravity. Part of the science desk; also see Earth’s atmosphere.

Educational schematic. The 3D view uses NASA JPL approximate Keplerian elements (eccentricity and inclination) for the eight planets. Pluto uses the same style of fit. Distances can be log-compressed or true to the AU. Planet sizes are exaggerated in both views so the bodies stay visible. Moon gaps, when shown, are stretched. The 2D control keeps the original flat map: circular orbits and approximate mean heliocentric longitudes. Not a JPL Horizons ephemeris. Periods and mean distances follow NASA NSSDCA fact sheets.

    Libration

    The same face stays toward Earth. An eccentric orbit and a 6.68° tilt let us peek past the limbs. About 59% over time. A classroom globe, not tonight’s Moon.

    Seasons

    Axial tilt, not distance — walk a year and see which hemisphere leans into the Sun.

    Noon Sun

    Analemma

    The figure-8 of solar declination and the equation of time. Tilt plus an uneven orbit. A classroom schematic, not a sundial.

    The day

    Sidereal

    One spin against the stars is 23.9345 hours. The mean solar day is 24.0000. The stars rise about four minutes earlier.

    The month

    Synodic

    One orbit of the Moon against the stars is 27.3217 days. New Moon to new Moon is 29.53. The Sun has moved on.

    The cone

    Precession

    The axis walks a slow circle. Polaris now; the tropical year is 365.242 days and the sidereal year is 365.256.

    Passing

    Retrograde

    Earth laps Mars on a faster inner orbit. The backward loop is the line of sight, not a reversed path.

    Lineup

    Opposition

    Earth between the Sun and an outer planet. Brightest geometry on the cartoon, not a reversed orbit.

    Phases

    Venus

    Crescent through gibbous from an orbit inside Earth’s. Inferior and superior conjunction, and the elongation stretches.

    The year

    Kepler

    For a circular orbit around the Sun, P² = a³ when P is in years and a is in AU. Drag the distance. The fact-sheet year sits beside the law.

    The speed

    Escape

    Circular orbit and escape at one altitude. √2 apart. Fact-sheet GM and volumetric radius. Earth’s surface escape is 11.186 km/s. A cannon diagram, not a launch.

    The coast

    Hohmann

    The minimum-energy half-ellipse between two circular orbits. Earth to Mars is 258.88 days and 5.594 km/s. Fact-sheet radii and the Sun’s GM. Not a mission.

    The five points

    Lagrange

    L1 through L5 of a two-body system. Sun–Earth L2 is 1,501,538 km from Earth. Earth–Moon L1 is 326,380 km from Earth and 58,020 km from the Moon. Hill approximation beside the numerical root. Not a mission.

    The distance

    Roche

    Fluid limit and rigid limit. Rhea and the Moon sit outside both. Phobos sits between them. Fact-sheet densities and radii. Not an ephemeris.

    Solar map controls

    3D is the default. The 3D / 2D choice is remembered in this browser. If WebGL is unavailable, the flat map is shown on its own. Sizes are exaggerated in every view. This is not a Horizons ephemeris.

    3D orrery

    3D orrery
    3D / 2D3D draws eccentric, inclined orbits. 2D is the original flat map: circular orbits and mean longitude. The choice stays in this browser.
    DragOrbit the view. One finger orbits on a phone.
    Right-dragPan. Two-finger drag pans as well.
    Wheel or pinchZoom toward the pointer. Two fingers pinch.
    OrbitsShow or hide the orbit lines.
    EclipticShow or hide the ecliptic grid.
    MoonsMoon, the Galilean moons, Titan, and Triton. Their gaps from the parent are stretched.
    CompressedLog radius so Neptune sits beside Mercury. The tilt of each orbit is kept.
    True AUReal distances in astronomical units. Inner planets bunch near the Sun. Sizes are still exaggerated.
    TopLook down on the ecliptic.
    EdgeLook across the ecliptic so the inclinations show.
    FollowKeep the camera on the selected body, or on Earth if the Sun is selected.
    Reset viewThree-quarter view in 3D. In 2D it clears pan and zoom.
    Arrow keysOrbit while the 3D map is focused. Plus and minus zoom. 0 or Home resets.
    Click or tapFills the card: distance from the Sun in AU, orbital period, and the current ecliptic position.
