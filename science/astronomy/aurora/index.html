Vol. Science · Astronomy · The shield

Why the polar
sky glows

Earth’s magnetic field is dipole-like, and the solar wind stands off it. NASA Science calls the cavity a magnetosphere: a comet-shaped bubble, generated by the convective motion of charged, molten iron in the outer core. The sun-facing side extends about six to 10 Earth radii. The night side stretches past the Moon’s orbit at 60 Earth radii. NOAA NCEI’s opening answer puts the dipole axis about 11° off the rotation axis. For the WMM2020 coefficients at 2020.0 the same page says 9.41°. NASA’s Earth Fact Sheet places that model’s geocentric dipole at 80.65° N, 72.68° W, and does not print the angle. Particles guided into the upper atmosphere excite oxygen and nitrogen, and the light is the aurora. Under average conditions NOAA’s glossary puts the oval near 75° magnetic latitude at noon and 67° at midnight. These drawings are a schematic. They are not a forecast and not a Kp map. The sunspot rhythm is the solar cycle desk. The seasons’ axial tilt is a different angle, on the seasons desk. The atmosphere desk puts the glow in the thermosphere.

Why it is usually high latitude

The field lines land near the poles

Average noon 75° · average midnight 67°

Earth’s field guides the electrons. NOAA’s aurora page says the aurora forms two ovals approximately centered on the magnetic poles, and that during major geomagnetic storms those ovals expand away from the poles so that aurora can be seen over most of the United States. The glossary’s average oval runs from about 75° magnetic latitude at local noon to about 67° at midnight. Noon is closer to the pole. Midnight is farther from it, which is why the fitted circle is shifted toward midnight. A substorm, the glossary says, widens the aurora toward both higher and lower latitudes. The slider does not draw that widening. It slides the same circle equatorward, up to a classroom 12°, and says so. The solar-cycle desk is where NASA’s “more common near solar maximum” sentence already lives. This page does not add a rate.

What the words mean

Dipole
NOAA NCEI says Earth acts like a large spherical magnet, very similar to the field of a dipole at the center. The opening answer offsets that axis from the rotation axis by approximately 11°. The same page, for WMM2020 at 2020.0, says the axis is inclined at 9.41°, with the geomagnetic north pole at 80.65° N, 72.68° W. NOAA SWPC’s glossary says about 11.5°, and places dipole north near 79° N, 71° W. NASA’s Earth Fact Sheet, model GSFC-1283, prints a dipole field strength of 0.306 Gauss-Re³, an offset of 0.076 Re, and a surface field of 0.24–0.66 gauss. Re there is 6,378 km. The same sheet’s WMM2020 geocentric dipole is 80.65° N, 72.68° W. It does not print 9.41°, and this page does not subtract the latitude from 90°. The drawing uses NCEI’s 9.41°. NCEI also says the surface intensity is roughly 25,000–65,000 nT (0.25–0.65 gauss). SWPC’s glossary says about 32,000 nT at the equator and 62,000 nT at the north magnetic pole. This page does not average those ranges.
Magnetosphere
NASA Science: the region dominated by Earth’s magnetic field, a comet-shaped bubble that shields the planet from solar and cosmic particle radiation and from erosion of the atmosphere by the solar wind. The dayside extends about six to 10 Earth radii. The magnetotail can measure hundreds of Earth radii, far past the Moon at 60. NOAA’s glossary calls it the cavity the field carves in the solar wind, and says the magnetopause is where magnetic pressure effectively equals the solar wind’s dynamic pressure. Along the Sun–Earth axis the magnetosheath, between the bow shock and the magnetopause, is about 2 Earth radii thick. The glossary also says there is evidence of the tail as far as 1000 Earth radii. NOAA NCEI’s separate sentence puts the sunward side at about 10 Earth radii and the tail at more than 100. The slider uses NASA’s six-to-ten span only.
Solar wind
NASA Science: the constant flow of charged particles streaming off the Sun. It compresses the dayside and stretches the tail. NOAA’s glossary says that at 1 AU the speed is typically near 375 km/s, the proton and electron densities near 5 per cubic centimeter, and the interplanetary magnetic field nominally 5 nT. A high-speed stream exceeds approximately 600 km/s, about double the average. The arrows are that flow. They are not a measured speed, and this page does not forecast an arrival.
Auroral oval
NOAA’s glossary: an elliptical band around each geomagnetic pole, from about 75° magnetic latitude at local noon to about 67° at midnight under average conditions. Those locations see the maximum occurrence. The aurora widens toward both higher and lower latitudes in the expansion phase of a magnetic substorm. NOAA’s aurora page says the two ovals are approximately centered on the magnetic poles, and that the best place to watch is under an oval shaped region between the north and south latitudes of about 60 and 75 degrees, on more than half the nights of a given year. During major storms the ovals expand so that aurora can be seen over most of the United States. The 60-and-75 sentence and the 67-and-75 sentence are both quoted. They are not treated as one number.
What glows
NOAA’s aurora page: electrons, energized in the nightside tail and along auroral field lines, follow the magnetic field into the polar regions and collide with oxygen and nitrogen. The atoms and molecules release the energy as light. Protons cause a faint, diffuse aurora, usually not easily visible. NASA Science: the color depends on which gas is hit and at what height. Oxygen gives green and red. Nitrogen gives blue, and below about 100 km a reddish-purple to pink lower edge. The glossary says the typical color is yellow-green, from a specific transition of atomic oxygen, and that above 250 km the light is a red spectral line of atomic oxygen. This page does not list wavelengths. The spectra desk is hydrogen in stars, not this glow.
On the ground
NASA’s solar-storms page says a geomagnetic storm can mean radio blackouts, power outages, and auroras, and that it does not cause direct harm to anyone on Earth, because the magnetic field and the atmosphere protect us from the worst of these storms. The same page says such storms are more common near solar maximum than near solar minimum. High-speed particles can follow the field toward the poles and enter the atmosphere. The page says they do not harm anyone on the ground. NASA’s magnetospheres page says the shield has played a role in habitability, including protection from atmospheric erosion by the solar wind. The solar-cycle desk counts the spots. The atmospheric-escape desk is which gases the planet can hold. This page does not give a storm rate, and it is not a forecast.

Where the sentences come from

  • NASA Science — Magnetospheres — a comet-shaped bubble generated by convective motion of charged, molten iron in the outer core. It shields Earth from solar and cosmic particle radiation and from erosion of the atmosphere by the solar wind. The dayside extends about six to 10 Earth radii. The nightside tail can measure hundreds of Earth radii, far past the Moon’s orbit at 60 Earth radii.
  • NASA Science — Auroras — energetic particles collide with atoms and molecules and the extra energy leaves as light. Green oxygen is the most common color, around 60 miles (100 km), and the table puts it at about 60–120 miles (100–200 km). Red oxygen is at or above 120 miles (200 km). Blue nitrogen is about 60–120 miles (100–200 km). Pink nitrogen is at or below about 60 miles (100 km). The magnetosphere protects us from harmful charged particles. The International Space Station orbits roughly 250 miles (400 km), and astronauts fly over auroral displays and sometimes through them.
  • NASA Science — Solar storms and flares — an Earth-directed storm can drive a geomagnetic storm, with radio blackouts, power outages, and auroras. The magnetic field and the atmosphere protect people on the ground. Storms are more common near solar maximum than near solar minimum. This page does not give a rate. The sunspot count is the solar-cycle desk.
  • NASA NSSDCA — Earth Fact Sheet — GSFC-1283 dipole field strength 0.306 Gauss-Re³, dipole offset 0.076 Re, surface field 0.24–0.66 gauss. Re is 6,378 km. WMM2020 geocentric dipole 80.65° N, 72.68° W. The sheet does not print a tilt angle. Obliquity to orbit is 23.44°. That obliquity is the seasons desk, not this inclination.
  • NOAA NCEI — Geomagnetism FAQ — a dipole-like field. The opening answer says the axis is offset from the rotation axis by approximately 11°. For WMM2020 at 2020.0 the axis is inclined at 9.41°, and the geomagnetic north pole is at 80.65° N, 72.68° W. Surface intensity roughly 25,000–65,000 nT (0.25–0.65 gauss). The magnetosphere is compressed on the sunward side to about 10 Earth radii and extended on the night side to more than 100 Earth radii. It deflects the flow of most solar wind particles.
  • NOAA SWPC — Aurora — electrons collide with oxygen and nitrogen and the light is released when they relax. Protons cause a faint diffuse aurora, usually hard to see. The glow typically forms 80 to 500 km up. Two ovals, approximately centered on the magnetic poles. During major geomagnetic storms they expand so that aurora can be seen over most of the United States. The best observing region is between the north and south latitudes of about 60 and 75 degrees, on more than half the nights of a given year. When activity increases, the aurora extends equatorward.
  • NOAA SWPC — Space Weather Glossary — auroral oval, about 75° magnetic latitude at noon and about 67° at midnight under average conditions. Altitude range 80 to 1000 km, typical aurorae 100 to 250 km, yellow-green from a specific transition of atomic oxygen, a red line of atomic oxygen above 250 km. Solar wind at 1 AU typically near 375 km/s, densities near 5 cm⁻³, interplanetary field nominally 5 nT. High-speed streams exceed about 600 km/s. Magnetosheath about 2 Earth radii thick along the Sun–Earth axis. Magnetotail traced as far as 1000 Earth radii. Centered-dipole inclination about 11.5° in that glossary entry, which is not the 9.41° this drawing uses.
  • Solar-cycle desk — the sunspot number, the equatorward drift of spots, and the sentence that storms and aurora are more common near solar maximum. Not a forecast, and not this schematic.
  • Seasons desk — axial tilt of about 23.4°, the cause of the seasons. A different lean from the magnetic dipole on this page.
  • Atmosphere desk — the thermosphere card is where that page puts the aurora. This page uses the NASA and NOAA altitude sentences above and does not copy that desk’s layer bounds.
  • Atmospheric escape — which gases a world can keep. The magnetosphere page at NASA names protection from atmospheric erosion. This page does not compute a loss rate.
