Sundial
and shadow length

A vertical stick is a height and a shadow. The shadow’s length is the height divided by the tangent of the Sun’s altitude, when the Sun is up. The direction is opposite the Sun. This page computes that altitude and azimuth with the equations in NOAA’s solar-calculation details, the Meeus series the Global Monitoring Laboratory solar calculator uses. The same series runs the twilight desk. Atmospheric refraction is left out; the lift near the horizon is the refraction desk. The plate beside the numbers is a horizontal sundial for the latitude you set. Its style is tilted at that latitude, so the style is parallel to Earth’s axis, and the hour lines follow tan θ = sin φ · tan(15° × (t − 12)). The curve on the plate is the path of a nodus shadow through the chosen day. The chart is a different shadow: the noon length of the person or the stick, through the year. Campbell, CA is the default, 37.287° N, −121.950°. The local-sky desk stores the same city as 37.29° N.

Educational approximations, not for navigation or survey, and not a legal clock. NOAA says the sunrise result is theoretically accurate to within a minute between ±72° latitude, and within 10 minutes outside that, and that the calculator is not certified. Refraction, the size of the solar disk, and the height of the ground are not in these altitudes. A shadow within 0.2° of the horizon is treated as having no useful finite length. On 21 June 2026 at Campbell, with the clock on UTC−7, this series puts solar noon at 13:10, declination 23.437°, altitude 76.15°, and a 1.8 m shadow of 0.44 m. The classroom estimate 90 − 37.29 + 23.44 is 76.15°, and 1.8 / tan of that angle is 0.44 m. On 21 December 2026, UTC−8, solar noon is 12:06, declination −23.438°, altitude 29.28°, shadow 3.21 m. The matching estimate 90 − 37.29 − 23.44 is 29.27°, and the shadow is 3.21 m. From 2020 through 2030 the equation of time in this series stays between −14.24 and +16.49 minutes.

Why the sundial and the clock disagree

Equation of time
Apparent solar time minus mean solar time. A clock averages the day to 24 hours. The true Sun does not. NOAA’s calculator, following Meeus, is the series used here. Over solar noon from 2020 through 2030 that series stays between −14.24 and +16.49 minutes. The analemma desk draws the same idea with a shorter classroom formula, and it cites NASA GISS for a published Earth range of −14.2 to +16.3 minutes. The two formulas are not the same truncation. A positive minute on this page means the sundial is ahead of mean solar time.
Time zones
Civil time is the mean time of a meridian, 15° times the UTC offset. Longitude east of that meridian makes the Sun early, four minutes per degree. Campbell at 121.950° W is 1.95° west of the UTC−8 meridian, 120° W, so mean noon falls about 7.8 minutes after 12:00 when the offset is −8. The zone term in the panel is 4 × longitude minus 60 × the offset in use. East longitude is positive, as in NOAA’s solar-equations note.
Daylight saving
The DST button adds one hour to the standard offset. The Sun does not move. Solar noon, read on the clock, jumps an hour later. On 21 June 2026 the usual Pacific offset is −7, and this calculator’s solar noon at Campbell is 13:10. On 21 December 2026 the usual offset is −8, and solar noon is 12:06. The button is a toggle, not a government feed. The place presets set it from the common U.S. and Australian date rules, and they skip the transition hour.
The style
Carl Sabanski’s Sundial Primer defines a horizontal sundial as a horizontal plate with a polar-pointing gnomon, and sets the style’s angle equal to the latitude. A style aimed at the celestial pole is parallel to Earth’s axis. The hour-line angle from the noon line is arctan of (sin φ times tan of the hour angle). The hour angle is 15° times the hours from noon. At latitude 0 the sine is 0, the hour lines fall on top of each other, and Sabanski calls that layout a polar dial with the gnomon parallel to the plate. At 90° the hours are 15° apart and the style is vertical.
Eratosthenes
The noon shadow is an angle, and an angle between two cities is a fraction of Earth’s circumference. NASA’s Imagine the Universe retells the comparison: on the summer solstice the Sun passed overhead at Syene, and at Alexandria, 5,000 stadia away, Eratosthenes measured 7.2°, which is 1/50 of a circle, so the circumference was 50 × 5,000 = 250,000 stadia. That page converts a stadium as about 0.15 km and gets about 40,000 km, set next to a modern circumference of 40,070 km. The kilometer figure is the conversion, not a second measurement. An Ohio State lecture says the length of the stadion is contentious, that the surviving account is Cleomedes rather than Eratosthenes’ own book, and that an Attic stadion of 185 m turns 250,000 stadia into 46,250 km, about 15% above 40,070 km. Other historical values of the stadion land closer. This page does not choose one.
Where the noon shadow vanishes
At solar noon the geometric altitude is 90° minus the absolute difference of latitude and declination. It is 90° when the declination equals the latitude, and the shadow length is 0. The declination stays inside about ±23.44°, the obliquity on NASA’s Earth Fact Sheet. So a noon Sun overhead, and a vanished noon shadow, happen only between the Tropic of Cancer and the Tropic of Capricorn. The Cancer button is 23.44° N, that obliquity, at longitude 0°. The Arctic Circle button is 66.56° N, 90° minus 23.44°. Near the December solstice its noon Sun sits on the horizon in this series, and the chart leaves a gap. These two buttons are the fact-sheet angles, not surveyed monuments. The seasons desk is the tilt that swings the declination.

Sources

  • NOAA Global Monitoring Laboratory — Solar calculation details — the sunrise, sunset, and solar-position calculators use equations from Jean Meeus, Astronomical Algorithms. Results are theoretically accurate to within a minute for locations between ±72° latitude, and within 10 minutes outside that. Atmospheric refraction is modeled in NOAA’s solar-position calculator. This page does not apply it. NOAA says the calculator is for research and recreational use and is not certified.
  • NOAA — Solar calculator — the calculator those details describe. The solar-equations note takes longitude positive east, which is the sign used here and on the twilight desk.
  • Carl Sabanski — The Sundial Primer, horizontal sundial — a horizontal plate and a polar-pointing gnomon. The style angle equals the latitude. The hour-line angle is arctan(sin φ · tan h), with h = 15° × (hours from noon). At 0° the gnomon lies parallel to the plate. At 90° it is perpendicular.
  • North American Sundial Society — the society for sundials. Albert E. Waugh, Sundials: Their Theory and Construction, is a standard book on laying a dial out. This page cites that book by title. It does not quote a page.
  • NASA Imagine the Universe — How big is Earth? — Eratosthenes, 7.2° at Alexandria, 5,000 stadia from Syene, 250,000 stadia, and a stadium taken as about 0.15 km.
  • Ohio State Astronomy — Measuring the Earth — the same 7.2° and 250,000 stadia, Cleomedes as the surviving account, and the argument that a stadion of 185 m gives 46,250 km against a modern 40,070 km. The lecture is used here for the disagreement about the stadion, not as a new measurement.
  • NASA NSSDCA — Earth Fact Sheet — obliquity to orbit 23.44°. The tropic and arctic-circle buttons use 23.44° and 66.56°.
