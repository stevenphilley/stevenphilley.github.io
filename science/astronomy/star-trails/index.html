Star trails
and sky rotation

Stars do not move. The ground does. A fixed camera records that turn as a trail, and the trail is an arc around the celestial pole. This page uses a mean sidereal day of 86,164.0905 s, the same rounding as the Foucault desk. JPL’s astrodynamic parameters print 86,164.09054 s, cited to Seidelmann’s 1992 explanatory supplement. Rounded to four decimals in the seconds, that is 86,164.0905. The calculator uses 86,164.0905. A full circle divided by that day is 15.041069″ per second of time. The angle around the pole is that rate times the exposure. It does not depend on declination. The path on the sky is the same rate times cos δ, so an equatorial star streaks fastest. Thirty seconds at declination 0° is 7.52′ (451.23″). A 24 mm lens and an illustrative 5.9 µm pixel then drift 0.2966 px/s, the small-angle length f · θ. The 500 rule and Frédéric Michaud’s NPF rule sit beside the pixel limit. Both are approximations. From Campbell, 37.29° N, the north celestial pole’s altitude is 37.29°, the latitude the local-sky desk stores. The sidereal day is why the rate is not 15″ from a 24-hour clock.

Why the trails look like that

Around the pole
A star keeps a nearly fixed declination for the length of an exposure. Proper motion, refraction, and precession are real, and they are not in this drawing. What is left is Earth’s turn. The turn is around the celestial pole, so the path is a circle of constant declination. Every star sweeps the same angle around the pole. The radius of the circle is what changes.
The equator is fastest
The path length on the sky is the sidereal rate times cos δ times the exposure. At δ = 0, cos δ is 1 and the path equals the angle around the pole. At 60°, cos δ is one half, so the path is half and the angle around the pole is unchanged. On the pole, cos δ is 0. The star does not move. A long lens does not change that geometry. It only magnifies whatever path the sky already has.
Sidereal, not solar
The Earth Fact Sheet lists a sidereal rotation of 23.9345 hours and a length of day of 24.0000 hours. This calculator does not divide by 23.9345. It divides by 86,164.0905 s, which is 23.934470 h before that four-decimal rounding. A mean solar day of 24.0000 h is exactly 15″ per second. The trails use 15.041069″ per second. The four extra minutes, and why the stars rise earlier, are the sidereal desk. The same spin, times sin φ, is the Foucault pendulum.
Two angles
The angle around the pole is the Earth’s turn, rate times time, and it does not know the declination. The streak on the frame is the path length, rate times cos δ times time. Thirty seconds at the equator is 7.52′ and 451.23″. Thirty minutes is sixty times that, and an arcminute is sixty arcseconds, so thirty minutes is 451.23′. The digits match because of that factor of sixty. They are not a second measurement.
On the sensor
For a short trail the length is f · θ, with θ in radians and f the focal length. Divide by the pixel pitch and the result is pixels. At 24 mm and 5.9 µm, declination 0°, that is 0.2966 px/s, and 8.8989 px in 30 s. One pixel of blur takes 3.371 s. Past about 1° the same product is still the arc length. A flat sensor does not lay a long arc down as that straight length. The telescope desk is the aperture and the eyepiece. This desk is the trail the focal length records.
Rules of thumb
The 500 rule, as night photographers usually write it, is 500 / (crop factor × focal length in millimetres) seconds. Michaud’s page calls it the older rule. He does not print this quotient. This page uses the usual quotient and labels it an approximation. It ignores declination and pixel pitch. At 24 mm and crop 1 it is 20.83 s, which is 6.1798 px at the opening pitch and at the equator. The NPF rule is printed by Frédéric Michaud of the Société Astronomique du Havre. Simplified, (35 N + 30 p) / f. Complete, k (16.9 N + 0.10 f + 13.7 p) / (f cos δ), with p in micrometres and f in millimetres. At f/2.8, 5.9 µm, 24 mm, and δ = 0, k = 1, those are 11.46 s and 5.440 s. This page does not re-derive the coefficients.
Tracking, and Campbell
An equatorial mount that turns once per sidereal day, about an axis aimed at the pole, holds a star on the same pixel when the aim is perfect. This page sets that residual to zero. Periodic error, polar misalignment, flexure, and refraction are not in the zero. The sky still sweeps the angle around the pole. From Campbell, the latitude on the local-sky desk is 37.29° N, so the north celestial pole stands 37.29° above the horizon. Altitude of the pole equals the latitude. South of the equator the south celestial pole is the one above the horizon, and the trails run the other way when you look toward it. Refraction near that horizon is the refraction desk, not this one.
