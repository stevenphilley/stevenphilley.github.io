Telescope
optics

What an aperture, a focal length, and an eyepiece actually decide. The focal ratio is focal length divided by aperture. Magnification is the telescope’s focal length divided by the eyepiece’s, and a Barlow multiplies that effective focal length. The exit pupil is the aperture divided by the magnification. True field is about the apparent field divided by the magnification. Light grasp is the area ratio against a 7 mm comparison pupil. Rayleigh’s criterion is 1.22 λ/D. Dawes’s limit is the empirical 116/D millimetre form, shown beside 4.56/D in inches. How faint a star looks is the magnitude desk. How wide something looks is angular size. What the horizon puts overhead is the local sky. Why the naked-eye limit moves is light pollution.

Simplified optics for a classroom. Not a buying guide. A real view depends on seeing, collimation, optical quality, coatings, a central obstruction this page ignores, the observer, and the sky. Rayleigh’s 1.22 λ/D is a diffraction criterion for two equal point sources and a filled circular aperture. Dawes’s limit is an empirical visual rule for close, similar doubles, not a guarantee. The limiting-magnitude lines are approximations. Hubble and JWST are not eyepiece telescopes.

Read this before the numbers

Not a buying guide

The arithmetic above is idealised. It assumes a filled circular aperture, perfect optics, no central obstruction, no coating losses, and two equal point sources. A Newtonian or a Cassegrain has a secondary in the beam. Seeing, collimation, and the observer move the real limit by more than the difference between Rayleigh and Dawes. The 50×-per-inch line and the 30×-per-inch line are rules of thumb. Tele Vue prints the 50×-per-inch claim as a myth in the sense that it is not a hard ceiling: a small refractor can look pleasant at higher power and still show no extra detail, and a large aperture often stops sooner because of the air.

The area limiting magnitude only counts photons the aperture collects, then adds that gain to the naked-eye limit you typed. It does not get fainter as you magnify, once the exit pupil fits in the eye. Magnification can still help by darkening the sky background. Bowen’s 1947 formula is the published line that includes magnification. Its constant is 6.5, from his dark-sky observations, not the sky you typed. Schaefer’s 1990 paper is why neither line is a prediction: sky brightness, magnification, throughput, age, and the observer all move the limit, and his own test set scattered by about three quarters of a magnitude. Real limits vary with seeing, observer, and optics.

Words

Focal ratio
Focal length divided by the clear aperture, in the same units. An f/6 mirror of 200 mm has a 1,200 mm focal length. A Barlow raises the effective focal ratio by its factor. The objective’s own ratio does not change.
Magnification
Telescope focal length divided by eyepiece focal length. A Barlow multiplies the effective focal length, so it multiplies the magnification. It does not add detail the aperture did not already collect.
Exit pupil
The little disk of light behind the eyepiece. Aperture divided by magnification, which is the same as eyepiece focal length divided by the effective focal ratio. If it is larger than the eye’s pupil, some of the beam misses the eye.
True field
The patch of sky in the eyepiece. Apparent field divided by magnification is the rough estimate. Tele Vue notes that eyepieces do not magnify evenly across the field, so a field-stop measurement is tighter. This desk has no field stop, so it uses the approximation and labels it.
Light grasp
Collecting area of the aperture divided by the collecting area of a 7 mm comparison pupil. The 7 mm figure is the traditional maximum Sky & Telescope quotes, not a measurement of your eye. Real dark-adapted pupils run from nearly 9 mm to under 4 mm and shrink with age.
Airy disk
The diffraction pattern of a point source through a circular aperture. The bright core is surrounded by a dark ring at the first zero of the Bessel function J1. The drawing sums two of those patterns.
Rayleigh and Dawes
Rayleigh’s criterion puts the peak of one equal point source on the first dark ring of the other: 1.22 λ/D, the conventional rounding of (the first zero of J1) / π. Dawes’s limit is empirical, from double-star measures, printed by Tele Vue as 4.56 arcseconds per inch of aperture. The millimetre form 116/D is that rule after multiplying by the international inch, 25.4 mm, and rounding 115.824 to 116. Dawes does not depend on wavelength. The two printed limits meet near 461 nm. That wavelength is computed on this page. It is not a number from Dawes.
Limiting magnitude
The area estimate is your naked-eye limit plus 5 log10 of the aperture ratio, the same magnitude rule as the magnitude desk (a factor of 100 is five magnitudes). If the exit pupil is larger than 7 mm, only the light that fits the comparison pupil is counted. Bowen’s formula, m = 6.5 + 2.5 log D + 2.5 log M with D in inches, adds a magnification term and assumes his dark sky. He stated it for exit pupils from about 0.3 inch down to about 0.03 inch. Outside that range this page still shows the number and says so.

Sources

  • Chuck Hawks, Sky & Telescope — Simple formulas for the telescope owner — Magnification is telescope focal length over eyepiece focal length. Focal ratio is focal length over clear aperture. True field, the easy way, is apparent field over magnification. Exit pupil is aperture over magnification, or eyepiece focal length over focal ratio. A dark-adapted pupil is “about 7mm.” He treats 50× per inch as the ceiling in excellent seeing and about 30× per inch as his average-seeing maximum. His inch is “approximately 25mm.” This desk converts with the international inch, exactly 25.4 mm.
  • Tele Vue — Telescope formulas, and common myths — The same definitions, plus Dawes’s limit as 4.56 arcseconds over the aperture in inches, and aperture gain as (objective diameter / eye pupil diameter) squared. Myth 5: 50× per inch is not a law. Myth 1: a 7 mm exit pupil is not a universal lowest useful magnification, especially on a refractor. Myth 6: a Barlow raises the effective f/number.
  • Tele Vue — Choosing your telescope’s magnification — “A rough approximation to the true field is obtained by dividing the apparent field of the eyepiece by the magnification.” Rough, because magnification is not constant across the field. The field-stop formula is the tighter one. This desk does not have a field stop.
  • Sky & Telescope — A pupil primer — Exit pupil equals aperture divided by magnifying power. The traditional line is that the pupil “dilates to a maximum diameter of 7 millimeters.” The same article says some eyes reach nearly 9 mm, some do not reach 4 mm, and the maximum declines with age. The lowest power that uses the whole aperture is aperture divided by your pupil.
  • Sky & Telescope — Telescope calculator — “As a rule of thumb, a telescope’s maximum useful magnification is 50 times its aperture in inches (or twice its aperture in millimeters).” The exit-pupil note on that page also uses 7 mm as the average adult dark-adapted pupil.
  • HyperPhysics — Rayleigh criterion — For a circular aperture the criterion is θ = 1.22 λ/D, the angle that puts one source’s maximum on the other’s first minimum. The 1.22 is the conventional rounding. This page also computes (first positive zero of J1) / π.
  • Dawes, W. R. 1867, Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society, 27, 217 — Catalogue of micrometrical measurements of double stars. The empirical separating power associated with Dawes is the historical source of the limit. The arithmetic forms used here are the later printings: 4.56/D in inches (Tele Vue; also Sky & Telescope’s statement of the rule) and 116/D in millimetres, the rounding of 4.56 × 25.4.
  • Dawes, W. R. 1867, Memoirs of the Royal Astronomical Society, 35, 137 — The Memoirs printing of the same double-star catalogue. Bibcode 1867MmRAS..35..137D.
  • Bowen, I. S. 1947, Publications of the Astronomical Society of the Pacific, 59, 253 — Limiting visual magnitude — m = 6.5 + 5 log D − 2.5 log E, equivalently m = 6.5 + 2.5 log D + 2.5 log M, with D and the exit pupil E in inches. He found it held, on clear moonless nights away from city lights, for exit pupils from about the dark-adapted eye (he used about 0.3 inch) down to about 0.03 inch.
  • Schaefer, B. E. 1990, Publications of the Astronomical Society of the Pacific, 102, 212 — Telescopic limiting magnitudes — Formulas of the shape constant + 5 log D leave out magnification and sky brightness. His physiological formula was tested on 314 observations. The scatter was large: a half-width of about 0.75 magnitude. This desk does not implement that full algorithm. It cites the paper as the reason the two simpler lines are approximations.
  • NIST Guide to the SI, SP 811, Appendix B.9 — The international inch is exactly 25.4 millimetres. Apertures typed in inches use that factor. Dawes’s 4.56 × 25.4 = 115.824, which the 116/D form rounds.
  • Colour & Vision Research Laboratory — CIE luminous-efficiency tables — The CIE 1931 photopic V(λ) peaks at 555 nm. The CIE 1951 scotopic V′(λ) peaks at 507 nm. This desk’s default wavelength is 550 nm, five nanometres short of the photopic peak, a round visual-band choice. It is not a measured constant, and it is not the scotopic peak.
  • NASA GSFC Imagine the Universe — night-sky answers — Under ideal conditions the average person should see stars as faint as magnitude 6.0 to 6.5. The sky buttons use those two edges. They are not a measurement of a particular night.
  • Sky & Telescope — How dark is your night sky? — Bortle class 9, an inner-city sky, has a limiting magnitude of 4.0 or less. The inner-city button uses 4.0 and is marked approximate. The idea of that limit is the light-pollution desk. The scale itself is the magnitude desk.
  • STScI — Hubble primer, optical performance — Ritchey–Chrétien. Aperture 2.4 m. Focal ratio f/24. PSF FWHM at 5000 Å is 0.043 arcsec, their figure, not this desk’s Rayleigh angle. The preset focal length is 24 × 2.4 m = 57.6 m. Wavelength is set to 500 nm for that line.
  • NASA Science — About Hubble — Primary mirror diameter 94.5 inches (2.4 m). 94.5 inches is the rounded companion of 2.4 m at 25.4 mm per inch (2.4 / 0.0254 = 94.49).
  • NASA Science — Hubble by the numbers — Focal length printed as 189 feet (56.7 m). The same page says the primary collects 40,000 times as much light as the human eye. This desk’s (2.4 m / 7 mm)² is a different comparison. Both numbers are shown. They are not the same claim.
  • NASA Science — James Webb Space Telescope fact sheet — Primary diameter about 6.5 m. Focal length 131.4 m. Clear aperture 25 m². Coverage 0.6–28.5 μm. Optical resolution about 0.1 arcsec, their figure. The preset uses 6.5 m, 131.4 m, and 600 nm, the short edge of that coverage. The clear-aperture line uses 25 m².
  • STScI — JWST telescope — Effective focal ratio f/20. Effective focal length 131.4 m. Eighteen hexagonal segments acting as a mirror about 6.5 m across. Unobscured collecting area 25.4 m². The preset does not substitute 25.4 for the fact sheet’s 25, and it does not force the focal ratio to 20. Dividing the two fact-sheet numbers is this page’s f/20.22.
  • Mason, Wycoff, Hartkopf, Douglass, and Worley 2001, Astronomical Journal, 122, 3466 — The Washington Double Star Catalog. VizieR B/wds, WDS 19307+2758, component STFA 43 AB: the later separation on the row consulted for this page is 34.70 arcsec (Obs2 year 2024), with magnitudes 3.190 and 4.68. A catalog row can move when the catalog is updated. The drawing puts two equal points at 34.70″. It does not paint those magnitudes. Rayleigh’s criterion assumes equal points, and this pair is not equal.
  • Bob King, Sky & Telescope — Will the real Albireo please stand up? — The familiar pair described as 34″ apart. That is the round figure. The button uses the catalog’s 34.70″.

Telescope desk

A classroom calculator. Ideal optics, a filled circular aperture, no obstruction, no atmosphere. Not a buying guide and not a prediction of what you will see.

Fields

Fields
ApertureClear aperture. Millimetres or inches. An inch is exactly 25.4 mm. The slider runs from 20 mm to 8 m. The number field accepts a wider span, including Hubble and JWST.
Focal lengthThe telescope’s focal length, same unit choice. Focal ratio is this divided by the aperture. A Barlow does not change the number you type. It changes the effective focal length used for magnification.
No eyepieceTurns off magnification, exit pupil, true field, the magnification rules of thumb, and both visual limiting-magnitude lines. Hubble and JWST select this. Resolution and light grasp remain.
EyepieceFocal length in millimetres. Magnification is telescope focal length times the Barlow factor, divided by this.
Apparent fieldThe eyepiece’s apparent field, in degrees. True field is this divided by magnification. That is the rough approximation.
BarlowA factor of 2 doubles the effective focal length, the magnification, and the effective focal ratio, and it halves the exit pupil and the true field. 1 means no Barlow. A factor below 1 is a reducer, same arithmetic.
WavelengthUsed only by Rayleigh’s criterion and the Airy drawing. Default 550 nm, near the CIE photopic peak at 555 nm. Night vision peaks nearer 507 nm. Dawes’s limit does not use this field.
Naked-eye limitThe faintest star you can see without the telescope, typed by you. Ideal-sky buttons are NASA’s 6.0 and 6.5. Inner city is Sky & Telescope’s Bortle class 9 figure, 4.0 or less, entered as 4.0. The light-pollution desk is the essay. This number feeds only the area limiting-magnitude line.
SeparationAngular separation of the two equal points in the drawing. Albireo sets 34.70″ from the Washington Double Star Catalog row cited on the page. “At Rayleigh” and “At Dawes” set the separation to the limit just computed for the current aperture.
