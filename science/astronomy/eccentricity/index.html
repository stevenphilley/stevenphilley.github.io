Orbital
eccentricity

Eccentricity is the number that turns a circle into a long ellipse. For a bound Keplerian orbit, 0 ≤ e < 1. Periapsis, the near end, is rp = a(1 − e). Apoapsis, the far end, is ra = a(1 + e). Their ratio is ra / rp = (1 + e) / (1 − e). When e = 0, both ends equal a and the path is a circle. As e approaches 1 the ellipse grows long and skinny, still bound, with the Sun at one focus. Speed is vis-viva, v = √[μ (2/r − 1/a)]. The same a and a smaller r make a larger speed, so periapsis is faster than apoapsis. Around the Sun, μ is the Sun Fact Sheet GM, 132,712 × 106 km3/s2. Earth’s orbital table prints a = 149.598 × 106 km and e = 0.0167, so rp = 147.100 × 106 km, ra = 152.096 × 106 km, vp = 30.286 km/s, and va = 29.291 km/s. The sheet lists 30.29 km/s and 29.29 km/s. Mercury’s e is 0.2056. Halley’s, on the comet fact sheet, is 0.967. The year still comes from a alone, which is the Kepler desk. The speed that does not come back is escape. The half-ellipse between two circles is Hohmann. A hyperbolic excess is C3. A flyby that turns a v∞ is a gravity assist. Where a moon shreds is Roche. Where a planet dominates is Hill. The solar map is where the bodies sit on a date. This page is only the shape.

The formulas

rp = a(1 − e) and ra = a(1 + e). a is the semimajor axis, half the long axis. e is the eccentricity. The Sun, for these heliocentric presets, sits at one focus, a distance ae from the center. The empty focus is the same distance on the other side. Their average is still a: (rp + ra) / 2 = a.

ra / rp = (1 + e) / (1 − e). The semimajor axis cancels. Earth’s 0.0167 gives 1.034. Mercury’s 0.2056 gives 1.518. Halley’s 0.967 gives 59.606. A circle, e = 0, gives 1.

v = √[μ (2/r − 1/a)]. μ is the Sun Fact Sheet GM, 132,712 × 106 km3/s2, on every preset here. At periapsis, r is smaller, so 2/r is larger, so vp is larger than va. When e = 0, r is a everywhere and both speeds are √(μ/a). The vis-viva desk slides r between the ends. This desk stays on the ends and moves e and a.

The year does not use e. P = 2π √(a³/μ) is Kepler’s period for this point-mass Sun. The Kepler desk writes the same idea as P² = a³ in years and AU, for a circle. A skinny ellipse with the same a takes the same time and is much faster at the near end.

What e is not

Not a clock, and not a launch

e = 0 is a circle. A small e, such as Earth’s 0.0167, is still nearly a circle: the two ends differ by a few percent, and the two speeds round to the sheet’s 30.29 km/s and 29.29 km/s. A large e, such as Halley’s 0.967, is a long ellipse. It is still bound. e = 1 would be a parabola, which is escape, and e > 1 would be a hyperbola. This slider does not go there.

Nothing here is a position for tonight, a perturbation from Jupiter, or a trajectory a spacecraft could fly. The solar map moves worlds by date. This page only scales the ends with e and a.

Words

Eccentricity
How far the orbit is from a circle. 0 is a circle. Toward 1, a long skinny ellipse, still closed. The focus sits a distance ae from the center, so e is also that offset divided by a.
Periapsis and apoapsis
The nearest and farthest points from the focus. Around the Sun the names are perihelion and aphelion. rp = a(1−e) and ra = a(1+e). This desk prints those formula ends, then sets the fact sheet’s printed distances beside them when a preset is on.
Why periapsis is faster
Vis-viva. v = √[μ (2/r − 1/a)]. Closer to the focus, 2/r is larger, so the speed is larger. The arrow at periapsis is the long one. The arrow at apoapsis shrinks by va / vp.
Which a
Planet presets use the orbital-parameters semimajor axis in 106 km, because μ is in km3/s2. The J2000 AU line is labeled beside it and is not substituted into rp = a(1−e). Halley has no kilometer line on the comet sheet. Its a is 17.94 AU times the IAU unit, 149,597,870.7 km.
What the ellipse leaves out
Other planets, the Sun’s finite size except as a warning when periapsis falls inside 695,700 km, inclination, and any date. Halley’s 162.2° tilt is a fact-sheet number this flat drawing does not show. Not Horizons. Not a mission. The escape desk is the speed that does not return. C3 is a hyperbolic excess. Hohmann assumes the circles this page is stretching.

  • NASA NSSDCA — Planetary Fact Sheet — index of the per-body sheets. Planet a, e, perihelion, and aphelion on this page are the orbital-parameter tables, in 106 km. The J2000 AU line is labeled and is not the a in rp = a(1−e).
  • NASA NSSDCA — Earth Fact Sheet — Semimajor axis 149.598 × 106 km. Semimajor axis (AU) 1.00000011. Perihelion 147.095 × 106 km. Aphelion 152.100 × 106 km. Orbit eccentricity 0.0167. Max. orbital velocity 30.29 km/s. Min. orbital velocity 29.29 km/s. Sidereal orbit period 365.256 days.

  • NASA NSSDCA — Comet Fact Sheet — 1P Halley: orbital period 76.1 yrs, perihelion date 1986-02-09, perihelion distance 0.587 AU, semi-major axis 17.94 AU, orbital eccentricity 0.967, orbital inclination 162.2 deg. The sheet does not print a kilometer line or an orbital velocity. Last updated 04 January 2016.
  • NASA Science — 1P/Halley — One passage, not the rounded elements above. At aphelion in 1948, 35.25 AU and 0.91 kilometers per second. At perihelion on 9 February 1986, 0.5871 AU and 54.55 kilometers per second. The period has been as short as 74.42 years (1835–1910) and as long as 79.25 years (451–530).   • IAU 2012 Resolution B2 — The astronomical unit is exactly 149,597,870,700 m, so 149,597,870.7 km. Halley’s 17.94 AU is multiplied by that length. The solar map uses the same kilometer length. Planet presets do not. They use the printed 106 km line.
  • Vis-viva desk — the same formula anywhere on the ellipse. Earth’s printed perihelion there is 30.287 km/s. This page’s Earth speed is vis-viva at a(1−e), 30.286 km/s. Both round to the sheet’s 30.29 km/s.
  • Kepler desk — P² = a³ in years and AU for a circular solar orbit. The period on this page is 2π √(a³/μ) from the kilometer a and the Sun’s GM. It does not depend on e.

Leaving

Escape

Circular orbit and escape at one altitude. √2 apart. e = 1 is the parabola this slider does not draw.

The coast

Hohmann

Half an ellipse between two circles. Earth to Mars is 258.88 days and 5.594 km/s. Those circles set e aside.

The departure

C3

C3 = v∞². At 400 km, C3 = 0 is a 3.178 km/s burn. A hyperbola, not this bound ellipse.

The flyby

Gravity assist

An unpowered hyperbola. Its eccentricity is 1 + r_p v∞² / μ, which is not this orbit’s e.

The shred

Roche

Fluid and rigid limits. A distance from a planet, not the stretch of a solar ellipse.
