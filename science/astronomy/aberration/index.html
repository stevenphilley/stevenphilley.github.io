Vol. Science · Astronomy · The tilt

Aberration of
starlight

Earth moves. A star’s light does not arrive from the direction a stationary observer would measure. The apparent direction tilts toward Earth’s velocity. The Earth Fact Sheet prints a mean orbital velocity of 29.78 km/s. The speed of light is c = 299,792.458 km/s exactly. Their quotient, in arcseconds, is 20.48939″ when the star is at a right angle to the velocity. The IAU constant of aberration for epoch 2000 is κ = 20.49552″. The two figures are both on this page. They are not averaged. James Bradley published the account in 1729, after observations of γ Draconis that had been meant to measure its parallax. The shift that is a distance, pointing toward the Sun and a quarter-year out of phase, is the parallax desk. The same β on a clock is time dilation. Distance over the same c is light time. Perihelion and aphelion speeds are the vis-viva desk. A star’s own drift across the sky, separate from this annual tilt, is the proper motion desk.

Educational schematic. Not an astrometric reduction tool. The sky figure is a circular orbit and the IAU constant of aberration. It ignores nutation, gravitational light deflection, planetary aberration, the few thousand kilometres between a telescope and Earth’s center, and the eccentricity of Earth’s orbit. The speed slider is a classroom angle, not a catalog place.

Earth’s mean orbital velocity on the fact sheet is 29.78 km/s. c is 299,792.458 km/s. At θ = 90°, first-order (v/c) sin θ is 20.48939″. The classical tan formula gives 20.48939″. The relativistic formula gives 20.48939″. Relativistic minus first-order is 3.371 × 10⁻⁸″. At this speed the three angles agree through the arcsecond digits this page prints. They are not averaged.

Day 0.0 of a 365-day classroom year. φ = 0°. Aberration sits on the velocity. Parallax sits toward the Sun. Those two directions are a quarter-year apart.

A quarter-year apart

Parallax points at the Sun. Aberration points along the velocity.

Bradley and Molyneux were timing γ Draconis for an annual parallax. The star did not move the way a nearby star would if the shift were the baseline of Earth’s orbit. The published account is the 1729 letter to Halley. The parallax of that star, from the Hipparcos new reduction, is 21.14 ± 0.10 mas. κ is 20.49552″. The ratio is 969.514. On the figure they share a scale, so the parallax ellipse is a dot until you zoom.

Through the year the two displacements are a quarter-cycle apart. At the phase this page calls φ = 0, the Sun has the star’s ecliptic longitude, the parallax arrow points toward the ecliptic, and the aberration arrow points to decreasing longitude, which is the direction of Earth’s velocity. The speed slider does not resize that figure. The figure uses κ. The slider is the angle formula, from an equatorial 0.465101 km/s up to 0.9c.

Literacy · the numbers on the readout

Annual aberration
The apparent direction of a star is tilted toward the observer’s velocity. For a circular orbit the maximum tilt is the constant of aberration. At the ecliptic pole the path is a circle of radius κ. At ecliptic latitude β the path is an ellipse with semi-axis κ parallel to the ecliptic and semi-minor axis κ |sin β|. On the ecliptic the minor axis is zero and the path is a line of half-length κ.
First-order v/c
(v/c) sin θ, in arcseconds. θ is the angle between the velocity and the star’s geometric direction. At θ = 90° and v = 29.78 km/s the arithmetic is 20.48939″. This is the small-angle form. It is not a sky angle once β is no longer small.
Classical tan
tan θ′ = sin θ / (β + cos θ). The shift is θ − θ′. At θ = 90° that shift is arctan β. At Earth’s orbital speed it prints as 20.48939″, the same digits as v/c. At 0.9c and θ = 90° it is 41.98721°.
Relativistic aberration
cos θ′ = (cos θ + β) / (1 + β cos θ). The same angle is tan θ′ = sin θ / (γ (β + cos θ)), with γ = 1/√(1 − β²). The shift is θ − θ′. At 29.78 km/s and θ = 90° it is 20.48939″, and the difference from the first-order value is 3.371 × 10⁻⁸″. At 0.9c and θ = 90° it is 64.15807°. The three angles are not averaged.
The constant κ
USNO Circular 163 prints the constant of aberration for standard epoch 2000 as 20.49552″, the IAU (1976) derived constant. It is the ratio of Earth’s mean orbital speed to c in that system, not the fact sheet’s two-decimal 29.78 km/s divided by c. 20.48939″ is not substituted for 20.49552″.
Diurnal aberration
The equator’s speed is 2πR/T. R is 6,378.137 km and T is 23.9345 hours, both from the Earth Fact Sheet. The speed is 0.465101 km/s. At θ = 90° the first-order angle is 0.32000″. A familiar round figure is 0.465 km/s. 0.465101 rounds to 0.465 at three decimals. 0.465 is not substituted.
γ Draconis
The star Bradley watched. van Leeuwen 2007, the Hipparcos new reduction, gives a parallax of 21.14 ± 0.10 mas. SIMBAD lists that value. 21.14 mas is 0.02114″. The distance 1000/21.14 is 47.3037 pc. The ±0.10 mas is not folded into the ellipse. The latitude slider is not that star’s ecliptic latitude. The button sets the parallax, and the latitude stays where you left it.
Out of phase
The aberration displacement at phase φ equals the parallax displacement at φ − 90°, scaled by κ/π. Parallax points toward the Sun. Aberration points along Earth’s velocity. The year on the slider is 365 classroom days, not the fact sheet’s 365.256-day sidereal period.
Voyager 1
NASA Science, for 21 August 2024, prints 38,026.79 mph (17.0 km/s) relative to the Sun. The button is 17.0 km/s. A later status line is not substituted.

Sources · the constants

Cited, not invented

  • USNO Circular No. 163 — the IAU (1976) system of astronomical constants. The constant of aberration for standard epoch 2000 is 20.49552″. The note defines it as the ratio of Earth’s mean orbital speed to the speed of light, computed in that system from F k τA / 86400. This page does not recompute F.
  • NASA NSSDCA — Earth Fact Sheet — mean orbital velocity 29.78 km/s, maximum 30.29 km/s, minimum 29.29 km/s. Equatorial radius 6,378.137 km. Sidereal rotation period 23.9345 hours. Semimajor axis 149.598 × 106 km. Perihelion 147.095 × 106 km. Aphelion 152.100 × 106 km. The 0.465101 km/s equatorial speed is 2πR/T from the radius and the period. It is not a separate line on the sheet.
  • NASA NSSDCA — Sun Fact Sheet — GM 132,712 × 106 km3/s2, the same value as the vis-viva desk. Perihelion speed 30.287 km/s and aphelion speed 29.291 km/s are vis-viva at the Earth sheet’s printed distances. They round to the sheet’s 30.29 and 29.29. The sheet’s rounded speeds are not substituted.
  • NIST — speed of light in vacuum — c = 299,792,458 m/s exactly, which is 299,792.458 km/s. The same defining constant as the light-time desk.
  • Bradley 1729, Philosophical Transactions 35, 637–661 — “A Letter … Giving an Account of a New Discovered Motion of the Fix’d Stars.” The observations were of γ Draconis, begun in the search for its parallax. The DNB biography at MacTutor says the letter was read to the Royal Society in January 1729.
  • van Leeuwen 2007, A&A 474, 653 — Hipparcos, the new reduction. γ Draconis, HIP 87833, parallax 21.14 ± 0.10 mas. SIMBAD lists 21.14 [0.10] with that bibcode. The ±0.10 is not folded in. 1000/21.14 = 47.3037 pc.
  • NASA Science — Voyager 1 — as of 21 August 2024, 38,026.79 mph (17.0 km/s) relative to the Sun. The button uses 17.0 km/s.
  • Einstein 1905, Annalen der Physik 17, 891 — the relativistic transformation of the direction of a light ray. This page uses cos θ′ = (cos θ + β) / (1 + β cos θ), the sign that moves a star at 90° toward the velocity. The equivalent form is tan θ′ = sin θ / (γ (β + cos θ)). It does not copy a page number.

The drift

Proper motion

A star’s own motion across the sky, separate from the annual tilt. Barnard's Star computes 90.076 km/s of tangential speed.

The baseline

Parallax

The same orbit, as a triangle. The shift points toward the Sun and is a distance. For γ Draconis it is 0.02114″, not 20.49552″.

The clock

Time dilation

The same β, as a rate. A moving clock runs slow by √(1 − v²/c²). This page is the direction, not the tick.

The delay

Light time

Distance over the same c. One AU is 499.005 seconds. Aberration is what that finite speed does to a direction.

The speed

Vis-viva

Earth’s perihelion is 30.287 km/s and aphelion is 29.291 km/s, from the Sun’s GM and the fact-sheet distances. The buttons here use those two.

The day

Sidereal

23.9345 hours is the spin in the equatorial speed. The 365.256-day orbit is a different count from the 365-day slider.
