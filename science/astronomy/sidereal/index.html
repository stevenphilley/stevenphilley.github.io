Vol. Science · Astronomy · The day

Sidereal
the shorter day

A clock day is not one spin of Earth. Mean solar time brings the Sun back to the same meridian. NASA’s Earth Fact Sheet lists that length of day as 24.0000 hours. A sidereal day is one full turn relative to the distant stars. The same sheet lists the sidereal rotation period as 23.9345 hours. NASA Space Place says the same thing in hours and minutes: a solar day is around 24 hours, and a sidereal day is almost exactly 23 hours and 56 minutes. The star day is the shorter one. After the meridian faces a star again, Earth has moved along its orbit, so the Sun is not quite back. The mark still has a little farther to turn. That is why the stars rise earlier the next night. JPL’s January 2024 What’s Up says the stars rise 4 minutes earlier each day. The mean in “mean solar” is the average the analemma desk takes apart. The orbit itself, used as a ruler, is the parallax desk. The same catch-up, over a month instead of a day, is why new Moon to new Moon outlasts one orbit of the Moon — the synodic desk. The same fact sheet’s tropical orbit period is shorter than its sidereal orbit period because the equinox itself moves — the precession desk.

Classroom schematic — a top-down disk, a fixed star direction, and a short arc of a circular orbit, viewed from the north so spin and orbit both run counterclockwise. The daily step on that orbit is 360° divided by the fact sheet’s 365.256-day sidereal orbit. The sketch draws that step much larger so a phone can see the leftover turn. The clocks and the degree readout use 23.9345 hours and 24.0000 hours. They are not enlarged. Not an ephemeris. Not a sidereal clock for navigation, surveying, or a telescope. Not a sundial. Not astrology. The fact sheet lists orbit eccentricity 0.0167; this circle leaves that out. NASA’s Basics of Space Flight states the published gap: rotation relative to the fixed stars is 3 minutes 56.55 seconds shorter than the mean solar day, the equivalent of one solar day per year. This page does not replace that sentence with a sharper subtraction of the rounded fact-sheet periods.

Geometry · not astrology

Not a horoscope

The four minutes are not a mood, a birth chart, or a reason the night “feels” different. They are the geometry of a planet that spins and also orbits. Basics of Space Flight calls the shorter spin the equivalent of one solar day per year. JPL uses the skywatching round number: the stars rise 4 minutes earlier each day, about 2 hours earlier in a month, and the stars of summer and winter trade places because Earth moved, not because the stars did. Nothing on this page aims a telescope or sets a watch.

Literacy · two kinds of day

Sidereal day
One full rotation relative to the distant stars. The Earth Fact Sheet lists the sidereal rotation period as 23.9345 hours. NASA Space Place rounds that spin: almost exactly 23 hours and 56 minutes. Basics of Space Flight says rotation relative to the fixed stars is 3 minutes 56.55 seconds shorter than the mean solar day. On this desk the reference star is the direction the meridian pointed at the first noon. The next time the mark faces that star is one sidereal period later.
Mean solar day
The average from noon to the next noon — the Sun back on the same meridian. The fact sheet lists the length of day as 24.0000 hours. Basics of Space Flight: Earth rotates relative to the Sun every 24.0 hours mean solar time, and mean solar time is an average over the non-circular orbit. The leftover after that average, true Sun minus mean Sun, is the equation of time on the analemma desk. This slider uses the mean. It does not draw the equation of time.
The extra turn
Basics of Space Flight calls the shorter spin the equivalent of one solar day per year. Spread over the fact sheet’s 365.256-day sidereal orbit, the step is 360° ÷ 365.256. After one full spin against the star, the mark still has to rotate through that step to face the Sun, because Earth moved along its orbit. The sketch enlarges the step. The “still to the Sun” figure does not. The seasons desk is a different angle: the axis, kept parallel, not this daily catch-up.
Stars rise earlier
JPL’s January 2024 What’s Up: the stars rise 4 minutes earlier each day. In one week a given star rises 28 minutes earlier. In one month the same star rises about 2 hours earlier. The strip draws three nights at one mean solar clock time and stretches the heights so the step can be seen. The heights are not altitudes. The pace in the labels is JPL’s 4 minutes a day. Two days at that pace is 8 minutes. This page does not turn the 4 minutes into a rise time for Campbell.

Sources · public NASA education

Cited, not invented

  • NASA NSSDCA — Earth Fact Sheet — sidereal rotation period 23.9345 hours; length of day 24.0000 hours; sidereal orbit period 365.256 days; orbit eccentricity 0.0167. The clocks use the two periods. The orbit step is 360° divided by 365.256. The circle does not use the eccentricity.
  • NASA Science — Basics of Space Flight, Chapter 2 — Earth rotates relative to the Sun every 24.0 hours mean solar time. Mean solar time averages the variations from the non-circular orbit. Rotation relative to the fixed stars is 3 minutes 56.55 seconds shorter than the mean solar day, the equivalent of one solar day per year.
  • NASA Space Place — How long is one day on other planets? — a solar day is one cycle of daytime to nighttime; on Earth a solar day is around 24 hours. A sidereal day is one full rotation; on Earth a sidereal day is almost exactly 23 hours and 56 minutes. The same page notes that Earth’s orbit is not a perfect circle, so some solar days run a few minutes longer than 24 hours and some a few minutes shorter. This slider stays on the mean.
  • NASA JPL — What’s Up, January 2024 — the stars rise 4 minutes earlier each day. In one week a given star rises 28 minutes earlier than it does tonight. In one month the same star rises about 2 hours earlier. The change happens at a pace of 4 minutes per day because Earth moves in its orbit and the stars are not the ones moving.
