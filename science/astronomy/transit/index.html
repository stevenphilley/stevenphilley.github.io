Transit
and the wobble

Two measurements, from the same orbit. If the planet crosses the star, the star dims by the area ratio δ ≈ (Rp / R★)². A random orientation transits with probability about R★ / a. The crossing itself lasts about (P / π) (R★ + Rp) / a when it is central. Whether or not it crosses, the star still orbits the balance point. For a circular orbit the toward-and-away speed is K = (2πG / P)1/3 (Mp sin i) / (M★ + Mp)2/3. Period and distance stay tied by the same pull, P² ∝ a³ / (M★ + Mp), the general form of the Kepler desk. The methods, without these numbers, are on exoplanets. The star’s size and mass sit on stars. Where that orbit falls in the starlight is the habitable zone. The pull itself is gravity.

Classroom model. One star, one planet, a circular orbit, and a star that is equally bright from center to limb. No limb darkening, no eccentricity, no starspots, no atmosphere on the planet, and no second planet tugging the clock. A dip drawn here is the geometric chord, not a fitted light curve. Not a catalog and not a discovery.

Why the numbers look like that

Small stars, missed orbits, and a mass that is only a minimum.

Depth is an area. Shrink the star and the same planet covers a larger fraction of it. TRAPPIST-1 is about a tenth of the Sun’s radius, so an Earth-size planet is a dip of thousands of parts per million instead of tens. The wobble grows too, because K scales roughly as Mp / M★2/3 and as P−1/3. A light star on a short orbit is tugged harder, as seen from here, than the Sun is by Earth.

Most systems never transit. R★/a for the Earth–Sun preset is 0.4650%, about 1 in 215 random orientations. Jupiter is closer to 1 in 1,119 because it is farther out. A hot Jupiter can be closer to 1 in 10, which is why the early finds were close-in giants. The probability is not the same thing as this page’s inclination. 51 Pegasi b has a measured tilt of 49.8°, so this line of sight misses the star even though a random observer would have had a fair chance.

The sine wave measures Mp sin i, not Mp. Face-on, sin i is 0 and the star’s speed along our line of sight is zero, whatever the planet weighs. Edge-on, sin i is 1 and K is the true mass. Anything between is a minimum: the true mass is larger by 1 / sin i. 51 Pegasi b was announced that way. The m sin i line on the card is the mass this K would imply if you did not already know i.

Instruments, at the precision their papers actually quote. HARPS, in its first-results report, reached a short-term precision of 0.2 m/s during one night and a long-term precision of order 1 m/s (Rupprecht, Pepe, Mayor, Queloz et al., SPIE 5492). An Earth at 9 cm/s is under that long-term floor. ESPRESSO was designed for 10 cm/s. Pepe et al. 2021 demonstrate better than 25 cm/s in a single night and 50 cm/s over months, and they call that performance compatible with a 10 cm/s instrument. At high signal-to-noise they reach 10 cm/s on M stars and 10–20 cm/s on K and G dwarfs. Kepler’s photometer was designed for 20 ppm in 6.5 hours on a 12th-magnitude star, and Caldwell et al. 2010 describe an Earth–Sun equivalent transit as an 80 ppm signal. This desk’s Earth preset, from the fact-sheet radii, is 83.86 ppm.

Literacy · the words on the card

Depth
δ = (Rp / R★)². The fraction of the star’s disk the planet covers, for a uniform disk and a planet fully in front. Parts per million are that fraction times 106. No limb darkening, so the dip is flat-bottomed when the planet is smaller than the star and fully on the disk.
R★ / a
The chance a randomly oriented circular orbit transits, if the planet is treated as a point. Including the planet’s radius gives (R★ + Rp) / a. Both are geometry, not a statement about the inclination you set.
Impact parameter
b = (a cos i) / R★. Zero is a central crossing. The planet misses when |b| is greater than 1 + Rp/R★. i = 90° is edge-on. i = 0° is face-on.
Duration
First contact to fourth, for a circular orbit: (P / π) times the arcsin of (R★ / a) √((1 + k)² − b²) / sin i, with k = Rp/R★. A central transit is close to (P / π) (R★ + Rp) / a. This is the geometric chord, not a fitted light curve.
K and m sin i
K is the semi-amplitude of the star’s toward-and-away speed. For e = 0 it is (2πG/P)1/3 (Mp sin i) / (M★ + Mp)2/3. This desk writes that with the fact-sheet GM values, so G never has to be chosen. m sin i is Mp times sin i, in the planet’s mass unit. It is what a measured K returns when the tilt is unknown.
One orbit, two clocks
Editing the period recomputes the distance. Editing the distance recomputes the period. Either way, P = 2π √(a³ / (GM★ + GMp)). Around the Sun, in years and AU, that is the P² = a³ of the Kepler desk, plus the planet’s own GM, which is small for Earth and visible for Jupiter. The fact-sheet semimajor axis is quoted in the note. The card uses the two-body distance so the period and the distance cannot disagree.

Transit and wobble

A classroom calculator. Circular orbit, uniform star, no limb darkening. The curves are the formulas, not a fitted light curve.

The curves

The curves
LightZoomed on the crossing so the dip is visible. A year-long orbit would hide an Earth-size dip in one pixel. The vertical scale is the depth, labeled in ppm or in percent.
WobbleOne full orbit. Mid-transit is the center, where the sine wave crosses zero. Positive is the star receding. The shaded band, when there is a transit, is the same window as the light curve.
No dipIf this inclination misses the star, the light curve is flat and the duration is blank. The geometric probability stays, because that is about a random observer.
No radius51 Pegasi b loads no planet radius. Depth and duration stay blank until you type one. Typing leaves the preset.

Controls

Controls
PresetsEarth, Jupiter, HD 209458 b, 51 Pegasi b, TRAPPIST-1 e, GJ 1214 b. Earth and Jupiter are edge-on external views. The others use the cited inclination.
UnitsEarth or Jupiter for the planet’s radius and mass. The star stays in solar units. Switching units keeps the same planet.
Period and aKepler’s third law with both masses. The last one you edit wins. The other updates.
Inclination0° is face-on. 90° is edge-on. K scales with sin i. The transit, if there is one, requires i near 90°.
MotionDip sweeps the crossing. Full orbit runs the year, and the light-curve marker is only in frame while the planet is crossing. Pause holds mid-transit.
