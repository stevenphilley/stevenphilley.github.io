Vol. Science · Astronomy · The sphere of influence

Hill
where the secondary takes over

A secondary on a circular orbit carries a sphere inside which it, not the primary, dominates a test particle. The common approximation for a low-mass secondary is rH ≈ a · (m / (3M))1/3. a is the secondary’s semimajor axis, m its mass, M the primary’s mass. When μ = m / (M + m) is much less than 1, the same neighborhood is rH ≈ a · (μ / 3)1/3. That second form is the Hill approximation on the Lagrange desk. Masses, radii, and semimajor axes are the NASA NSSDCA fact sheets, the same figures the escape, Hohmann, Lagrange, and Roche desks already use. For the Sun and Earth the first form is 1,496,565 km (2.151 solar radii, 0.010004 of Earth’s semimajor axis). The μ form is 1,496,563 km. The Moon’s semimajor axis, 384,400 km, is 0.257 of Earth’s Hill radius, so the Moon sits inside. Jupiter’s sphere is 53,147,220 km. Mars’s is 1,084,138 km, and Phobos at 9,378 km is deep inside it, while the Roche desk puts that same orbit between the fluid and rigid limits. Hill is who dominates a test particle. Roche is where a moon of a given density shreds.

Classroom schematic. One plane, top-down from the north, so the orbit runs counterclockwise. The primary sits at the center. The secondary rides a dashed circle of radius a. The Hill sphere is a circle of radius rH centered on the secondary. The full view and the lower panels do not share a scale. Body disks on the full view are marks when a world would otherwise be a speck. On the Hill panel, the circle and a moon’s orbit, when that orbit is large enough to draw, share one scale. A world smaller than a few pixels is a mark, not its radius. Mars–Phobos adds a third panel for Phobos and the Roche band, and that panel has its own scale. The Hill circle is off that panel. Not an ephemeris. Not a mission-design tool.

Not a wall

The edge is a balance, and it is soft

The Hill sphere is not a fence. It is the distance, in this approximation, where the secondary’s gravity and the primary’s tidal field are in the same rough balance for a test particle. Inside it, the secondary tends to dominate. Outside it, the primary does. Orbits near the edge are not stable parking spots. A moon can live well inside and still be ordinary. A test particle near rH wanders off. Nothing here is a precision ephemeris or a mission-design tool. The Roche desk asks a different question: where a fluid or rigid satellite of a given density shreds. The Lagrange desk uses the μ form as the distance from the secondary to L1 and L2, then solves the collinear balance beside it.

The formulas

rH ≈ a · (m / (3M))1/3. Circular orbit. Secondary mass m much smaller than primary mass M. a is the secondary’s semimajor axis about the primary. This is the headline number on every preset. It is a classroom approximation, not a fitted measurement from a spacecraft.

rH ≈ a · (μ / 3)1/3, with μ = m / (M + m). The same neighborhood when μ is tiny, because m / (M + m) and m / M then nearly agree. This is the form the Lagrange desk prints beside its numerical L1 and L2. For Sun–Earth the two forms round to 1,496,565 km and 1,496,563 km. For Sun–Jupiter they round to 53,147,220 km and 53,130,319 km. The gap is the difference between the two approximations. It is not a measurement error.

The inputs. Sun: mass 1,988,400 × 1024 kg, volumetric mean radius 695,700 km. Earth: mass 5.9722 × 1024 kg, volumetric mean radius 6,371.000 km, semimajor axis 149.598 × 106 km. Moon: mass 0.07346 × 1024 kg, semimajor axis 0.3844 × 106 km. Jupiter: mass 1,898.13 × 1024 kg, volumetric mean radius 69,911 km, semimajor axis 778.479 × 106 km. Saturn: mass 568.32 × 1024 kg, volumetric mean radius 58,232 km, semimajor axis 1,432.041 × 106 km. Mars: mass 0.64169 × 1024 kg, volumetric mean radius 3,389.5 km, semimajor axis 227.956 × 106 km. Phobos, in the Mars satellites table: mass 10.6 × 1015 kg, mean orbital distance 9,378 km. This desk keeps the Earth Fact Sheet’s 5.9722 even where another sheet’s Earth column differs.

Hill is not Roche. Hill is where the secondary dominates the primary’s tide for a test particle. Roche is where a satellite of density ρs cannot hold itself together. The Roche desk’s fluid distance is 2.44 R times the cube root of the density ratio, and the rigid distance uses 1.26. For Mars and Phobos those distances are 10,541 km and 5,443 km. Phobos’s 9,378 km sits between them, and also deep inside Mars’s Hill sphere of 1,084,138 km. Both statements use the fact sheets. They do not contradict.

Where this shows up

Sun and Earth. Earth’s Hill radius is 1,496,565 km, 2.151 times the Sun’s volumetric mean radius, and 0.010004 of Earth’s own semimajor axis. In Earth radii that is 234.903. The μ form, 1,496,563 km, is the number on the Lagrange desk. The Moon at 384,400 km is 0.257 of the sphere. The Earth–Moon button draws that orbit. The Sun–Earth button is the sphere itself.

Earth and the Moon. The preset is still Earth’s sphere about the Sun. The Moon is the comparison, not the secondary in the formula. The Moon’s own Hill sphere, with Earth as the primary and the Moon as the secondary, is a different arithmetic: 61,525 km on the m/(3M) form and 61,274 km on the μ form. The Lagrange desk prints that 61,274 km beside Earth–Moon L1. This page does not draw the Moon’s own sphere. It draws the sphere the Moon sits inside.

Sun and Jupiter. Jupiter’s Hill radius is 53,147,220 km, 76.394 solar radii and 0.068271 of Jupiter’s semimajor axis. That is 0.355 of Earth’s semimajor axis, from dividing the two fact-sheet kilometer figures. NASA’s account of the Trojan asteroids puts one swarm nearly 60° ahead of Jupiter, at L4, and another at L5. Those points sit on Jupiter’s orbit. They are not inside the Hill circle drawn around Jupiter. The Lagrange desk draws L4 and L5.

Mars and Phobos. Mars’s Hill radius is 1,084,138 km, 1.558 solar radii and 0.004756 of Mars’s semimajor axis. Phobos’s mean orbital distance, 9,378 km, is 0.00865 of that sphere, about 320 Mars radii in from the edge to a moon at 2.767 Mars radii. The close panel draws the Roche band from the same coefficients as the Roche desk: fluid 10,541 km, rigid 5,443 km, Phobos between them. Phobos’s own Hill sphere, Mars as primary, comes out near 17 km on this formula, on the order of the sheet’s axes 13.0 × 11.4 × 9.1 km. The point-mass picture is a poor fit at that size. This preset does not draw that circle.

Sun and Saturn. Saturn’s Hill radius is 65,405,072 km, 94.013 solar radii and 0.045673 of Saturn’s semimajor axis. The μ form is 65,398,842 km. Rhea’s semimajor axis on the Roche desk is 527,040 km, which is 0.00806 of this sphere, so Rhea sits inside it. The Roche question for Rhea is the other way: 527,040 km is outside both shredding distances. Hill says Saturn dominates the Sun out here. Roche says Rhea is too far from Saturn to be pulled apart.

Literacy · a sphere, not a mission

Hill sphere
The region around a secondary where that body, rather than the primary, dominates a test particle. This page uses rH ≈ a (m / (3M))1/3. The edge is an approximation for a circular, low-mass case. It is not a surface you cross.
Two forms
The μ form replaces m/M with m/(M+m). They match when the secondary is light. Sun–Earth differs by 2 km after rounding. Sun–Jupiter differs by 16,901 km. Both are printed. The headline is the m/(3M) form.
Primary radii and a
rH / Rprimary uses the fact-sheet volumetric mean radius of the body at the center of the big orbit. rH / a is the same distance in units of the secondary’s semimajor axis. Earth’s 0.010004 a is the hundredth-of-an-orbit scale.
A moon inside
Earth–Moon and Mars–Phobos compare a moon’s mean distance to the planet’s Hill sphere about the Sun. The Moon is at 0.257 rH. Phobos is at 0.00865 rH. Inside does not mean safe from Roche. Phobos is the pair that shows both.
Trojans
Jupiter’s L4 and L5 lead and trail by about 60°, on the orbit. The Hill circle is a neighborhood around Jupiter, 0.068 of that orbit’s radius. The swarms and the sphere are different drawings. Lagrange has the five points.
What the circles leave out
Eccentricity, inclination, the pull of every other body, and the stability boundary, which is softer and more complicated than one radius. Orbits are circles. The full view’s disks are marks when the true radius would vanish. The lower panels have their own scales. The Mars close-up is the Roche band, still inside the Hill sphere, which does not fit on that panel. Mean fact-sheet values. Not an ephemeris. Not a mission-design tool.

Formulas

Formulas
m / (3M)rH ≈ a · (m / (3M))1/3. Headline on every preset. a, m, and M are the fact-sheet values named above. Rounded to the nearest kilometer.
μ / 3rH ≈ a · (μ / 3)1/3, μ = m / (M + m). Printed beside the headline. This is the Lagrange desk’s Hill approximation. The two forms agree when μ is tiny.
Primary radiirH divided by the primary’s volumetric mean radius. For these presets the primary is the Sun, so the unit is solar radii.
Units of arH / a. Earth’s figure is 0.010004. Jupiter’s is 0.068271. That is the fraction of the orbit the sphere occupies.
Moon / r_HOn Earth–Moon and Mars–Phobos, the moon’s mean distance divided by the planet’s Hill radius. Less than 1 means the moon’s orbit is inside the sphere.
