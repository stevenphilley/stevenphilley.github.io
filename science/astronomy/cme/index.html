Vol. Science · Astronomy · The wind

From the Sun
to Earth

The solar wind leaves the Sun all the time. A coronal mass ejection is a separate cloud of plasma and magnetic field. NOAA says CME speeds range from slower than 250 km/s to as fast as near 3,000 km/s. The same agency’s solar-wind page puts the usual slow wind in the equatorial plane at about 400 km/s, and coronal-hole wind from 500 to 800 km/s. NASA’s visualization uses about 300–500 km/s for the slow wind and about 500–800 km/s for the fast wind. Marshall prints 300 km/s over streamers, about 400 km/s as the ordinary figure, and 800 km/s over coronal holes. The readout divides the Earth Fact Sheet semimajor axis, 149.598 × 106 km, by the speed on the slider. That is a straight line. It is not an arrival. The sunspot rhythm is the solar cycle. What the wind meets at Earth, the magnetosphere and the oval, is the aurora desk. Particles already trapped on that field are the Van Allen belts. This page is the wind and the ejections that disturb them.

Two sentences, one sketch

The field is wound up

Named for Parker · 27 days in NOAA’s sentence

NASA Science says that as the solar wind gushes out, it drags the Sun’s magnetic field with it, and the stretched field ends up in a spiral because the Sun rotates. Charged particles follow those spiraled field lines. NOAA says that because the Sun rotates every 27 days, the wind becomes a complex spiral of high and low speeds and high and low densities, like the skirt of a twirling ballerina. When high speed wind overtakes slow speed wind, NOAA calls that a corotating interaction region. This canvas does not draw that catch-up. The radial dashed line is there so Earth can be found. It is not the spiral.

What the words mean

Solar wind
NOAA: a continuous flow, mainly protons and electrons in a plasma, with the solar magnetic field embedded in it. Coronal holes produce high speed wind from 500 to 800 km/s. In the equatorial plane the most common state is the slow wind, about 400 km/s. Marshall says about 400 km/s, about 1 million miles per hour, high at 800 km/s over coronal holes and low at 300 km/s over streamers. NASA’s Scientific Visualization Studio puts the slow average at about 180–300 miles per second (about 300–500 km/s), from streamers, and the fast average at about 300–500 miles per second (about 500–800 km/s), from coronal holes. NASA Science says the wind exceeds one million miles per hour, and names coronal holes, active regions, and streamers as sources. The “about 1 million” sentence and the “exceeding one million” sentence are both quoted. They are not averaged, and this page does not convert either one.
Coronal mass ejection
NOAA: a large expulsion of plasma and magnetic field from the corona. It can eject billions of tons and carry an embedded field stronger than the background solar-wind field. Speeds range from slower than 250 km/s to as fast as near 3,000 km/s. The fastest Earth-directed ones can reach Earth in as little as 15–18 hours. Slower ones can take several days. NASA Science says CMEs are immense clouds blasted out at over a million miles per hour, often after a flare, and that the fastest travel at millions of miles per hour. Directed at Earth, NASA’s sentence is as little as 15 hours, and several days for the slowest. A CME faster than the background wind can drive a shock. This page does not compare the slider with a background speed, because these pages do not print one background number.
Straight-line time
The Earth Fact Sheet lists a semimajor axis of 149.598 × 106 km. The readout divides that distance by the selected speed and reports hours and days. The same sheet prints a J2000 semimajor axis of 1.00000011 AU. This page does not convert that figure. Perihelion and aphelion, 147.095 and 152.100 × 106 km, are on the sheet and are not used. The division is not NOAA’s 15–18 hours and not NASA’s 15 hours. At the fast end the division rounds below 15.00 hours. The page leaves the difference in place.
Earth-directed or glancing
NASA Science says CMEs can detonate in different directions and move at different speeds. NOAA says size, speed, and direction are the parameters forecasters infer from coronagraph imagery, and that Earth-impact likelihood is part of that work. The two buttons move a blob onto the line or off it. The offset is a classroom shift so the cartoons can be told apart. It is not an angle, not a cone, and not a hit or a miss. The division does not change.
The last stretch
NOAA says imminent arrival is first seen at DSCOVR, in the L1 orbital area, and that the jump there can often provide 15 to 60 minutes advanced warning of shock arrival at Earth. Marshall puts the L1 point about 1.5 million km, about 1 million miles, sunward of Earth, and says ACE monitors the wind there. This page does not divide 1.5 million km by the slider, and it does not show a live alert. The 15 to 60 minutes sentence stays a different clock from the straight-line time.
What this drawing refuses
NOAA classifies geomagnetic storms on a five-level space-weather scale and discusses them in a forecast. This page does not assign a G-scale, a Kp index, or a storm rate. It is not a live CME catalog. It is not a mission design tool. NOAA says high speed winds bring geomagnetic storms and slow winds bring calm. That sentence is quoted and not applied to the slider. The solar-cycle desk is the sunspot rhythm, and the place NASA says storms are more common near solar maximum. The aurora desk is the magnetosphere and the oval the wind stands off. The Van Allen desk is the trapped particles inside that shield. People on the ground are the subject of NASA’s solar-storms sentence: the magnetic field and the atmosphere protect them. Nothing here is a forecast.

Where the sentences come from

  • NOAA SWPC — Coronal mass ejections — plasma and magnetic field, billions of tons, speeds from slower than 250 km/s to near 3,000 km/s. Fastest Earth-directed arrivals in as little as 15–18 hours. Slower CMEs take several days. Larger CMEs can reach nearly a quarter of the space between Earth and the Sun. A CME faster than the background wind can drive a shock. Direction is inferred from coronagraph imagery. DSCOVR at L1 can often provide 15 to 60 minutes advanced warning. Storms use a five-level scale. This page assigns none of that scale.
  • NOAA SWPC — Solar wind — protons and electrons in a plasma. Coronal holes, 500 to 800 km/s. Equatorial slow wind, about 400 km/s. The Sun rotates every 27 days, and the wind becomes a spiral like a ballerina’s skirt. High speed wind overtaking slow wind makes a corotating interaction region. High speed winds bring geomagnetic storms and slow winds bring calm. This page does not turn the slider into that sentence.
  • NASA Science — Solar storms and flares — CMEs are immense clouds at over a million miles per hour. They can detonate in different directions. The fastest travel at millions of miles per hour. Directed at Earth, as little as 15 hours; the slowest, several days. They can drive geomagnetic storms and the aurora. The magnetic field and the atmosphere protect people on the ground.
  • NASA Science — What is the solar wind? — speeds exceeding one million miles per hour. Sources named there: coronal holes, active regions, and coronal streamers. The wind drags the magnetic field into a spiral because the Sun rotates. The spiral is named for Eugene Parker, whose theory is dated 1958. At Earth most of the wind is deflected. Some particles get through and can contribute to the aurora.
  • NASA Scientific Visualization Studio — Fast and Slow Solar Wind — slow wind averages about 180–300 miles per second (about 300–500 km/s), from streamers, and is the usual state in the equatorial plane. Fast wind averages about 300–500 miles per second (about 500–800 km/s), from coronal holes. Both unit pairs are quoted. This page does not convert one into the other.
  • NASA Marshall Solar Physics — The Solar Wind — about 400 km/s, about 1 million miles per hour. High, 800 km/s, over coronal holes. Low, 300 km/s, over streamers. The L1 point is about 1.5 million km, about 1 million miles, from Earth. ACE monitors the wind there. This page does not divide that distance.
  • NASA NSSDCA — Earth Fact Sheet — semimajor axis 149.598 × 106 km. That is the distance in the division. The J2000 semimajor axis 1.00000011 AU is printed and not converted. Perihelion 147.095 and aphelion 152.100, in the same units, are not used.
  • Solar cycle — the sunspot rhythm. Storms and ejections are more common near solar maximum on NASA’s solar-storms page. Not a forecast, and not this travel time.
  • Aurora — the magnetosphere and the oval. The wind in that drawing is the flow this page times. A schematic, not a Kp forecast.
  • Van Allen belts — trapped particles inside that shield. A schematic of the cited altitude spans. Not a dose calculator.
