Planet
days

A solar day brings the Sun back to the same meridian. A sidereal rotation brings a star back. They differ because the planet moves along its orbit while it spins. Each NASA NSSDCA planet sheet prints a sidereal rotation period, a length of day, and a sidereal orbit period. This page computes 1/Tsolar = 1/Tsidereal − 1/Torbit. Venus and Uranus print a minus sign on the rotation, and that minus sign flips the first term. The arithmetic is printed beside the sheet’s length of day. The two are not averaged. Earth’s pair, 23.9345 hours beside 24.0000, is the sidereal desk. Mercury’s arithmetic solar day is about two Mercury years. The comparison table’s orbital period is a tropical year. It is not the period in the formula.

What the difference is

Prograde: the Sun is a little ahead of the star. Mercury, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, and Neptune spin the same way they orbit. After one turn against the stars, the planet has moved along its orbit, so the marker still has to catch the Sun. The solar day is longer than the sidereal rotation. Earth’s sheet prints 23.9345 hours and 24.0000 hours. The sidereal desk is that pair, and the four-minute skywatching round number. This page does not replace those two printings.

Retrograde: the minus sign flips the first term. The comparison notes say negative numbers indicate retrograde rotation, backwards relative to the Earth. The per-planet notes say a minus sign indicates retrograde rotation. Venus prints −5832.6 hours. Uranus prints −17.24 hours. Orbit and spin then both carry the Sun off the marker, so the length of day is shorter than the absolute sidereal period. The sheet’s length of day is a positive count of hours, noon back to noon. The signed arithmetic is negative. This page prints the positive length beside the sheet, and does not average them.

Mercury is about two years to a solar day. The Mercury sheet prints a sidereal rotation of 1407.6 hours, a length of day of 4222.6 hours, and a sidereal orbit of 87.969 days. The arithmetic of the rotation and the orbit is 4223.376 hours, which is 2.0004 sidereal orbits. Two printed orbits are 4222.512 hours. The printed periods are 1.4999 orbits per sidereal rotation, near 3/2, not exactly 3/2. 4223.376 rounded to one decimal place is 4223.4, not 4222.6. The three figures stay side by side.

The giant planets name a reference system. Jupiter’s sidereal rotation is 9.9250 hours, marked “System III (1965.0) coordinates.” Saturn’s is 10.656 hours, marked “Saturnian System III coordinates.” Uranus’s −17.24 hours and Neptune’s 16.11 hours are each marked “Magnetic coordinates (as determined by the Voyager 2 Radio Science experiment).” The length of day line on those sheets does not repeat the asterisk. This page uses the starred rotation in the formula and says so. It does not invent a cloud-tracked day.

The comparison table’s year is tropical. Its notes say the orbital period is the time from one vernal equinox to the next, also known as the tropical orbit period. Earth’s comparison cell is 365.2 days. Earth’s own sheet prints a sidereal orbit of 365.256 days and a tropical orbit of 365.242 days. The formula uses 365.256. The tropical figures stay in the second table. They are not averaged with the sidereal ones. The precession desk is why a tropical year and a sidereal year differ.

What the words mean

Length of day
The fact-sheet notes call it the average time in hours for the Sun to move from the noon position in the sky at a point on the equator back to the same position. On Earth, that note says, this defines a 24 hour day. The Earth sheet prints 24.0000. This slider starts at a schematic noon and walks to the next one.
Sidereal rotation
One turn relative to the fixed stars, not relative to the Sun. The comparison notes use that wording. A minus sign means retrograde. Earth’s 23.9345 hours is the sidereal desk. The star on this drawing is a direction, not a catalog star.
The formula
1/T_solar = 1/T_sidereal − 1/T_orbit. T_orbit is the sheet’s sidereal orbit period, converted to hours by × 24. The 24 is the Earth sheet’s length of day, 24.0000, not the sidereal 23.9345. For a negative T_sidereal the first term flips sign. The positive result is the length this page sets beside the sheet.
Mercury’s 3:2
Near three sidereal rotations in two sidereal orbits, so one solar day is about two Mercury years. The printed ratio is 1.4999 orbits per rotation, not exactly 3/2. The resonances desk is a period ratio of two orbits. This 3:2 is a spin and one orbit. The Mercury desk is the phase, from Earth.
Retrograde spin
Venus and Uranus. Not the apparent backward loop of an outer planet, which is the retrograde desk. The Venus sheet prints obliquity 177.36°. The Uranus sheet prints 97.77°. The comparison notes say almost 180 degrees, and almost on its side. This drawing does not tip the globe. The Venus and Uranus desks are phases.
What the circle leaves out
Eccentricity, so a real solar day runs long and short of the mean. Earth’s equation of time is the analemma desk. Obliquity, so seasons are the seasons desk. The tropical year, so precession is a different leftover. The Moon’s longer phase cycle is the synodic desk, the same kind of catch-up on an orbit around Earth. Gas-giant cloud belts that drift relative to System III. A point on the equator, which is the note’s wording for the length of day, not a weather report. Not an ephemeris.
