Vol. Science · Astronomy · The coast

Hohmann
two burns, one ellipse

The cheap way from one circular orbit to another, in the classroom, is not a straight line. It is half an ellipse, tangent to the inner circle and tangent to the outer circle, with the central body at one focus. Two impulses do the work. The first burn leaves the departure circle. The craft coasts. The second burn joins the arrival circle. For circles of radii r₁ and r₂, the transfer semi-major axis is at = (r₁ + r₂) / 2. The coast lasts half an ellipse period, t = π √(at³ / μ). Speeds come from a circular orbit, v = √(μ / r), and from vis-viva, v = √(μ (2/r − 1/a)). Around the Sun, μ is the Sun Fact Sheet GM, 132,712 × 106 km3/s2. The radii are each planet’s orbital-parameters semimajor axis in 106 km, and the periods used for the phase angle are the sidereal orbit periods on those same NSSDCA fact sheets. Earth 149.598 × 106 km to Mars 227.956 × 106 km takes 258.88 days and 5.594 km/s. That is about 8.5 months of Earth’s 365.256-day sidereal year. The year of one circle is the Kepler desk. The speed to stay on a circle, or to leave a world, is the Escape desk.

Classroom schematic assuming circular, coplanar orbits and impulsive burns. It ignores planetary gravity wells and patched conics, inclination, eccentricity, and real mission design. It is not mission-planning software. Top-down, from the north, so motion runs counterclockwise. The Sun (or Earth, in the around-Earth case) sits at the focus of the transfer ellipse, not at the ellipse’s center. Planets are marks, not worlds drawn to size. Play is a time-lapse of the coast, about 8 seconds, not a clock. The 400 km low-orbit altitude in the around-Earth case is a round classroom example. The Earth Fact Sheet does not print it. The geosynchronous radius is computed from that sheet’s GM and its 23.9345-hour sidereal rotation period.

Classroom idealization · not mission-planning software

Circles, one plane, two kicks

Nothing here is a launch window a flight dynamics team would sign, a fuel load, or a path through a planet’s gravity well. Real orbits are eccentric and tilted. A real spacecraft falls into the departure planet’s well, burns for minutes rather than in an instant, and often needs a mid-course trim. This page does not do patched conics. It draws two circles in one plane and the ellipse that kisses both. The Kepler desk is the year of one of those circles. The Escape desk is the local speed at one radius. The solar map is where the worlds sit on a date.

The formulas

at = (r₁ + r₂) / 2. The transfer ellipse is tangent to the inner circle at one end of its long axis and tangent to the outer circle at the other. Those two radii are the periapsis and the apoapsis, so the semi-major axis is their average. The central body is at the focus, a distance |r₂ − r₁| / 2 from the ellipse’s center.

t = π √(at³ / μ). A full ellipse has period 2π √(a³ / μ). The Hohmann coast is half of that, from one apsis to the other. μ is GM of the body at the focus. For planets, μ is the Sun Fact Sheet’s 132,712 × 106 km3/s2. For the around-Earth case, μ is the Earth Fact Sheet’s 0.39860 × 106 km3/s2.

v = √(μ / r) on a circle. v = √(μ (2/r − 1/a)) on the ellipse, which is vis-viva. At the inner end the ellipse is faster than the inner circle, so an outward trip speeds up. At the outer end the ellipse is slower than the outer circle, so the second burn speeds up again to join that circle. An inward trip slows down at both ends. Δv₁ and Δv₂ are the absolute differences. Total Δv is their sum.

θ = 180° − (360° × t / Ptarget). During the coast the target moves 360° × t / P. The craft moves 180° of the transfer. The target must already lead by θ at departure so the two meet at the far apsis. A negative θ means the target is behind the departure body, along the direction of motion. Ptarget is the fact-sheet sidereal period for a planet. Around Earth, P is the circular period 2π √(r³ / μ) from the same GM and radius. The window spacing is the synodic period |P₁ P₂ / (P₁ − P₂)|, from those same periods. When the formula’s θ passes ±180°, the drawing places the target at that angle modulo 360°. The printed θ stays the formula.

Literacy · a coast, not a mission

Which radius
Each fact sheet prints a semimajor axis twice. The Mean Orbital Elements (J2000) line is in AU. The orbital-parameters table is in 106 km, with a rounded ratio to Earth beside it. This desk uses the 106 km line, because μ is in km3/s2. It does not multiply the AU line by a kilometer conversion, and it does not average the two prints. Earth’s kilometer line is 149.598 × 106 km. The AU line, 1.00000011, is the Kepler desk’s slider.
Impulsive burn
The model changes speed in an instant, at one point, and then coasts. Δv is that change of speed. A rocket burns for minutes and climbs while it burns. Those minutes are not on this page. Direction is along the circle: speed up means prograde, slow down means retrograde. Nothing here points out of the plane.
Phase lead
How far ahead the target must sit at the moment of the first burn, in the direction of motion. Earth to Mars, with these inputs, is 44.34°. Mars’s fact-sheet period is 686.980 days, and the coast is 258.88 days, so Mars moves 135.66° while the craft moves 180°. The lead is the difference. Earth to Venus comes out negative: Venus is faster, so it starts behind.
Window spacing
The synodic period of the two orbits, |P₁ P₂ / (P₁ − P₂)|. Earth and Mars, from 365.256 and 686.980 days, give 779.93 days. That is how often the same phase angle comes back. It is not a calendar of launch dates, and it is not an ephemeris.
Around Earth
The focus is Earth. μ and the volumetric mean radius, 6,371.000 km, and the sidereal rotation period, 23.9345 hours, are the Earth Fact Sheet. The geosynchronous radius is the circle whose period equals that spin: r³ = μ T² / (4π²). The low orbit is that volumetric radius plus the altitude slider. 400 km is the slider’s start. It is a classroom example, not a line on the sheet. This is still one focus and two circles. It does not model the atmosphere or a launch from the ground.
What the ellipse leaves out
Eccentricity, so the real path is not the fact-sheet semimajor axis drawn as a circle. Inclination, so the planes do not match and a third burn would be needed. Planetary gravity, so you do not simply start already on a heliocentric circle. Finite burns, gravity assists, mid-course corrections, and arrival into orbit around the target world. The Escape desk is the speed at one world. This desk starts after you are already on the departure circle. Not a trajectory to fly.

On the drawing

On the drawing
FocusThe Sun, or Earth. The circles are centered on it. The ellipse is not. Its center sits toward the outer apsis.
Inner and outer circlesThe two circular orbits, to scale with each other. A far arrival makes the departure orbit a small ring. That scale is the point.
TransferThe bright half of the ellipse, the half the craft flies. The faint other half is the rest of the same ellipse, not a second route. An outward trip uses the upper half. An inward trip uses the lower half, so the coast stays counterclockwise.
CraftThe moving mark. At the start it sits on the departure body. At the end it sits on the target.
Phase arcThe angle at the focus between the departure body and the target at the first burn, drawn modulo 360°. The readout’s θ is the formula, which can pass a full turn when the target is much faster.
NNorth, the view. Motion runs counterclockwise, the same sense as the other top-down desks.
