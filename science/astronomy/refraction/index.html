Refraction
the lifted star

A star near the horizon is not where the straight line says it is. Air is denser toward the ground, so a ray bends concave to the Earth, and the eye looks back along the last tangent and sees the star higher. Bennett (1982) takes the apparent altitude ha in degrees and returns the lift in arcminutes: R = cot(ha + 7.31/(ha + 4.4)). Sæmundsson (1986) starts from the true altitude h: R = 1.02 cot(h + 10.3/(h + 5.11)). Meeus, Astronomical Algorithms, chapter 16, multiplies both by (P/1010)×(283/(273+T)), with P in hPa and T in °C. At 1010 hPa and 10°C the factor is 1. Bennett at apparent altitude 0° is 34.5′. At 45° it is 0.995′. At 90° the formula’s residual is −0.001′. It is not forced through zero. The same air is the airmass desk, which dims the star as well as lifting it. Sunrise’s fixed −0.833° is the twilight desk.

Educational calculator. Not a forecast and not an ephemeris. Bennett and Sæmundsson are standard approximations for a standard atmosphere. Meeus reports Bennett within 0.07′ of Garfinkel from the zenith to the horizon, and Sæmundsson within about 0.1′ of Bennett. Real horizon refraction varies a lot with temperature inversions. A ground temperature and a pressure do not describe that profile.

The typed altitude is apparent, so Bennett’s formula is the lift: 34.5′ at 1010 hPa and 10°C. The true altitude is −0.575°. Sæmundsson at that true altitude is 34.4′. The gap is 0.037′. Sæmundsson at the typed altitude, as if that number were already true, is 29.0′. The factor (P/1010)×(283/(273+T)) is 1.0000. The hour-angle method compares the center at −0.841° with −0.267°. Day length is 2H/15 hours, and cos H = (sin a − sin φ sin δ) / (cos φ cos δ). At 37.29° N and declination 0° the extra daylight, sunrise and sunset together, is 5.78 min. The simple estimate 2R / (15°/h · cos φ · cos δ) is 5.78 min. It assumes sin H = 1. NOAA’s fixed pair, −0.833° against −0.267°, is 5.69 min and does not follow the sliders. The same pair against 0° is 8.38 min, refraction and semi-diameter together. The horizontal diameter stays 32′, twice NOAA’s 16′. With that true diameter and the lower limb at apparent 0°, the apparent vertical diameter is 26.8′. The flattening, Bennett on the lower limb minus Bennett on the upper limb, is 5.18′. Bennett(0°) − Bennett(32′) is 6.06′, a different subtraction: there the apparent separation is held at 32′.

Why the ray bends

Denser air, slower light
The refractive index of air rises as the air gets denser, and light travels a little slower there. A ray from a star meets faster air above and slower air below, so it curves concave toward the ground. The observer, looking back along the final tangent, places the star above its geometric direction. At the zenith the ray is radial and that lift is zero, apart from the formula’s −0.001′ residual at 90°. Near the horizon the path is long and grazing. Bennett’s evaluation at 0°, 1010 hPa, and 10°C is 34.5′.
Two formulas, two altitudes
Bennett answers “I measured this apparent altitude. How far has the air lifted it?” Sæmundsson answers “the ephemeris says this true altitude. What lift do I add?” Typing 0° into both is two questions. Bennett at apparent 0° is 34.5′. Sæmundsson at true 0° is 29.0′. Feed Sæmundsson the true altitude Bennett implies, −0.575°, and it returns 34.4′. The gap on the card is that comparison.
The standard air
The factor is (P/1010)×(283/(273+T)). 283 is 273+10, so 1010 hPa and 10°C give 1. 1010 hPa is 101.0 kPa. Cold dense air multiplies the lift. The arctic button, −30°C and 1030 hPa, is 40.9′ at the horizon. The Mauna Kea button, 615 hPa and 0°C, is 21.8′. Thin air lifts less.
Sunrise is already below
NOAA’s sunrise standard is −0.833° for the center: about 34′ of refraction plus about 16′ of solar semi-diameter. This page’s Bennett evaluation plus that same 16′ puts the center at −0.841° when the upper limb sits on the apparent horizon. The Sun you see touching the horizon is geometrically below it. The horizontal diameter here is 32′, twice the 16′ allowance. A geometric diameter from distance is the angular-size desk. The two 32′ figures are not the same measurement.
Extra daylight
The hour-angle method: cos H = (sin a − sin φ sin δ) / (cos φ cos δ), and the day length is 2H/15 hours. The extra minutes are sunrise and sunset together, the difference between the center at −(R + 16′) and the center at −16′. −16′ is −0.267° at three decimals. At Campbell, 37.29° N, and declination 0°, that difference is 5.78 min. The simple estimate, 2R / (15°/h · cos φ · cos δ), assumes sin H = 1 and matches the hour-angle result at the equinox. Off the equinox the Sun’s path is shallower and the simple estimate runs short. NOAA’s fixed −0.833° against −0.267° is 5.69 min here and does not follow P and T. Against 0° the same NOAA altitude is 8.38 min, refraction and semi-diameter together.
The flattened Sun
Refraction is a vertical lift, and the lift is larger at a lower altitude. The lower limb is raised more than the upper limb. The left and right limbs sit at the same altitude, so the horizontal diameter stays 32′. Holding the true diameter at 32′ with the lower limb at apparent 0°, Bennett’s apparent vertical diameter is 26.8′. The flattening, R on the lower limb minus R on the upper limb, is 5.18′. Holding the apparent separation at 32′ instead, Bennett(0°) − Bennett(32′) is 6.06′. That is a different subtraction.
Green flash, Novaya Zemlya
The air also disperses the Sun. The last sliver at sunset can be a green flash, because the red image sets while a green image is still lifted clear of the horizon. A strong temperature inversion can duct the ray and show a distorted Sun when it is geometrically well below the horizon. That is the Novaya Zemlya effect. Both are the temperature profile along the path. This page names them and does not compute them. The factor (P/1010)×(283/(273+T)) has no inversion in it.

Sources

  • Bennett, G. G., Journal of Navigation 35, 255–259 (1982) — astronomical refraction from apparent altitude. R = cot(ha + 7.31/(ha + 4.4)), R in arcminutes, ha in degrees. Meeus reports the formula within 0.07′ of Garfinkel’s algorithm from the zenith to the horizon. This page evaluates the formula. It does not re-fit that bound.
  • Sæmundsson, Þ., Sky & Telescope 72, 70 (1986) — refraction from true altitude. R = 1.02 cot(h + 10.3/(h + 5.11)). Meeus reports it within about 0.1′ of Bennett. The gap on this page is computed, not copied from that sentence.
  • Jean Meeus, Astronomical Algorithms, chapter 16 — both formulas, and the factor (P/1010)×(283/(273+T)). P is in millibars, which are hPa. 1010 hPa and 10°C give a factor of 1. 1010 hPa is 101.0 kPa.
  • NOAA — Solar calculation details — sunrise and sunset use 0.833° for refraction at the horizon plus the size of the solar disk. The NOAA solar equations use zenith 90.833°. The about 34′ and the about 16′ are that allowance, shared with the twilight and airmass desks. They are not a new measurement of the solar diameter.
  • NASA NSSDCA — Earth Fact Sheet — obliquity to orbit 23.44°. The June and December buttons use that tilt as the solar declination. The local sky stores Campbell as 37.29° N. This desk uses that classroom latitude. It is not a new survey.
