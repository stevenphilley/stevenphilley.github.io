Exoplanets

Planets beyond our solar system — usually orbiting another star in the Milky Way. NASA’s public archive now lists more than 6,000 confirmed worlds (the tally keeps rising), plus thousands of candidates still waiting on follow-up. This desk is a literacy schematic: how we find them, and a handful of famous confirmed examples. Pair with the nearest-stars yardstick — Proxima b is the closest known exoplanet, still about four light-years out.

    Glossary · starter terms

    Exoplanet
    A planet outside our solar system. Most confirmed cases orbit another star; a few are free-floating (rogue) or circle pulsars.
    Confirmed vs candidate
    A candidate is a likely signal that still needs follow-up. Confirmed worlds have passed extra checks so a false positive (eclipsing binary, starspot, noise) is unlikely.
    Transit
    Planet crosses the star’s disk and the light curve dips. Depth hints at radius; repeat dips give the period. Alignment is required — we miss most systems this way.
    Radial velocity
    The star wobbles toward and away from us. Spectral lines shift blue then red. That gives a minimum mass (m sin i) and the period.
    Habitable zone
    The Goldilocks band where a rocky planet could hold liquid water. Red-dwarf zones hug the star; Sun-like zones sit near 1 AU. Climate and atmosphere still decide.
    Hot Jupiter
    A gas giant on a few-day orbit — 51 Pegasi b’s class. Common in early RV samples because they tug hard and transit often. Not like our Jupiter.
    Mini-Neptune / super-Earth
    Worlds between Earth and Neptune in size. Super-Earths are often rocky-ish; mini-Neptunes keep thick envelopes. The galaxy is full of them; our solar system is not.
    Naming
    Host designation + a lowercase letter. The first planet found is b (the star is the unspoken A). Earth as an exoplanet would be Sun d.
    Kepler / TESS / JWST
    Kepler surveyed one deep field; TESS watches bright nearby dwarfs. JWST does not hunt thousands of new planets — it reads atmospheres of worlds we already have.
    Archive, not this page
    For the live tally and tables, use the NASA Exoplanet Archive. Counts change weekly.
