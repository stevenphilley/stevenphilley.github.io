Vol. Science · Astronomy · The crater

Impacts
and the scale

A diameter and a speed are an energy, and an energy is not yet a crater. The mass of a sphere is m = π/6 · ρ · L³. The kinetic energy at the speed you set is E = ½mv². One megaton of TNT on this page is 4.184 × 1015 J. Collins, Melosh, and Marcus print 4.18 × 1015 J. The transient crater and the final rim are their pi-scaling, equations 21, 22, and 27 in the 2005 Earth Impact Effects Program paper. The angle is measured from the horizontal. 45° is the most probable angle in that program. The velocity floor is escape speed: the Earth Fact Sheet lists 11.186 km/s, and the program’s minimum template is 11.2 km/s. Typical asteroid encounters in the paper are 12–20 km/s. The program’s typical-asteroid template is 17 km/s. The top of the slider is 72 km/s, their maximum for an object bound to the Sun. Speed on an orbit is the vis-viva desk. The coasting speed that leaves a body is the escape desk. A grain that becomes a streak, not a crater, is the meteor desk.

Educational calculator. Simplified scaling. Order-of-magnitude estimates. Not a hazard assessment, not a damage map, and not a statement that a crater forms. The paper says the leading constants are a best estimate inside 0.8 to 1.5, not known to three decimal places. This page does not compute an airburst altitude. For a real near-Earth object, use NASA CNEOS and the Planetary Defense Coordination Office.

Equation 21 gives a transient crater of 1.17 km at the pre-impact surface. Under 2.56 km, equation 22 multiplies by 1.25, so the final rim is 1.46 km. The Earth Impact Database rim is 1.186 km. That cited rim sits inside the paper’s 0.8–1.5 range on the leading constant.

8.34 Mt uses 4.184 × 10^15 J. Collins et al. 2016, as corrected by Kring, is 8.6 Mt for a heavier iron mass at the same 42 m and 15 km/s. The whole-Earth recurrence from their equation 3 is about 570 years at this energy.

42 m is under the 1 km cutoff, so Collins et al. would run their entry procedure. Iron is the strong end of that relation. A small, strong body can reach the ground intact in their account. This page does not compute the airburst altitude.

What the scaling does

A sphere, a speed, and two diameters.

Energy. The speed on the slider is the speed in ½mv². In the paper that speed, called v₀, is the speed at the top of the atmosphere. The crater formulas use the speed at the surface after the atmosphere has had its say. For a body of 1 km or larger, Collins et al. say entry has no significant influence, so the two speeds are the same in their treatment. Under 1 km, this page still drops the slider speed straight into equation 21. It does not slow the body down. The crater number is what the scaling says if that speed is the surface speed.

Transient crater. Equation 21 is Dtc = 1.161 (ρi/ρt)1/3 L0.78 v0.44 gE−0.22 sin1/3θ, with L in meters, v in meters per second, and Dtc in meters, measured at the pre-impact surface. gE here is 9.81 m/s². For water the 1.161 becomes 1.365. The paper says both constants are a best estimate inside 0.8 to 1.5.

Final rim. If the transient crater is 2.56 km or smaller, equation 22 is Dfr ≈ 1.25 Dtc. Above 2.56 km, equation 27 is Dfr = 1.17 Dtc1.13 / Dc0.13, with Dc = 3.2 km and both diameters in kilometers. The two pieces do not meet exactly at the join. Water does not get either collapse law. The program’s marine target also has a rock seafloor under a water depth. This desk has no water depth, so the water button is only the water-layer cavity.

Literacy · the words on the readout

Megaton
This page divides joules by 4.184 × 1015. Collins et al. print 4.18 × 1015. Brown et al. 2013 use 4.185 × 1012 J per kiloton. The three differ by about a tenth of a percent, which is smaller than the crater-scaling band.
Angle
Degrees from the horizontal, the way the paper measures θ. A vertical impact is 90°. Equation 21 scales the transient crater with sin1/3θ, so 45° is smaller than vertical. The program calls 15° the shallowest angle it associates with a circular crater. The slider still runs down to 5° and says the scaling is the same formula.
Recurrence
Equation 3, TRE ≈ 109 E0.78 years, E in megatons, somewhere on Earth. It is their fit to the Near-Earth Object Science Definition Team (2003) size distribution. It is not a forecast for one place. The 2018 NASA Tunguska workshop concluded that the interval between Tunguska-class events is millennia, not centuries. This line stays the 2005 fit.
Atmosphere
Collins et al. apply their breakup procedure only under 1 km. Above that, they say entry does not significantly change the body’s energy. Under 1 km, breakup begins when ram pressure exceeds strength. Strength in their program rises with density from 1000 to 8000 kg/m³. Cometary ice is the weak end. Iron is the strong end, and a small, strong body can reach the ground intact. They define an airburst as the altitude where the body has spread to seven times its original diameter. This desk does not solve that altitude.
What the rim leaves out
Drag, ablation, a strewn field, melt, ejecta, shaking, and the blast wave are all in the 2005 program and not on this page. The gravity-regime scaling is meant for craters larger than a couple of hundred meters. A final rim under that is an extrapolation. The constant band is only the paper’s 0.8–1.5 range on the leading factor, carried through the same exponents. It is not a full error bar. Comparing a fresh rim-to-rim diameter with an eroded terrestrial structure needs the caution the paper already gives.

Sources · the formulas and the presets

Cited, not invented

  • Collins, Melosh, and Marcus, Meteoritics & Planetary Science 40, 817–840 (2005) — Earth Impact Effects Program. Equation 1 is the kinetic energy, π/12 · ρ · L³ · v², which is ½mv² for a sphere. They print 1 Mt = 4.18 × 1015 J. Equation 3 is TRE ≈ 109 E0.78 years. Equation 21 is the transient crater, constant 1.161 in rock and 1.365 in water, proportional to sin1/3θ. Equation 22 is Dfr ≈ 1.25 Dtc for a simple crater. Equation 27 is the complex-crater rim, used when the transient diameter is greater than 2.56 km, with Dc = 3.2 km. Target densities: sedimentary 2500, crystalline 2750, water 1000, and a marine seafloor at 2700 that this page does not model. Rocky asteroids about 2000–3000 kg/m³, iron about 8000, comets about 500–1500. Asteroid speeds about 12–20 km/s. Comet speeds about 30–70 km/s. Maximum for a body bound to the Sun, 72 km/s. Their entry procedure is only for impactors under 1 km. The constants 1.161 and 1.365 are a best estimate inside 0.8 to 1.5.
  • Earth Impact Effects Program — the same authors’ calculator. Density templates: 1000 ice, 1500 porous rock, 3000 dense rock, 8000 iron. Speed templates: minimum 11.2 km/s, typical asteroid 17 km/s, typical comet 51 km/s, maximum 72 km/s. Most probable angle 45°. Diameter templates used as familiar lengths here: double-decker bus 5 m, Chelyabinsk 20 m, Nelson’s Column 52 m, Queen’s Tower 87 m, Wembley Stadium 320 m, Ben Nevis 1.3 km, Jersey 10 km, Isle of Wight 20 km, and a Chicxulub template of 14 km. The program’s 20 m Chelyabinsk template is not the JPL estimate.
  • NASA NSSDCA — Earth Fact Sheet — escape velocity 11.186 km/s. Volumetric mean radius 6,371.000 km, so the diameter drawn as a familiar length is 12,742 km. Mean surface gravity 9.820 m/s². Equation 21 on this page uses 9.81 m/s². The −0.22 power makes that difference negligible.
  • NASA JPL — Additional details on the 15 February 2013 fireball — about 18 m, about 11,000 tons, 18.6 km/s, peak brightness at 23.3 km, about 440 kilotons. An update on the same page revises the estimate to 17 m, 10,000 tons, and nearly 500 kilotons. The fragments are ordinary chondrites. This page does not assign them a measured bulk density.
  • Brown and others, Nature 503, 238–241 (2013) — Chelyabinsk about 19 m, the range they print is 17–20 m, energy about 500 ± 100 kilotons. They take 1 kiloton of TNT as 4.185 × 1012 J.
  • Morrison, NASA/TM–2018, Tunguska workshop (NTRS 20190002302) — abstract: 10–20 megatons, airburst height about 10 km, and, for a stony body like Chelyabinsk, a diameter of roughly 50–80 m. The interval between such events is millennia, not centuries. No crater. Talks inside the same memorandum quote other diameter bands. The 50 m button is the low end of the abstract’s range, not a preferred reconstruction.
  • Kring, LPI Contribution 2040, chapter 11 (2017) — Barringer energies in the literature run from a fraction of a megaton to over 60. The Collins et al. 2016 row, with Kring’s mass correction, is a 42 m projectile, 15 km/s, 3.2 × 108 kg, and 8.6 Mt. Artemieva’s disruption cases in the same table are different diameters and speeds, around 10–11 Mt in the 2006 row and 7–15 Mt in 2009. This page’s iron density is the program’s 8000 kg/m³, not that 3.2 × 108 kg.
  • Meteoritical Bulletin — Barringer — writeup from the Earth Impact Database: D = 1.186 km, age 0.049 ± 0.003 Ma.
  • USGS Astrogeology — Meteor Crater sample collection — 1.2 km diameter, 180 m deep, formed about 50,000 years ago. The caption also gives an approximate impactor of about 30 m at 12–20 km/s and an energy of over 2 million tons of TNT. That caption and the Kring table are not the same estimate. Both are cited. The button follows the 42 m, 15 km/s model.
  • Morgan, Bralower, Brugger, and Wünnemann, Nature Reviews Earth & Environment (2022) — Chicxulub is an approximately 200 km structure. The asymmetric shape suggests an oblique impact and an energy of about 1023 J. The 14 km button is the program template, not a figure from this review.
  • NASA CNEOS and NASA Planetary Defense — where a real close approach is tracked. This desk is not that catalog.
