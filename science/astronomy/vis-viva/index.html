Vis-viva
speed on the ellipse

On a Keplerian ellipse the speed is not one number. It is v = √[μ (2/r − 1/a)]. μ is GM of the body at the focus. r is the current distance from that focus. a is the semimajor axis. At periapsis, rp = a(1−e) and the craft is fastest. At apoapsis, ra = a(1+e) and it is slowest. A circular orbit at the same r would move at vc = √(μ/r). Leaving from that r, and not coming back, takes vesc = √(2μ/r). The year of the ellipse is still Kepler’s P = 2π √(a³/μ). It does not depend on e. For Earth around the Sun, a is 149.598 × 106 km and e is 0.0167 on the Earth Fact Sheet. μ is the Sun Fact Sheet GM, 132,712 × 106 km3/s2. Vis-viva at the printed perihelion, 147.095 × 106 km, is 30.287 km/s. The sheet lists a maximum orbital velocity of 30.29 km/s. At aphelion, 152.100 × 106 km, it is 29.291 km/s. The sheet lists 29.29 km/s. When e = 0 the formula collapses to √(μ/a). When r = a, the speed is √(μ/a) even if the orbit is not a circle. The circle and the escape speed at one altitude are the Escape desk. The half-ellipse between two circles is the Hohmann desk. The year in years and AU is the Kepler desk. Why the same Δv changes the energy more where v is larger is the Oberth desk. What a chosen Δv costs in propellant is the rocket desk. A flyby that changes heliocentric speed by turning v∞, with no burn, is the gravity assist desk.

Classroom schematic. Point-mass, two-body, one focus. No drag, no oblateness, and no third body. Not an ephemeris and not a mission planner. Top-down, from the north, so motion runs counterclockwise. The central body sits at the focus, not at the center of the ellipse. The empty focus is marked. The body is a dot unless the orbit sits just above the volumetric radius, as in the 400 km example. The arrow length is proportional to speed. On Earth’s orbit that change is about 3 percent, so the arrow barely shortens. The 400 km altitude is a classroom example. The Earth Fact Sheet does not print it. The ellipse with e = 0.5 is not a real satellite.

What the formula is

v = √[μ (2/r − 1/a)]. μ is GM of the central body. r is the distance from the focus right now. a is the semimajor axis, half the long axis of the ellipse, not the current distance. The same a fixes the period. Moving r along the ellipse changes the speed and does not change the year.

vp = √[μ (2/rp − 1/a)] with rp = a(1−e). va = √[μ (2/ra − 1/a)] with ra = a(1+e). Periapsis is the near end. Apoapsis is the far end. For a planet around the Sun those ends are perihelion and aphelion. For the Moon they are perigee and apogee.

vc = √(μ/r) is a different orbit: a circle that passes through the same point. vesc = √(2μ/r) is the coasting speed that leaves and does not return. Escape is circular times √2, at that same r. The Escape desk is those two speeds at one altitude. This page is the speed the ellipse already has.

When e = 0, vis-viva collapses to √(μ/a). Then r is a everywhere, 2/r − 1/a becomes 1/a, and the square root is the circular speed. When r = a, v = √(μ/a) even if e is not 0. The craft is not at the center. It is one semimajor axis from the focus, which happens twice on every ellipse that is not a circle. The speed there matches a circular orbit whose radius is a. The period P = 2π √(a³/μ) is that same a, and it ignores e.

The round numbers

The sheet and the square root

The Earth Fact Sheet prints maximum orbital velocity 30.29 km/s and minimum 29.29 km/s. Vis-viva at the printed perihelion and aphelion, with the printed semimajor axis and the Sun’s GM, is 30.287 km/s and 29.291 km/s. Rounded to the hundredth, those are the sheet’s figures. The printed eccentricity 0.0167 does not land exactly on the printed perihelion. a(1−e) is 147.100 × 106 km. The sheet’s perihelion is 147.095 × 106 km. The slider can sit on either distance. Both speeds still round to 30.29 km/s.

The Moon sheet lists 1.082 km/s at mean perigee and 0.970 km/s at mean apogee. Vis-viva with the Earth Fact Sheet GM is 1.076 km/s and 0.964 km/s. The sheet’s note says those distances were used for the maximum and minimum velocities. This desk does not adjust μ to force the match. Mars does match: 26.500 km/s and 21.970 km/s round to the sheet’s 26.50 and 21.97.

Words

Vis-viva
The speed on a Keplerian orbit, from μ, the current r, and the semimajor axis a. It does not ask where the craft is along the ellipse except through r. Faster when closer. Slower when farther.
Focus
The central body sits at one focus. The other focus is empty. The center of the ellipse is halfway between them, a distance a·e from each focus. A circle is the case e = 0, when the two foci and the center are the same point.
Periapsis and apoapsis
The nearest and farthest points. rp = a(1−e) and ra = a(1+e). Their average is a. Around the Sun the names are perihelion and aphelion. Around Earth they are perigee and apogee.
Circular speed
√(μ/r) at the current distance. Same place, different orbit: a circle instead of this ellipse. On the ellipse the craft is faster than that circle near periapsis and slower than it near apoapsis.
Local escape
√(2μ/r). The coasting speed that reaches infinity. It depends on r and μ, not on a. Climbing out of a low orbit, escape speed falls, and so does circular speed. Their ratio stays √2.
What the picture leaves out
Air, the planet’s bulge, other planets, and a finite size except as a warning when r falls inside the volumetric mean radius. The 400 km circle is the Earth Fact Sheet radius 6,371.000 km plus a classroom altitude. It is the low orbit on the Hohmann desk, not a line the fact sheet prints. The e = 0.5 ellipse is a shape for the formula. It is not a satellite.

The burn

Oberth

The same Δv buys more energy where this speed is higher. Δε = v·Δv + ½(Δv)². Not a mission.

The propellant

Rocket

The rocket equation turns a chosen Δv into a mass ratio. Ideal, constant exhaust speed, no drag. Not a mission.

The flyby

Gravity assist

An unpowered hyperbola turns v∞. Heliocentric speed changes because the planet keeps moving. Not this ellipse.

Semimajor aHalf the long axis. Changing it leaves the preset. The period and both end speeds change. r is kept if it still lies on the new ellipse.
Eccentricity eFrom 0 to just under 1. Zero is a circle. rp = a(1−e) and ra = a(1+e) follow.
Distance rClamped between the near end and the far end. On a fact-sheet preset the ends are the printed periapsis and apoapsis when those differ from a(1±e).
rp and raType the two ends. a becomes their average. e becomes (ra − rp) / (ra + rp). Apoapsis has to be farther than periapsis.
