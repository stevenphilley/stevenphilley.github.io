Vol. Science · Astronomy · The snow line

The frost
line

The papers cited here call this boundary the snow line. It is the distance in a young disk beyond which water ice can condense. Hartmann, Ciesla, Gressel, and Alexander 2017 print Hayashi’s 1981 minimum-mass solar nebula temperature as T = 280 K × (r / 1 AU)−1/2. Podolak 2009, and Sasselov and Lecar 2000, say water ice in that model condenses near 170 K and that the radius is 2.7 AU. The arithmetic of those two temperatures is (280/170)2 = 2.713 AU. This page prints both and does not average them. The 170 K figure depends on pressure. Podolak says 150 to 180 K, depending on density. Hartmann and coauthors say about 120 to 170 K. Today’s orbits are the NASA NSSDCA J2000 semimajor axes. The band between Mars and Jupiter is the belt. Its gaps are the asteroids desk. The habitable zone is a different boundary.

The profile is T = 280 K times (r / 1 AU) to the power −1/2, the Hayashi minimum-mass solar nebula temperature as Hartmann, Ciesla, Gressel, and Alexander 2017 print it. The teaching scale is 1, so the normalization is the printed 280 K. It is not a measured disk. Water ice in the 2.7 AU sentence is roughly 170 K. Podolak 2009 says that figure is for a solar-composition gas at a pressure typical of protostellar disks, and that the condensation temperature can be 150 to 180 K depending on density. Hartmann and coauthors print about 120 to 170 K for typical midplane conditions. The two ranges are not averaged. At 170 K this profile computes 2.713 AU. Podolak, and Sasselov and Lecar 2000, print 2.7 AU for the 170 K step. 2.713 AU and 2.7 AU are not averaged. Mercury, Venus, Earth, and Mars sit inside the line on this drawing. Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune sit outside it. The band from Mars to Jupiter is NASA Science’s “between Mars and Jupiter,” not a measured belt edge. The asteroids desk is that belt. Rock inside, ice outside

Inside the line, the building blocks are rock and metal. A NASA Astrobiology note says planets formed inside the snow line will generally be rocky and small, and that outside it the grains get coated by icy water and grow larger, leading to gas and ice giant planets. That is the classroom picture. It is not a rule that every world follows. The densities below are the fact sheets, not a cutoff this page invented.

A core can grow where the solids pile up. Hartmann and coauthors say the concentration of solids just outside the snow line may seed the early stages of giant-planet formation. Podolak, retelling Stevenson and Lunine 1988, says that mechanism would put the line around 5 AU if it formed Jupiter. Hartmann and coauthors also name 5 AU as that assumed location, and they say disk temperatures put a solar-mass snow line closer to 1–3 AU, with Jupiter possibly migrating. This page does not pick a birthplace.

The line moved, and the models disagree. NASA Science, reporting Martin and Livio, says the snow line in our solar system currently lies in the middle of the asteroid belt, and that previous models had it much closer to the Sun 4.5 billion years ago. The same page says a conventional model starts the line perhaps at least one billion miles out. This page does not turn “at least one billion miles,” or “roughly 93 million miles” for an AU, into a distance. The table below keeps the printed figures side by side.

Earth’s water is a separate sentence. The same NASA page says less than one percent of our planet’s mass is locked up in water, and that even that may have been delivered by comets and asteroids after Earth’s initial formation. “May have been” stays. Martin and Livio’s model, as that page reports it, forms Earth from rocky debris inside the snow line. The page also says chance determined the precise end result. This desk does not decide which delivery happened.

What the words mean

Snow line
The distance beyond which water ice is stable in the disk. The papers use that name. This desk’s URL says frost line. The two labels are this page’s, for one boundary. The habitable zone is starlight and an equilibrium temperature at a planet. It is not this line.
170 K
The temperature Podolak, and Sasselov and Lecar, use for the 2.7 AU step. Podolak says it is for a solar-composition gas at a pressure typical of protostellar disks. He also prints 150 to 180 K, depending on density. Hartmann and coauthors print about 120 to 170 K. The slider can visit those ends. It does not measure a pressure.
Teaching scale
A multiplier on the printed 280 K, from 0.5 to 2. At 1 the profile is the one Hartmann and coauthors print. Moved, it is a classroom knob. It is not a measured disk and not a young-Sun luminosity. The exponent stays −1/2, because that is the printed profile. Podolak’s equilibrium, flux falling as 1/r2, is why a blackbody temperature goes as r to the −1/2. The blackbody desk is the curve and L = 4πR2σT4. This page does not recompute 280 K from a solar radius.
The belt
NASA Science says most asteroids orbit between Mars and Jupiter. The band on the drawing is that sentence, from Mars’s axis to Jupiter’s. It is not a measured inner edge or outer edge. The histogram, and the Kirkwood gaps, are the asteroids desk.
What the cut leaves out
Height above the midplane, a vertical snow line, viscous heating, dust growth, and migration. Other ices are left out: this desk does not quote a condensation temperature for carbon monoxide, carbon dioxide, or ammonia. Orbits are today’s J2000 semimajor axes, drawn as ticks, not as ellipses. The dwarf planets and the classical Kuiper belt sit far outside every distance in the table above. The ocean worlds are moons now, not this disk. Mean densities. Not a disk model and not an ephemeris.

Sources

Where the numbers come from

  • Hartmann, Ciesla, Gressel, and Alexander 2017 — Space Science Reviews, also arXiv:1708.03360. They print Hayashi’s temperature T = 280 (r / 1 AU)−1/2 K, and a surface density this desk does not draw. The abstract expects water to freeze out at distances greater than 1–3 AU from solar-type stars. Typical midplane snow lines fall near 120–170 K. Solids just outside the line may seed giant-planet cores. They also name an assumed 5 AU line, and say a solar-mass snow line may have sat closer to 1–3 AU, with Jupiter possibly migrating.
  • Podolak 2009 — “The Location of the Snow Line in Protostellar Disks.” Hayashi 1981, water near 170 K for a solar-composition gas at a typical disk pressure, giving 2.7 AU. Depending on density, 150–180 K. The neighborhood of 2 AU, and 3 AU if the opacity is raised by a factor of 5, from Lecar, Podolak, Sasselov, and Chiang 2006. Around 5 AU if Stevenson and Lunine 1988 is the Jupiter-forming mechanism. The neighborhood of 10 AU, from Lodders 2004.
  • Sasselov and Lecar 2000 — The Astrophysical Journal 528, 995. Hayashi’s minimum-mass solar nebula condenses ice below 170 K at 2.7 AU. Their dusty models bring the line to the neighborhood of 1 AU. Larger accretion rates can push it beyond 4 AU, and they say those rates do not match the observed disks.
  • NASA Science — Why is Earth so dry? — 17 July 2012, reporting Martin and Livio. The snow line currently lies in the middle of the asteroid belt. Previous models had it much closer to the Sun 4.5 billion years ago. A conventional model starts perhaps at least one billion miles out. An AU is roughly 93 million miles on that page. Less than one percent of Earth’s mass is water, and even that may have been delivered by comets and asteroids. This desk quotes those sentences and does not convert the miles.
  • NASA Astrobiology — Rethinking the snow line — 19 July 2016. Inside the snow line, planets are generally rocky and small. Outside, icy water coats the grains, leading to gas and ice giants. The note is also about a disk around another star. No solar-system distance is taken from that image.
  • NASA Science — Asteroid facts — most asteroids orbit between Mars and Jupiter. The band on this drawing is that sentence. The gaps are the asteroids desk.
  • NASA NSSDCA — Planetary Fact Sheet — comparison table, updated 18 March 2025. Earth’s density there is 5,514 kg/m³. This page uses the Earth sheet’s 5513 instead, and says so.
