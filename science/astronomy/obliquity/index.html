Obliquity

Obliquity is how far a planet’s equator is tipped from its own orbital plane, with north set by the right-hand rule. The NASA NSSDCA sheets print that angle. Earth’s is 23.44°. Held parallel for one year, that lean is the seasons desk. The slow walk of where the axis points is the precession desk, which keeps the angle fixed. Venus prints 177.36°. Uranus prints 97.77°. Those are past 90°, the retrograde convention. Drag the axis, or pick a planet, and the tropics and polar circles follow the angle.

Educational schematic. A classroom side view at one solstice: the axis is drawn tipped toward the Sun, not walked around a year. Not an ephemeris, not a climate model, not a weather forecast, and not a navigation aid. Tropics and polar circles are this page’s subtraction from the angle. They are not a surveyed latitude. The fact-sheet numbers below were read from NASA’s pages. They are not averaged with the coarser comparison table. Colors are a key, not photographs.

What the angle does

Up to 90°, the seasonal angle is the obliquity. The Sun’s overhead point at the solstice of this drawing sits at that latitude. Tropics at ±obliquity. Polar circles at ±(90 − obliquity). On Earth the northern ones are the Tropic of Cancer and the Arctic Circle. This page prints both as ± the angle, and does not rename them on the other bodies. Poleward of the polar circles, that solstice is midnight sun in summer and polar night in winter. At 0° the equator lies in the orbital plane, and there is no such band.

Past 90° is the retrograde convention. The comparison notes measure the axis from a line perpendicular to the orbit, north pole by the right-hand rule, so the angle can pass 90°. Venus and Uranus do. The seasonal swing on this drawing is 180 minus the obliquity. For Venus, 180 − 177.36 = 2.64, and the sheet’s inclination of the equator is 2.64. For Uranus, 180 − 97.77 = 82.23, and the sheet prints 82.23. Pluto’s sheet prints 119.51 beside 57.47, and 180 − 119.51 = 60.49, which is not 57.47. The circles follow 60.49. They do not replace 57.47. The two are not averaged.

A larger seasonal angle is a stronger season. NASA’s Milankovitch page says the greater Earth’s axial tilt, the more extreme the seasons, because the summer hemisphere receives more solar radiation and the winter hemisphere less. This desk uses that sentence for the comparison with Earth’s 23.44°. It does not publish a separate scale of “weak” and “strong,” and it does not model a climate.

The spin’s minus sign is a different page. Venus’s sidereal rotation is −5832.6 hours, and Uranus’s is −17.24 hours. Those minus signs, and the solar day, are the planet days desk. The backward loop of Mars in Earth’s sky is the retrograde desk. This page is only the tilt of the axis.

Uranus, Venus, Earth, Mars

Uranus, almost on its side. The Uranus sheet, updated 2 October 2024, prints obliquity 97.77° and an inclination of the equator of 82.23°. The comparison notes say Uranus rotates almost on its side relative to the orbit. On this drawing the solstice subsolar latitude is 82.23°. Tropics at ±82.23°. Polar circles at ±7.77°. 90 − 82.23 = 7.77, and 97.77 − 90 = 7.77. Those are this page’s subtractions, not a second sheet. The Uranus phase desk names 97.77° and does not tip the disk.

Venus, almost 180°. The Venus sheet, updated 11 January 2024, prints obliquity 177.36° and an inclination of the equator of 2.64°. The comparison notes say Venus rotates retrograde, opposite the other planets, so the tilt is almost 180 degrees, with its north pole pointing downward. The seasonal swing is the small one, 2.64°. Tropics at ±2.64°. Polar circles at ±87.36°. The minus sign on the spin is the planet-days desk, not this tilt.

Earth’s angle changes, slowly. NASA Science (27 February 2020) says that over the last million years Earth’s obliquity has varied between 22.1 and 24.5 degrees, and that the axis is currently tilted 23.4 degrees, about halfway between those extremes, in a cycle that spans about 41,000 years. A greater tilt makes the seasons more extreme. The Earth Fact Sheet prints 23.44°. The 23.4 and the 23.44 are not averaged. That page does not name the Moon as what holds the tilt in that range. PIA17859 (9 January 2014) says Earth’s obliquity appears to have been limited to small variations from its current value because of the stabilizing gravitational influence of the Moon. The caption does not print 22.1 to 24.5. The Milankovitch page does not print the Moon sentence. This desk does not merge them. The precession desk is the direction the axis points, not this change in the angle.

Mars varies more, on that caption’s wording. PIA17859 says Mars’ obliquity is currently about 25 degrees, not much different from Earth’s 23 degrees, and that numerical calculations suggest the near-agreement is a coincidence. It says Mars’ obliquity varies chaotically, probably reaching values greater than 60 degrees and lower than 10 degrees, and that if the calculations are correct it was greater than 25 degrees for most of the Solar System’s history. The Mars sheet prints 25.19°. The caption’s “about 25” and “about 23” stay the caption’s roundings. This page does not add a tighter range, and it does not drop “probably” or “if the calculations are correct.”

What the words mean

Obliquity
The comparison notes: the angle the axis is tilted relative to a line perpendicular to the planet’s orbit, north pole by the right-hand rule. The parameter notes: the angle between the body’s equator and the body’s orbital plane, north by the right-hand rule, at J2000. This page uses the number in the obliquity column. It does not treat the two sentences as two measurements.
Inclination of the equator
The parameter notes: the angle between the equator and the orbital plane with north defined as the pole above the plane of the solar system, also called axial tilt, at J2000. Saturn’s sheet does not print it. Venus, Uranus, and Pluto print a different number from their obliquity. Only Venus and Uranus match 180 minus the obliquity.
Tropics
Latitudes ± the seasonal angle, where the Sun can stand overhead at the solstice of this drawing. Up to 90° that angle is the obliquity. Past 90° it is 180 minus the obliquity. Earth’s northern tropic is the Tropic of Cancer. This page does not rename the lines on other bodies.
Polar circles
Latitudes ±(90 − the seasonal angle). Earth’s northern circle is the Arctic Circle. On this page, from 23.44°, that is 66.56°. Poleward of those circles the drawing’s solstice is midnight sun or polar night. At 0° the circles sit on the poles, and there is no band.
Midnight sun, polar night
A season when the Sun stays up, or stays down, through a turn of the planet. On this schematic that happens poleward of the polar circles, and only when the seasonal angle is not zero. It is geometry of the tilt, not a sunrise almanac. Refraction is the twilight desk.
What the side view leaves out
A year of motion. The seasons desk walks Earth’s 23.4° around the Sun. Eccentricity, so distance is not this tilt. The precession cone, so the aim among the stars can change while this angle stays 23.44° on that desk. The Milankovitch cycle of the angle itself, quoted above and not animated. Weather, ice, and a climate model. The Moon’s 6.68° obliquity to its own orbit. Not an ephemeris.
