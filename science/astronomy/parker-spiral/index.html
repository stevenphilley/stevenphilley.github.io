Solar wind &
the Parker spiral

The solar wind is a plasma that leaves the Sun all the time, carrying the solar magnetic field with it. NOAA SWPC says the most common slow wind in the equatorial plane is about 400 km/s, and that coronal holes run from 500 to 800 km/s. The Sun rotates, so a parcel that left earlier stays on its radial ray while the footpoint moves on. The field, frozen into the flow, is wound into an Archimedean spiral. Eugene Parker set that picture out in 1958. The nickname is the garden-hose angle. The straight-line time across one Earth distance, without this angle, is the solar wind and CME desk. That page does not compute a pitch.

This desk uses the constant-speed equatorial form tan ψ = Ω r / v. ψ is the angle of the field from the radial direction. Ω is the Sun’s sidereal angular speed. The Sun Fact Sheet prints a sidereal rotation period of 609.12 hours. Divided by 24 that is 25.38 days. The footnote assigns that period to 16° latitude, not to the equator. The same footnote’s equatorial term is 14.37 degrees per day, and 360 divided by 14.37 is 25.05 days. The calculator opens on 25.38 days. 25.05 is not substituted. One astronomical unit is the IAU 2012 length, 149,597,870,700 m.

At 400 km/s and 1 AU the arithmetic is a spiral angle of 46.98°, a radial transit of 4.33 days (103.89 hours), and a solar turn of 61.40°. That turn is the western footpoint offset, Ω r / v. A familiar round figure is about 45°. 46.98 does not round to 45. They are not averaged. NOAA’s page says the Sun rotates every 27 days. Feeding 27 days into this formula at the same speed and distance gives 45.21°. That period is the sentence on the solar-wind desk. It is not this sidereal period, and 45.21° is not substituted for 46.98°.

The radial time is distance over speed. It ignores the acceleration near the Sun, and it does not stop the winding inside a source surface. Potential-field models often place that surface near 2.5 solar radii. With the sheet’s volumetric mean radius, 695,700 km, 2.5 radii is 0.0116 AU. The slider starts at 0.1 AU, outside that surface, and the formula does not subtract it. Charged particles travel more easily along the field than across it, so a flare near the western footpoint is the one magnetically connected to the observer. An eastern-limb flare is not on that line. What the wind does when it arrives is the aurora desk and the magnetospheres desk. The sunspot rhythm is the solar cycle.

Why the field winds

What the solar wind is
NOAA SWPC: a continuous flow, mainly protons and electrons in a plasma, with the solar magnetic field embedded in it. In the equatorial plane the most common state is the slow wind, about 400 km/s. Coronal holes produce high-speed wind from 500 to 800 km/s. The same sentences are on the solar wind and CME desk, which divides the Earth Fact Sheet’s 149.598 × 106 km by a speed and does not compute an angle. At 400 km/s this page’s 1 AU, the IAU length, also prints 4.33 days and 103.89 hours. The two distances are not substituted for each other. They happen to agree at those two decimals.
Why it spirals
A parcel keeps the radial direction it had when it left. The footpoint rotates out from under it. Parker 1958 showed that a radial wind with the field frozen in, and a rotating Sun, winds that field into an Archimedean spiral. In the equatorial plane, with a constant radial speed and the inner boundary dropped, tan ψ = Ω r / v. ψ is measured from the radial direction, not from the azimuth.
The three angles
ψ = arctan(Ω r / v) is the garden-hose angle at the observer. The Sun’s turn during the transit is Ω r / v itself, in degrees, because the time is r / v. That same number is the western footpoint offset. At the opening, 46.98° is the spiral and 61.40° is the turn. They are not the same angle. The tangent of 46.98° is the radian measure of 61.40°.
The period
609.12 hours is the Sun Fact Sheet’s sidereal period, adopted at 16° latitude. The footnote gives the rate as (14.37 − 2.33 sin²L − 1.56 sin⁴L) degrees per day. At the equator that rate is 14.37 degrees per day, and the period is 25.05 days. The page can be edited from 20 to 40 days. It opens at 25.38. NOAA’s 27 days is the synodic sentence on the solar-wind page. At 400 km/s and 1 AU it would give 45.21°. It is not used.
What is left out
The real wind accelerates through the corona. The speed is not constant, and it is not purely radial near the Sun. Inside a source surface, often taken near 2.5 solar radii, the field is still shaped by the corona and the plasma roughly corotates. 2.5 times 695,700 km is 0.0116 AU. This formula starts the winding at the center and does not subtract that length. Differential rotation, a tilted dipole, and a real source surface are not in the drawing.
Streams and a CIR
NOAA says high-speed wind overtaking slow wind makes a corotating interaction region. The overlay draws a 750 km/s spiral and a 400 km/s spiral whose footpoints differ by a classroom 25°. Those two curves meet once. At 25.38 days the meeting is at 0.8725 AU. The band beyond that meeting is the cartoon of the fast wind running into the slow wind ahead of it. The 25° is not a measured stream. The 750 is not a measured wind.
Why the west matters
The spiral trails the rotation. Viewed from the north, the Sun turns counter-clockwise, and the footpoint that connects to an observer on the right is counter-clockwise of that observer: west, on the solar disk. Solar energetic particles follow the field much more readily than they cross it. A flare near that western longitude is on the line that reaches the observer. A flare on the eastern limb is not. At the opening the offset is 61.40°, on the western half of the disk. Farther out the same product can exceed a full turn. The card then says where the footpoint sits once those turns are removed. This page does not score a flare, and it does not read a magnetogram.
