Magnitude
the backwards scale

Apparent magnitude m is how bright something looks from here. Absolute magnitude M is how bright it would look from a standard distance of 10 parsecs. The difference is the distance modulus, m − M = 5 log10(d / 10 pc). At 10 pc, m = M. The scale runs backwards: a smaller number is brighter, and the brightest objects go negative. Five magnitudes is a factor of 100 in flux, because 100.4·5 = 100. Slide the distance and M and watch m move. Distance in parsecs is the reciprocal of parallax in arcseconds, the rule on the parallax desk. The nearest stars are the neighborhood that yardstick measures, and the stars desk is what their letters mean. Luminosity versus temperature, which is not the same axis as visual magnitude, is the HR diagram.

Classroom schematic. Visual (V) band: Johnson V for the star ticks, and the visual magnitudes printed on the NASA fact sheets. Extinction is ignored. Dust between us and a star would make m fainter than this formula. The naked-eye lines are approximate ranges for an average person, not a forecast for a city or a night. Why a city sky is brighter is the light-pollution desk. Not a research catalog, not a finder chart, and not a sky survey.

Why the numbers run backwards

Brighter is smaller

Hipparchus sorted stars into six classes, 1 the brightest and 6 the faintest. Pogson kept that order and made it a logarithm: a gap of five magnitudes is a factor of 100 in flux. A smaller m is the brighter object. Past the bright end the numbers go negative. The Sun at −26.74 is not “minus bright.” It is so much flux that the old classes ran out of room.

Absolute magnitude uses the same backwards scale, after the star is moved to 10 pc in the formula. A star can look faint because it is far, and still have a small or negative M. Extinction is a third dimming, and this page leaves it out.

Literacy · words on the scale

Apparent magnitude m
How bright the object looks from the observer. Visual magnitude on this page is the V band, not a bolometric sum over every wavelength.
Absolute magnitude M
The apparent magnitude the object would have at 10 parsecs, with extinction left out. NASA’s Marshall activity sheet calls that standard distance 10 parsecs, about 32.6 light-years. The Sun’s fact-sheet value is +4.83.
Distance modulus
m − M = 5 log10(d / 10 pc). Flux falls as 1/d², and magnitudes are a base-10 log of flux, so the distance shows up as that factor of 5. At d = 10 pc the log is zero and m = M. Dust is not in the formula.
Flux ratio
100.4·Δm, which is the same number as 100Δm/5. Δm = 5 gives 100. Δm = 1 gives about 2.512. The inverse is Δm = 2.5 log10(ratio).
Parallax tie-in
d in parsecs is 1 divided by the annual parallax p in arcseconds. At 10 pc, p = 0.1″. The parallax desk draws that angle. Very small distances make a huge angle; that is not a stellar parallax.
Naked-eye limit
The faintest star an average person can see, as a magnitude. NASA’s Imagine the Universe says that under ideal conditions that is about 6.0 to 6.5. Sky & Telescope’s account of Bortle class 9 puts an inner-city sky at about 4.0 or brighter. Both are approximate. The light-pollution desk is why the city number is brighter.

  • ESA Science & Technology — Stellar distances — d(parsec) = 1 / p(arcsecond). 1 parsec = 3.26 light-years. Sirius is the worked example at parallax 0.37921″.   • IAU 2012 Resolution B2 — The astronomical unit is exactly 149597870700 m. The unrounded quotient is 3.261563777…. NASA and ESA round the same length to 3.26 ly. The slider uses 3.2616 so a typed light-year matches the sentence.
  • Sky & Telescope — How dark is your night sky? — Bortle class 9, an inner-city sky, has a limiting magnitude of 4.0 or less. Approximate. The idea of a naked-eye limit under a bright sky is the light-pollution desk.
