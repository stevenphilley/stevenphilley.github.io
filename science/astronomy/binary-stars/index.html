Vol. Science · Astronomy · The pair

How a star
gets weighed

A star’s mass is not something you put on a scale. Almost every stellar mass in a textbook comes from a pair. Two stars orbit one balance point. The size of that orbit and the time around it fix the total, M₁ + M₂ = a³ / P², with a in astronomical units, P in years, and the mass in solar masses. That is Kepler’s third law for two bodies. The Kepler desk is the one-sun form, P² = a³. How far each star sits from the balance point splits the total: the heavier star stays closer. A spectrograph sees only the toward-and-away part, so the amplitude shrinks by sin i. When the orbit is nearly edge-on and the stars are large enough to cover one another, the light dips. Those three views — visual, spectroscopic, and eclipsing — are the same ellipse.

Two bodies and one ellipse. The total mass is a³/P² when a is in AU, P is in years, and the mass is in solar masses. A year, when a day count is converted, is 365.256 days, Earth’s sidereal orbit period on the NASA fact sheets the Kepler desk uses. The classical form is tied to the Gaussian year; 365.256 days is the unit this site already uses, and the difference is far smaller than the mass uncertainties quoted here. The center of mass splits the axis in the inverse ratio of the masses. Each star’s circular speed for its own axis is 2π a / P. Periastron and apastron speeds scale that by √((1+e)/(1−e)) and √((1−e)/(1+e)). The radial-velocity semi-amplitude is that circular speed times |sin i| / √(1−e²). The curve is offset by K e cos ω; K is half the peak-to-peak. One AU in one of these years is 29.785 km/s, from the IAU astronomical unit of 149,597,870,700 m. The top-down drawing is the orbit plane. Inclination does not squash it. The light curve, when both radii are set, is two uniform disks. Surface brightness goes as T⁴ only when both temperatures are set. No limb darkening, no Roche surface, and no third light. Not an ephemeris and not a spectroscopic orbit fit.

Three ways to see the same pair

Visual binary
Both stars are seen apart on the sky, and the orbit is measured in angle. A parallax turns the angle into AU. Sirius and Alpha Centauri are this case. The period and that axis weigh the total. The fraction of the axis each star occupies splits it. Bond et al. 2017 give Sirius A an absolute semimajor axis of 2.4761″ against a relative axis of 7.4957″.
Spectroscopic binary
The stars may be a single point of light. The lines shift as each star approaches and recedes. The clock is the period. The size of the shift is K. One K and an inclination give a minimum mass. Both K’s give both masses once the inclination is known. Algol’s published K values are 44.1 and 194.2 km/s. This page recomputes 44.112 and 194.205 from the masses, the period, and i = 81.4°.
Eclipsing binary
The orbit is close enough to edge-on that the stars cover one another. The light curve gives the inclination, and often the radii in units of the axis. Algol is the prototype. Its inner pair dips on this page. Alpha Centauri, at 79°, does not: the stars are about a solar radius across and about 23 AU apart. Sirius is loaded without radii.
Why binaries weigh stars
A single star’s light tells you temperature, and with a distance it tells you luminosity. Mass does not fall out of that. Newton’s law does, once two bodies share an orbit you can time and size. Planets do the same job in miniature: the transit desk is a star pulled by a planet, and K there is the star’s wobble. The mass ratio is the whole trick. The heavier body moves less.
The sin i blind spot
A face-on orbit still has a full orbital speed. None of it is toward us, so the spectrograph reads zero. K scales with sin i. At 90°, sin i is 1. At 0°, it is 0. An inclination above 90° is retrograde. Sirius at 136.336° has the same |sin i| as 43.664°. The speed along the line of sight is not the orbital speed.
Center of mass
a₁ = a M₂ / (M₁ + M₂) and a₂ = a M₁ / (M₁ + M₂). They add to a. The ratio a₁/a₂ equals M₂/M₁. In “axis and period” mode the boxes are only that ratio, and a³/P² is the total the drawing splits. In the other two modes the boxes are the masses, and the law fills in the axis or the period.

Where the numbers come from

  • NASA — Basics of Space Flight, Kepler’s third law — the square of the period is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis. This page writes the two-body form M₁ + M₂ = a³/P². The one-sun case is the Kepler desk.
  • NASA NSSDCA — Earth Fact Sheet — sidereal orbit period 365.256 days. That day count is the year when this page converts a period in days. It is the same unit as the Kepler desk.
  • IAU 2012 Resolution B2 — the astronomical unit is exactly 149,597,870,700 m. One of those units in one 365.256-day year is 29.785 km/s on this page.
  • IAU 2015 Resolution B3 (Prša et al. 2016) — nominal solar radius 6.957 × 10⁸ m. An axis in solar radii uses that length. The same resolution’s nominal GM☉ is not required, because a³/P² is already in solar masses.
  • Bond et al. 2017, ApJ 840, 70 — Sirius. Table 4: P = 50.1284 ± 0.0043 yr, a = 7.4957 ± 0.0025″, e = 0.59142 ± 0.00037, i = 136.336 ± 0.040°, ω = 149.161 ± 0.075°. Table 6: 2.063 ± 0.023 and 1.018 ± 0.011 solar masses, total 3.081 ± 0.034. Table 7: parallax 0.3789 ± 0.0014″, absolute semimajor axis of A 2.4761 ± 0.0045″. Also arXiv:1703.10625.
