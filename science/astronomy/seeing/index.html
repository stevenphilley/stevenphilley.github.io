Seeing and
adaptive optics

A star is a point. The air between it and the telescope is not uniform, so a long exposure is a blur. The usual size of a still-coherent patch of that wavefront is Fried’s parameter, r0. This desk takes r0 at 500 nm and the zenith, then scales it as λ6/5 and as (sec z)−3/5. The long-exposure seeing disk has FWHM 0.98 λ / r0, so the blur shrinks only as λ−1/5. Tokovinin (2023) prints 0.981 for an infinite outer scale. This page uses 0.98 and does not substitute 0.981. An r0 of 10 cm at 500 nm and the zenith is a seeing of 1.011″. The same air at K, 2.2 µm, has r0 = 59.18 cm and a seeing of 0.752″.

Diffraction pulls the other way. Rayleigh’s criterion is 1.22 λ / D, the same conventional factor as the telescope desk. A 20 cm aperture at 550 nm is 0.692″. An 8 m aperture at 550 nm is 0.0173″. The wavefront is called seeing-limited when D > r0, because more than one coherence cell fits across the mirror. The angle this page calls delivered is the larger of the seeing FWHM and the Rayleigh angle. Those two angles meet at D = (1.22/0.98) r0, a little past D = r0. The mean lift of the whole image is the refraction desk. The dimming along the path is airmass. Skyglow, which hides faint stars without blurring them this way, is light pollution.

What the blur is

Twinkling is not the same thing as seeing. Twinkling, or scintillation, is the star’s brightness flickering. A small aperture — the eye, or a 50 mm binocular — is covered by one or two turbulent cells, so the intensity is not averaged and the star flashes. A large mirror averages many cells. The flashes die down, and a long exposure records a blurred disk instead. That width is the seeing. The eye notices the flicker. A camera notices the blur.

Why the infrared is kinder to adaptive optics. r0 grows as λ to the 6/5. The 10 cm cell at 500 nm is 59.18 cm at 2.2 µm, for the same air at the zenith. A deformable mirror then needs fewer actuators across the pupil. The coherence time τ0 = 0.314 r0 / v grows with r0, so the same wind allows a slower correction. The isoplanatic angle grows with r0 too, so the corrected patch of sky is wider. The seeing disk itself only shrinks as λ to the −1/5, from 1.011″ to 0.752″ in that example. Diffraction grows in proportion to λ, so a large mirror’s diffraction limit and the seeing disk move toward each other. That is why a telescope that is hopelessly seeing-limited in the visible can be corrected, and can be closer to diffraction-limited, in the infrared.

Why the loop has to be fast, and the field small. At r0 = 10 cm, a wind of 10 m/s, and 500 nm, τ0 is 3.14 ms. The mirror has to keep up with that. θ0 = 0.314 r0 / h̄ at the zenith. With an effective height of 5 km that angle is 1.295″. A guide star a few arcseconds away is looking through different high-altitude air. Off the zenith the same definition picks up a cos z, because the ray crosses the high layer on a slant, and r0 itself has already shrunk as (sec z)−3/5. The height here is one number you choose. Real turbulence is a stack of layers. High layers set θ0. A ground layer barely does.

Laser guide stars, in outline. A natural star bright enough to measure the wavefront is uncommon inside a patch only a few arcseconds wide. A laser can make a beacon in the sky. A sodium laser excites sodium high in the mesosphere, so the beacon sits far above a Rayleigh beacon scattered from the lower air and is a little closer to a star at infinity. The light goes up and comes down the same path, so the absolute tilt cancels, and tip and tilt still have to come from a real star. The beacon is also a cone rather than a cylinder, and it does not sample every bit of high air a star at infinity would cross. This page names those limits. It does not design a laser, a beacon altitude, or a control loop.

The site buttons are round illustrations. Suburban is 5 cm, which this formula turns into 2.021″ at 500 nm and the zenith. Backyard seeing is often described as a couple of arcseconds, sometimes several. Good mountain is 10 cm, the round “good site” figure in Dhillon’s teaching note, and it is 1.011″. Excellent is 15 cm, which is 0.674″. An ESO VLTI interface document lists a Paranal DIMM 50% quantile of 0.73″ at 500 nm at the zenith. Lyman, Cherubini, and Businger (2020) list a Maunakea DIMM median of 0.65″ for the nightly averages in their Table 1. The 15 cm button sits near that neighbourhood. It is not either statistic, and it is not a measurement from either mountain.

Terms

Fried parameter
r0 is the aperture that would match the seeing-limited resolution of a perfect telescope, in Fried’s definition. Typed here at 500 nm and the zenith. It grows as λ6/5 and shrinks as (sec z)3/5 in the denominator. At D = r0 the residual phase variance is about a radian squared. A high-Strehl image wants the aperture somewhat smaller than r0. “D ≤ r0” on this page means one coherence cell, not a perfect Airy disk.
Seeing FWHM
0.98 λ/r0, in radians, then converted to arcseconds. Tokovinin’s equation (6) is 0.981 λ/r0 for an infinite outer scale. A finite outer scale makes the core a bit sharper. This page does not apply that correction, and it does not substitute 0.981.
Rayleigh
1.22 λ/D, the conventional rounding of (first positive zero of J1) / π. It is the separation at which one star’s peak sits on the other’s first dark ring. It is not the FWHM of the Airy core. The telescope desk uses the same 1.22, and the same 0.692″ for 200 mm at 550 nm.
Delivered angle
The larger of the seeing FWHM and the Rayleigh angle. That is the classroom answer to “what limits this exposure.” The wavefront regime is a separate test: seeing-limited when D > r0. Between r0 and 1.245 r0 the wavefront is already seeing-limited and the Rayleigh angle is still the larger number, because 1.22 and 0.98 are different coefficients.
Coherence time
τ0 = 0.314 r0 / v, with r0 at the wavelength and zenith angle on the sliders and v an effective wind speed. Dhillon’s teaching pair, 10 cm and 10 m/s at 500 nm, is 3.14 ms. It is not a forecast of the wind.
Isoplanatic angle
At the zenith, θ0 = 0.314 r0 / h̄. Off the zenith, θ0 = 0.314 cos(z) r0 / h̄, and r0 already includes (sec z)−3/5. Quirrenbach’s example, r0 = 20 cm and an effective height of 7 km, is 1.850″ with this factor. That chapter rounds it to 1.8″. 1.8 is not substituted. h̄ weights the high turbulence. Dome seeing and a ground layer barely move it.

Sources

  • Fried, D. L., Journal of the Optical Society of America 56, 1372–1379 (1966) — Optical resolution through a randomly inhomogeneous medium. r0 is defined so that a long-exposure image through the atmosphere has the resolution of a diffraction-limited aperture of diameter r0. The wavelength and zenith scalings used here follow from r0−5/3 ∝ k2 sec(z) ∫ Cn2 dh, with k = 2π/λ.
  • Tokovinin, A., Atmosphere 14, 1694 (2023) — The elusive nature of seeing. Equation (6): the FWHM of the atmospheric PSF is ε = 0.981 (λ/r0) for an infinite outer scale. r0 ∝ λ6/5, so ε ∝ λ−1/5, and at zenith distance γ the seeing scales as (sec γ)3/5. This desk’s factor is 0.98. 0.981 is not substituted. A finite outer scale is discussed there and is not applied here.
  • Quirrenbach, A., The effects of atmospheric turbulence on astronomical observations — course chapter. r0 ∝ λ6/5 and r0 ∝ (sec z)−3/5. The isoplanatic angle is θ0 = 0.314 cos(z) r0 / H. The printed example, r0 = 20 cm and H = 7 km, is 1.8″. This page computes 1.850″ and does not substitute 1.8. The chapter’s seeing width ≈ 1.2 λ/r0 is a coarser factor and is not used.
  • Vik Dhillon, PHY217 — adaptive optics — a teaching note. A good site is described as typically r0 = 10 cm at 500 nm. The more accurate coherence time is given as t0 ~ 0.314 r0 / v, and the isoplanatic angle as θ0 ~ 0.314 r0 / h. The 10 cm mountain button is that round figure. “Typically” is the note’s word. It is not a median for a named observatory.
  • ESO-045686, Interface Control Document between VLTI and its instruments — Table 7, seeing measured by the DIMM at 500 nm at zenith. The 50% quantile in that table is 0.73 arcsec. The 15 cm button is not that quantile.
  • Lyman, R., Cherubini, T., and Businger, S., Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society 496, 4734 (2020) — Forecasting seeing for the Maunakea Observatories. Table 1 lists a DIMM median of 0.65 arcsec for the nightly averages. The 15 cm button is not that median.
  • HyperPhysics — Rayleigh criterion — for a circular aperture, θ = 1.22 λ/D. The same statement as the telescope desk. The drawn Airy pattern uses J1. The reported angle uses 1.22.
  • Telescope desk — Rayleigh 0.692″ for a 200 mm aperture at 550 nm is the same arithmetic as the 20 cm button here. Eight inches is 20.32 cm. The button uses 20 cm and does not substitute 20.32.

The mean bend that raises a star. Seeing is the turbulent leftover after that mean bend is taken out. Bennett at the horizon is 34.5′. This page does not compute it.

A bright sky hides faint stars. It does not set r0. Seeing and skyglow are different ways to lose the night.
