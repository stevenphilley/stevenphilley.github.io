Vol. Science · Astronomy · The five points

Lagrange
five places that keep station

Two bodies on circular orbits have five points where a third, tiny mass can sit still in the frame that rotates with them. L1, L2, and L3 lie on the line through the primary and the secondary. L4 and L5 complete equilateral triangles, 60° ahead of the secondary and 60° behind. The mass parameter is μ = m₂ / (m₁ + m₂). A Hill-sphere approximation puts L1 and L2 a distance r ≈ a · (μ / 3)1/3 from the secondary. That is an approximation. This desk also solves the collinear force-balance equation and prints that distance beside it, in kilometers and in units of the separation a. Masses and semimajor axes are the NASA NSSDCA fact sheets. With those inputs, Sun–Earth L1 is 1,491,557 km from Earth and L2 is 1,501,538 km. Earth–Moon L1 is 326,380 km from Earth and 58,020 km from the Moon. The year of one solar circle is the Kepler desk. The speed at one radius is the Escape desk. The half-ellipse between two circles is the Hohmann desk.

Circular restricted three-body classroom schematic. Two bodies on circles about their barycenter, and a test mass with no pull of its own. One plane. Top-down, from the north, so the pair’s motion runs counterclockwise. Body disks are marks, not radii, so the picture is not to scale where a world is drawn. L1 through L5 are drawn at the computed positions. For Sun–Earth, L1 and L2 sit almost on Earth in the full view; the lower panel magnifies that neighborhood, and that panel has its own scale. Not mission-planning data. Not an ephemeris. Not a halo orbit.

Classroom idealization · not mission-planning data

Two circles and a test mass

Nothing here is a halo orbit, a station-keeping budget, or a launch window. The model is the circular restricted three-body problem: the primary and the secondary on circles, the third body massless, everything in one plane. Real orbits are eccentric. The Sun pulls on the Earth–Moon pair, and Jupiter does not orbit the Sun alone. A spacecraft at L1 or L2 usually orbits the point, on a halo or a Lissajous path, and still fires thrusters. NASA’s Webb orbit page says Webb orbits around L2 and does not sit stationary on the point. The Kepler desk is the year of one circle. The Escape desk is the local speed. The Hohmann desk is the coast between two circles.

The formulas

μ = m₂ / (m₁ + m₂). m₁ is the primary, m₂ the secondary. Sun–Earth uses the Sun Fact Sheet mass 1,988,400 × 1024 kg and the Earth Fact Sheet mass 5.9722 × 1024 kg. Earth–Moon uses that same Earth mass and the Moon Fact Sheet mass 0.07346 × 1024 kg. Sun–Jupiter uses the Sun’s mass and the Jupiter Fact Sheet mass 1,898.13 × 1024 kg. The separation a is the secondary’s fact-sheet semimajor axis: Earth 149.598 × 106 km, Moon 0.3844 × 106 km, Jupiter 778.479 × 106 km.

r ≈ a · (μ / 3)1/3. This Hill-sphere approximation is one distance from the secondary, used here for both L1 and L2. It is labeled as an approximation on every readout. It is closer when μ is tiny. For Sun–Earth it and the numerical L1 differ by about 5,000 km. For Earth–Moon the Hill figure is 61,274 km from the Moon and the numerical L1 is 58,020 km.

The collinear balance. In units where a = 1 and the mean motion is 1, the primary sits at x = −μ and the secondary at x = 1 − μ. A point on that line is in equilibrium when x − (1−μ)(x − x₁)/|x − x₁|³ − μ(x − x₂)/|x − x₂|³ = 0. That condition is a quintic in the distance from the secondary. This page solves it by bisection: L1 between the bodies, L2 beyond the secondary, L3 beyond the primary. L4 and L5 are the equilateral points, each one separation from both bodies, so the angle at either body is 60°.

Routh. In this model L4 and L5 are linearly stable when μ(1−μ) < 1/27. That is a mass ratio m₁/m₂ greater than 24.96, the threshold this page computes from that inequality. L1, L2, and L3 are unstable. A spacecraft there needs station-keeping. NASA’s Webb orbit page calls L1, L2, and L3 metastable: an object drifts away unless it maintains its position, for example with small periodic rocket thrust. The same page says objects at L4 and L5 orbit those points with no assistance. The mass-ratio test is the classroom criterion. The three pairs on this desk all pass it.

Where this shows up

Sun–Earth L2. NASA’s Webb mission page says the James Webb Space Telescope orbits the Sun 1.5 million kilometers from Earth at L2. The Webb orbit page says the same distance, and says Webb orbits around L2 rather than sitting on the point. This desk’s arithmetic, from the fact-sheet masses and 149.598 × 106 km, puts numerical L2 at 1,501,538 km from Earth.

Sun–Earth L1. The same Webb orbit page says L1 is 1.5 million km from Earth toward the Sun, and names SOHO among the solar observatories there. NASA’s SOHO mission page says SOHO was placed 1.5 million kilometers from Earth in an elliptical Lissajous orbit around the L1 libration point. This desk’s numerical L1 is 1,491,557 km from Earth. The same Webb page says some small asteroids are known at Sun–Earth L4 and L5.

Sun–Jupiter L4 and L5. NASA’s account of the Trojan asteroids says Max Wolf’s 1906 asteroid stayed nearly 60° in front of Jupiter, at Jupiter’s L4, and that another swarm was found at L5. Those asteroids lead or follow Jupiter in its orbit around the Sun. On this desk L4 and L5 are the equilateral points of the Sun–Jupiter pair.

Literacy · a point, not a mission

L1
Between the primary and the secondary, closer to the secondary. Unstable. Sun–Earth L1, from these inputs, is 1,491,557 km from Earth, sunward. NASA’s round figure for that neighborhood is 1.5 million km, which is where SOHO loops.
L2
On the line of centers, beyond the secondary. Unstable. Sun–Earth L2 is 1,501,538 km from Earth on this page. Webb orbits around that point, about 1.5 million km from Earth, and does not sit on it.
L3
Beyond the primary, opposite the secondary. Unstable. For a small μ it sits about one separation from the primary. Nothing on this page claims an object there.
L4 and L5
The equilateral points. L4 is 60° ahead of the secondary, in the direction of motion. L5 is 60° behind. Each is a distance a from the primary and a distance a from the secondary. They are linearly stable in this model when the Routh test passes. Jupiter’s Trojan asteroids occupy the Sun–Jupiter pair’s L4 and L5.
Hill approximation
r ≈ a · (μ / 3)1/3, measured from the secondary. One number for both L1 and L2. The dashed circle in the lower panel is that radius. The dots are the numerical collinear points. When μ is not tiny, the circle and the dots separate. Earth–Moon is the pair where you can see it.
What the circles leave out
Eccentricity, inclination, and every other body. The Earth–Moon mass ratio passes the Routh test, so L4 and L5 are stable in this two-body model. The Sun is not in that model. A real L1 or L2 mission flies a halo or a Lissajous around the point and budgets station-keeping. This page marks the equilibrium. The lower panel is magnified. Body disks are not radii. Not a trajectory to fly.
