Vol. Science · Astronomy · What the body is made of

Density

Mean density is mass divided by volume. For a sphere, ρ = M / (4/3 π R³). R on a planet sheet is the volumetric mean radius, the radius of a sphere with the same volume. Io, Europa, Ganymede, Callisto, and Titan use the one radius their satellite sheets print. The arithmetic sits beside the printed mean density. They are not averaged. The side readout is g = G M / R², the 2022 CODATA G times the printed mass, over that same R². The sheet’s gravity, where one is printed, stays beside it. The sheet’s GM is not substituted. A high density hints at iron and rock. A low density hints at ice, or at a hydrogen-helium envelope. Self-compression makes a large body denser than the same materials would be with the squeeze taken off. The fact sheets do not print an uncompressed density. This page does not invent one. Saturn’s sheet is 687 kg/m³, below water. The temperatures desk is a sunlight balance. The scale height uses the sheet’s g. Escape uses GM and a radius. Those are different numbers.

What the number is

One number for the whole body. The fact-sheet notes define mean density as mass divided by volume, and the volumetric mean radius as the radius of a sphere with that volume. This page puts the printed mass and that radius into ρ = M / (4/3 π R³). The printed density stays beside the result. Where the tenth does not round to the printed integer, both numbers stay. The comparison table, updated 13 November 2026, prints coarser masses, and for density it prints Earth 5,514, the Moon 3,340, and Pluto 1,850. The individual sheets print 5513, 3344, and 1854. This page uses the individual sheets. They are not averaged.

Density is a hint, not a layer cake. A mean near the rock mark, or above it and still under the iron mark, is the usual hint of rock mixed with metal. NASA Science says Mercury is the second densest planet, after Earth, and that it has a large metallic core with a radius of about 2,074 kilometers, about 85% of the planet’s radius. That radius is NASA Science’s. This page’s Mercury radius is the fact sheet’s 2,439.7 km. They are not averaged, and the core radius is not R. The Earth sheet prints a core radius of 3485 km and does not print a core mass or a core density. The Mars sheet prints a core radius of 1830 km with a double asterisk, and a note that the core may only be 1650–1675 km. Neither Mars figure goes into the formula. The Galilean sheets print Io 3530, Europa 3010, Ganymede 1940, and Callisto 1830 kg/m³. Titan’s sheet prints 1880. A lower average can mean a larger share of lighter material. This page does not split that average into a rock fraction and an ice fraction, because the sheets do not print those fractions. NASA Science says Jupiter’s composition is similar to the Sun’s, mostly hydrogen and helium, and that Saturn is made mostly of hydrogen and helium.

Big bodies squeeze. A body’s own weight compresses the interior, so the mean density is higher than the density those materials would have with the squeeze taken off. NASA Science says pressure inside Jupiter compresses hydrogen gas into a liquid. Jupiter’s sheet is 1,326 kg/m³. Saturn’s sheet is 687 kg/m³. Both are mean densities, squeeze included. They are not averaged. An uncompressed density would be the materials without that squeeze. The fact sheets do not print one. This page does not invent one. The water line is 1 g/cm³, which is 1,000 kg/m³. NASA’s StarChild densities page says water is 1 gram per cubic centimeter, and that an object less dense than water floats. NASA Science says Saturn is the only planet in our solar system with an average density that is less than water. 687 is less than 1,000. The rock mark at 3 g/cm³ and the iron mark at 7.9 g/cm³ are reference marks on this chart. They are not fact-sheet densities.

What the words mean

Mean density
ρ = M / (4/3 π R³), in kg/m³. Divide by 1,000 for g/cm³. The gram figure on this page is the printed tenth of a kg/m³, divided by 1,000, so 5513.4 kg/m³ is 5.5134 g/cm³ and not a second rounding.
Volumetric mean radius
The notes: the radius of a sphere with the same volume as the body. Mean density is mass divided by that volume. An equatorial radius is a different line. Jupiter’s equatorial radius at 1 bar is 71,492 km. The volumetric mean radius is 69,911 km. The formula uses 69,911. A polar radius is printed on the planet sheets and is not R either.
Surface gravity
g = G M / R². G is the 2022 CODATA value 6.67430 × 10−11 m³ kg−1 s−2. The notes define the sheet’s mean gravity as GM over the square of the volumetric mean radius, using the sheet’s GM. This page multiplies the printed mass by G. The sheet’s GM stays a different line. Where the sheet prints no gravity, the cell is a dash.
Water, rock, iron
The water line is 1 g/cm³. StarChild calls that 1 gram per cubic centimeter. The rock mark is 3 g/cm³. The iron mark is 7.9 g/cm³. Those two marks are reference lines on the drawing. They are not fact-sheet densities and not measurements of these bodies. Every preset on this page sits below 7.9.
1-bar level
Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune have no solid surface on their sheets. The radius in the formula is still the volumetric mean radius. The gravity the sheet prints is labeled at 1 bar. The scale-height desk uses that 1-bar gravity as g. This page’s g is computed from mass and radius.
What this picture leaves out
A core mass, an ice fraction, and an uncompressed density. The Earth sheet’s core radius is 3485 km, with no core mass beside it. The Mars sheet’s 1830 km carries a double asterisk, and the note says the core may only be 1650–1675 km. Self-compression is named and not given a number. The escape desk uses the printed GM. The temperatures desk does not use mass or radius. Not an interior model.

Where the numbers come from

  • NASA NSSDCA — Notes on the fact sheet parameters — updated 6 May 2024. Mass: mass of the body in 10^24 kilograms. Volumetric mean radius: radius of a sphere with the same volume as the body. Mean density: average density of the body, mass/volume. Surface gravity (mean): GM over the square of the volumetric mean radius.
  • NASA NSSDCA — Planetary Fact Sheet notes — updated 20 December 2021. Density is the average density, mass divided by volume. For the gas giants the gravity is given at the 1 bar pressure level.
  • NASA NSSDCA — Planetary Fact Sheet — comparison table, updated 13 November 2026. Density in kg/m³: Mercury 5429, Venus 5243, Earth 5514, Moon 3340, Mars 3934, Jupiter 1326, Saturn 687, Uranus 1270, Neptune 1638, Pluto 1850. Masses there are coarser than the individual sheets. This page uses each body’s own sheet. Earth 5,514, Moon 3,340, and Pluto 1,850 are not substituted.
  • Mercury Fact Sheet — updated 11 January 2024. Mass 0.33010 × 10^24 kg. Volumetric mean radius 2,439.7 km. Equatorial radius 2,440.5 km. Mean density 5429 kg/m³. Surface gravity (mean) 3.70 m/s².
  • Venus Fact Sheet — updated 11 January 2024. Mass 4.8673 × 10^24 kg. Volumetric mean radius 6,051.8 km, the same figure as the equatorial radius. Mean density 5243 kg/m³. Surface gravity (mean) 8.87 m/s².
  • Earth Fact Sheet — updated 15 November 2024. Mass 5.9722 × 10^24 kg. Volumetric mean radius 6,371.000 km. Equatorial radius 6,378.137 km. Mean density 5513 kg/m³. Surface gravity (mean) 9.820 m/s². Core radius 3485 km. No core mass and no core density.
  • Moon Fact Sheet — updated 11 January 2024. Mass 0.07346 × 10^24 kg. Volumetric mean radius 1,737.4 km. Equatorial radius 1,738.1 km. Mean density 3344 kg/m³. Surface gravity 1.62 m/s². The Earth column on that sheet prints mass 5.9724 × 10^24 kg and density 5514 kg/m³. This page does not use that column for Earth.
  • Mars Fact Sheet — updated 19 May 2025. Mass 0.64169 × 10^24 kg. Volumetric mean radius 3,389.5 km. Equatorial radius 3,396.2 km. Mean density 3934 kg/m³. Surface gravity (mean) 3.73 m/s². Core radius 1830 km, marked with a double asterisk. The note says the radius of the core of Mars may only be 1650–1675 km.
  • Jupiter Fact Sheet — updated 2 October 2024. Mass 1,898.13 × 10^24 kg. Volumetric mean radius 69,911 km. Equatorial radius at 1 bar 71,492 km. Mean density 1,326 kg/m³. Gravity (mean, 1 bar) 25.92 m/s².
  • Saturn Fact Sheet — updated 18 March 2025. Mass 568.32 × 10^24 kg. Volumetric mean radius 58,232 km. Equatorial radius at 1 bar 60,268 km. Mean density 687 kg/m³. Gravity (mean, 1 bar) 11.19 m/s².
  • Uranus Fact Sheet — updated 2 October 2024. Mass 86.811 × 10^24 kg. Volumetric mean radius 25,362 km. Equatorial radius at 1 bar 25,559 km. Mean density 1,270 kg/m³. Gravity (mean, 1 bar) 9.01 m/s².
  • Neptune Fact Sheet — updated 3 October 2024. Mass 102.409 × 10^24 kg. Volumetric mean radius 24,622 km. Equatorial radius at 1 bar 24,764 km. Mean density 1,638 kg/m³. Gravity (mean, 1 bar) 11.27 m/s².
  • Pluto Fact Sheet — updated 11 January 2024. Mass 0.01303 × 10^24 kg. Volumetric mean radius 1,188 km, the same figure as the equatorial radius. Mean density 1854 kg/m³. Surface gravity (mean) 0.62 m/s².
  • Jovian Satellite Fact Sheet — updated 6 December 2023. Bulk parameters: Io mass 893.2 × 10^20 kg, radius 1821.5 km, mean density 3530 kg/m³. Europa 480.0 × 10^20 kg, 1560.8 km, 3010 kg/m³. Ganymede 1,481.9 × 10^20 kg, 2631.2 km, 1940 kg/m³. Callisto 1,075.9 × 10^20 kg, 2410.3 km, 1830 kg/m³. The table does not print a surface gravity.
  • Saturnian Satellite Fact Sheet — updated 22 July 2025. Titan mass 1,345.5 × 10^20 kg, radius 2,575 km, mean density 1880 kg/m³. No surface gravity. The scale-height desk uses this same mass and radius for GM/r².
  • NIST CODATA — Newtonian constant of gravitation — G = 6.67430 × 10−11 m³ kg−1 s−2, 2022 CODATA. Used for every g = G M / R² on this page, including the presets. The sheet’s GM is not substituted.
  • NASA Science — Mercury facts — Mercury is the second densest planet, after Earth. It has a large metallic core with a radius of about 1,289 miles (2,074 kilometers), about 85% of the planet’s radius. That radius is not the R in this formula.
  • NASA Science — Jupiter facts — The composition of Jupiter is similar to that of the Sun, mostly hydrogen and helium. Deep in the atmosphere, pressure and temperature increase, compressing the hydrogen gas into a liquid.
  • NASA Science — Saturn facts — Saturn is made mostly of hydrogen and helium. Saturn is the only planet in our solar system with an average density that is less than water.
  • NASA GSFC StarChild — Densities — Water is 1 gram per cubic centimeter. If the density of the object is less than that of the water, the object floats. The page’s planet densities are round teaching figures and are not the numbers in this table.
