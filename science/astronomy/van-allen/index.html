Vol. Science · Astronomy · The belts

Trapped in
the field

Earth’s magnetic field holds charged particles in two torus-shaped regions, the Van Allen belts. NASA’s Webb launch note says the inner belt ranges from about 400 to 6,000 miles (about 650 to 9,660 kilometers) above the surface, and the outer belt stretches from 8,400 to 36,000 miles (about 13,500 to 60,000 kilometers). Basics of Space Flight says Explorer 1, early in 1958, found bands of rapidly moving charged particles trapped in toroidal, doughnut-shaped, regions surrounding the equator. The drawings walk those sentences. They are a schematic. They are not a radiation forecast, not a dose calculator, and not a live belt model. The shield and the oval are the aurora desk. The sunspot rhythm is the solar cycle. The wind and the ejections that disturb them are the solar wind and CME desk. Orbit altitude, on a different cited number, is the satellites desk. The atmosphere desk is the thermosphere. Which gases a magnetosphere helps a world keep is atmospheric escape.

South Atlantic Anomaly

Closer, with no distance drawn

A classroom note · not a hazard map

NASA’s ICON article says an unusually weak spot in the field over South America and the southern Atlantic Ocean, called the South Atlantic Anomaly, allows particles to dip closer to the surface than normal. The same article says the forces in the core and the tilt of the magnetic axis together produce the anomaly, allowing charged particles trapped in Earth’s magnetic field to dip closer to the surface. Basics of Space Flight says the inner belt has a portion called the South Atlantic Anomaly which extends down into low-Earth-orbital altitudes. No dip distance is printed in those sentences, and none is drawn here. The button above switches the card. It does not move the lobes. Currently, the ICON article says, the anomaly creates no visible impacts on daily life on the surface. The station still passes through it. That pass is not drawn.

What the words mean

Inner belt
NASA’s Webb launch note: the inner belt ranges from about 400 to 6,000 miles (about 650 to 9,660 kilometers) above Earth’s surface. Basics of Space Flight: the inner region consists largely of protons with an energy greater than 30 million eV and is centered about 3,000 km up. NASA Science: the inner belt results from interactions of cosmic rays with Earth’s atmosphere. The Van Allen Probes page says about 600 miles from Earth’s surface is the first of two donut-shaped electron swarms. That 600 miles is not the Webb note’s about 400 miles. This page does not average them. The slider walks only the Webb kilometer span, and the 3,000 km sentence is a preset inside it.
Outer belt
The Webb note: the outer belt stretches from 8,400 to 36,000 miles (about 13,500 to 60,000 kilometers) above Earth’s surface. Basics of Space Flight: centered about 15,000 to 20,000 km up, with electrons in the hundreds of millions of eV, and a high flux of protons of lower energy than the inner belt. NASA Science: the outer belt is made up of billions of high-energy particles that originate from the Sun. The Probes page calls the outer belt, in the traditional picture, the larger and more dynamic of the two, dominated by electrons. The slider walks the Webb kilometer span. The 15,000 and 20,000 km sentences are presets. They are not averaged with 13,500 or 60,000.
Slot
The Webb caption: the slot region is the gap between Earth’s two radiation belts. It is thought to have less radiation than other parts of near-Earth space, but it is highly variable. The drawing leaves the unmarked stretch between about 9,660 km and about 13,500 km, the far end of one quoted range and the near end of the other. That stretch is not a measured width. The Probes page says the traditional picture has an empty slot, and that the inner belt, the slot, and the outer belt can appear different depending on the energy of electrons and on conditions in the magnetosphere. During geomagnetic storms the empty region can fill in completely with lower-energy electrons. The same page says scientists traditionally thought this happened only in the most extreme storms, about once every 10 years, and that new data shows it is not uncommon for lower-energy electrons, up to 0.8 MeV, to fill this space during almost all geomagnetic storms. This page quotes both sentences. It does not fill the slot, and 0.8 MeV is not a setting.
L-shell
NASA’s Scientific Visualization Studio, on a 2015 electron event: the L-shell value identifies a field line in a magnetic dipole. The numerical value corresponds to the furthest distance from Earth in Earth radii, in that note’s case two Earth radii, for a temporary belt inside the L=2 shell. This page does not draw that 2015 belt. The number next to a marker is the fact sheet’s 6,378 km plus the altitude, divided by 6,378 km, the distance from Earth’s center. The note says “from Earth.” This page does not treat that phrase as a surface altitude, and it does not publish an L catalog. The curves are a dipole sketch so the line can be drawn. NOAA NCEI’s WMM2020 inclination, 9.41°, tilts them. The direction of the tilt in this cut is a sketch, the same classroom choice as the aurora desk. The fact sheet’s dipole offset of 0.076 Re is not drawn.
South Atlantic Anomaly
ICON: over South America and the southern Atlantic Ocean, an unusually weak spot lets particles dip closer to the surface than normal. The forces in the core and the tilt of the magnetic axis together produce the anomaly. Basics of Space Flight: the inner belt has a portion, the South Atlantic Anomaly, which extends down into low-Earth-orbital altitudes. No distance for that dip is printed in those sentences. None is drawn. ICON says the anomaly creates no visible impacts on daily life on the surface, and that the station passes through it, is well protected, and that astronauts are safe from harm while inside. Those are the article’s sentences. This page is not a dose calculation and not a map of the passes. A caption on the Probes page says that at the highest electron energies measured, above 1 megaelectron volt, researchers saw electrons in the outer belt only. That energy cut is not a control on this page.
What this drawing refuses
NASA Science says the belts are two permanent donuts and also reports a transient third radiation belt, which can form during times of intense solar activity. The third belt is not drawn. The Probes page says the belts can shrink and swell, and that a geomagnetic storm can raise or lower the number of energetic electrons for a time. This page does not draw that change. It is not a radiation forecast, not a dose calculator, not a mission design tool, and not a live belt model. People on the ground are the subject of the aurora desk’s NASA sentence: the magnetic field and the atmosphere protect them. The solar-cycle desk is the rhythm of the storms that page says are more common near solar maximum. The atmospheric-escape desk is which gases a world can keep. This page does not add a rate or a loss.

Where the sentences come from

  • NASA — Ahead of Webb Launch, NASA is Watching the Weather in Space — 22 December 2021. The inner belt ranges from about 400 to 6,000 miles (about 650 to 9,660 kilometers) above Earth’s surface. The outer belt stretches from 8,400 to 36,000 miles (about 13,500 to 60,000 kilometers). The slot region is the gap between the two belts. It is thought to have less radiation than other parts of near-Earth space, but it is highly variable. The belts are doughnut-shaped rings of high-energy particles, trapped by the magnetic field, named for James Van Allen. The sliders walk only those two kilometer spans.
  • NASA Science — Basics of Space Flight, Chapter 1 — Explorer 1, early in 1958, a Geiger counter devised by James Van Allen and his University of Iowa team, found bands of rapidly moving charged particles trapped in toroidal regions surrounding the equator. The inner region, largely protons above 30 million eV, is centered about 3,000 km up. The outer belt is centered about 15,000 to 20,000 km up, with electrons in the hundreds of millions of eV. The South Atlantic Anomaly is a portion of the inner belt that extends down into low-Earth-orbital altitudes.
  • NASA Science — What are the Van Allen Belts and why do they matter? — two permanent belts, enormous donuts. The outer belt’s particles originate from the Sun. The inner belt results from cosmic rays and the atmosphere. Located beyond low-Earth orbit. Discovered in 1958. The station’s orbit, once the station was completed in 2011, is approximately 250 miles. A transient third belt can form during intense solar activity. That third belt is not drawn.
  • NASA — Van Allen Probes Revolutionize View of Radiation Belts — about 600 miles up is the first of two donut-shaped electron swarms, a different near-edge sentence from the Webb note’s about 400 miles. The traditional picture is a smaller, more stable inner belt, an empty slot, and a larger, more dynamic outer belt. All three regions can look different with electron energy. During geomagnetic storms the slot can fill with lower-energy electrons. The old expectation of only the most extreme storms, about once every 10 years, is quoted next to the page’s own correction: up to 0.8 MeV, during almost all geomagnetic storms, is not uncommon. A caption says that above 1 megaelectron volt the researchers saw electrons in the outer belt only. This drawing does not switch energy and does not fill the slot.
  • NASA — Researchers Track Slowly Splitting ‘Dent’ in Earth’s Magnetic Field — the South Atlantic Anomaly, over South America and the southern Atlantic, lets trapped particles dip closer to the surface than normal. The innermost belt begins about 400 miles up. The station passes through the anomaly. The article says the station is well protected and that astronauts are safe from harm while inside, and that the anomaly creates no visible impacts on daily life on the surface. No dip distance from that article is drawn.
  • NASA Scientific Visualization Studio — Leaky Radiation Belts — an L-shell identifies a dipole field line. The number is the furthest distance from Earth in Earth radii. The note’s example is two Earth radii, a temporary belt inside L=2 during a 2015 event. This page uses the definition and does not draw that event.
  • NASA Science — Auroras — the International Space Station orbits roughly 250 miles (400 km). That is the station mark. The same sentence is already on the aurora desk.
  • NASA NSSDCA — Earth Fact Sheet — the radius used for the equatorial mark is 6,378 km. Dipole offset 0.076 Re is printed on the sheet and is not drawn. The mark is that radius plus the altitude, divided by the radius. It is not an L catalog.
  • NOAA NCEI — Geomagnetism FAQ — WMM2020 at 2020.0, the dipole axis is inclined at 9.41°. The drawing uses that angle. The direction in this cut is a sketch.
  • Aurora desk — the magnetosphere, the oval, and the same 9.41° tilt. A schematic, not a Kp forecast. This page is the trapped particles on that field.
  • Solar-cycle desk — the sunspot rhythm. Storms are more common near solar maximum on NASA’s solar-storms page. This page does not add a rate.
  • Solar wind and CMEs — the wind and the ejections that disturb this field. A straight-line time across the fact-sheet semimajor axis. Not a forecast, and not a dose.
  • Satellites desk — the 2023 lithograph’s average altitude of about 420 km, and the circle computed from it. A different sentence from the 400 km mark used here.
  • Atmosphere desk — the thermosphere card. This page does not copy that desk’s layer bounds.
  • Atmospheric escape — which gases a world can keep. The aurora desk already cites NASA’s sentence on the magnetosphere and atmospheric erosion. This page does not compute a loss.
