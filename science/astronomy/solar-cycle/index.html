How the Sun’s
spottedness rises

Sunspots come and go together. The count used here is the international sunspot number, version 2.0, from SILSO at the Royal Observatory of Belgium. The snapshot was fetched on 5 November 2026. Monthly means run from January 1749 through October 2026. The heavier line is the 13-month smoothed series SILSO uses to time minima and maxima. Numbering starts with Cycle 1 at the February 1755 minimum. Cycle 19, the highest smoothed peak in the file, is 285.0 in March 1958. Cycle 25’s minimum is December 2019, smoothed number 1.8. In this snapshot the highest smoothed number since then is 160.9 in October 2024. That is the high-water mark of the file, not a forecast. A sunspot is a cooler, magnetically active patch. The activity cycle is about 11 years. The magnetic poles reverse from one cycle to the next, so the magnetic Hale cycle is about 22 years. The Maunder Minimum, 1645 to 1715, is before this series starts.

They are not recomputed and not extended. A value of −1 in those files is omitted. Flag 1 is definitive. Flag 0 is provisional, and SILSO may still revise it. Spörer’s law · a sketch

Toward the equator

The bands move. The dots do not.

New-cycle spots favor higher latitudes. Later in the cycle they sit closer to the equator. NASA’s Earth Observatory describes the bands forming at mid-latitudes and moving toward the equator, and says that by the time the spots reach the equator a new cycle is beginning again at mid-latitudes. NASA Marshall lists that equatorward drift, seen in the butterfly diagram, among the facts a dynamo model has to meet. A review in Space Science Reviews calls the equatorward progression Spörer’s law. This drawing is only that sentence, turned into two moving chords. It is not the Royal Greenwich record, and it is not a latitude for Cycle 25.

What the words mean

Sunspot
A dark patch on the photosphere where a strong magnetic field holds back the heat. NASA Science calls sunspots cooler regions caused by a concentration of magnetic field lines, often around 6,000 °F while the rest of the photosphere is around 10,000 °F. NOAA SWPC says the heart of the magnetic field is cooler, about 7,000 °F, than the surrounding photosphere at about 10,000 °F. Both are saying the spot is cooler. This page does not pick one temperature.
Monthly mean
SILSO’s monthly mean total sunspot number for that calendar month. The thin line is that column, copied from SN_m_tot_V2.0.csv. NOAA SWPC says the official daily and monthly sunspot numbers are determined by WDC-SILSO, and describes the usual construction: a group counts as 10 and each umbra as 1, so an empty disk is 0 and the next possible number is 11. The series still contains values between 0 and 11 in the early years. Those values are in the file. This page does not edit them.
Smoothed number
The 13-month tapered boxcar SILSO publishes in SN_ms_tot_V2.0.csv. Weight 1/2 on the months six before and six after, weight 1 on the months between, divided by 12. SILSO says this series is the conventional clock for cycle minima and maxima. The first six months and the last six months are blank. A dashed stretch on the chart is a month SILSO still marks provisional.
Minimum and maximum
The minimum month is the month in SILSO’s TableCyclesMiMa.csv. The peak is the highest smoothed number from that month up to, and not including, the next cycle’s minimum. The length is the time from one minimum month to the next. Cycle 25 has no following minimum in the CSV, so it has no length. The highest monthly mean is shown beside the smoothed peak and is not the cycle maximum.
About 11 years, and Hale’s 22
NASA Science says the Sun’s activity rises and falls roughly every 11 years, and that the magnetic poles flip roughly every 11 years at the height of the cycle. NOAA SWPC says a new cycle is considered to have begun when groups emerge at higher latitudes with the magnetic polarity of the leading spots opposite that of the previous cycle. NASA Marshall names Hale’s polarity law and the 22-year magnetic cycle: two sunspot cycles bring the leading polarity back. This page does not measure 22 years off the chart.
Maunder Minimum
NASA’s Mysteries of the Sun says well-documented records show very few sunspots from 1645 to 1715, called the Maunder Minimum. The monthly file on this page starts in January 1749, so that interval is not on the chart. The lowest smoothed peaks among Cycles 1 through 24 in this snapshot are Cycle 6, 81.2 in May 1816, and Cycle 5, 82.0 in February 1805. Those are numbers in the file. They are not the Maunder Minimum.
Flares and aurora
Sunspot groups sit in active regions. NOAA SWPC says rapid changes in the magnetic field of those regions are the most likely sources of solar flares, coronal mass ejections, radiation storms, and radio bursts. NASA Science says flares and coronal mass ejections typically burst from active regions, and that such storms are more common near solar maximum than near solar minimum. When the eruption is aimed at Earth, the same NASA page says the particles can drive a geomagnetic storm and the aurora. Earth’s magnetic field and atmosphere protect people on the ground. This page does not predict a flare, and it does not give a rate. How the shield and the oval work is the aurora desk. That page is a schematic. It is not a Kp map. Particles already trapped on that field are the Van Allen belts. That page is a schematic too. It is not a dose model. The wind and the ejections that disturb them are the solar wind and CME desk. That page divides a fact-sheet distance by a cited speed. It is not a forecast.

Where the numbers come from

  • SILSO — SN_m_tot_V2.0.csv — monthly mean total sunspot number, version 2.0. January 1749 through October 2026 in the copy fetched 5 November 2026. Column 4 is the monthly mean. Column 7 is 1 when definitive and 0 when provisional. October 2026 is 45.7 and provisional. The copy is sunspots.json. The numbers are unchanged.
  • SILSO — SN_ms_tot_V2.0.csv — 13-month smoothed monthly total sunspot number. A stored −1 is omitted here. The last smoothed month in the copy is April 2026, 88.6, provisional. December 2025, 107.0, is the last smoothed month marked definitive.
  • SILSO — smoothed-series note — the tapered boxcar, weights 1 except 0.5 at ±6 months, divided by 12. No smoothed value for the first six and last six months. Flag 1 is definitive and flag 0 is provisional in the CSV. SILSO states the data are under CC BY-NC 4.0: Creative Commons Attribution-NonCommercial 4.0. This page attributes WDC-SILSO, Royal Observatory of Belgium, and does not add a further restriction. SILSO does not endorse the page.
  • SILSO — monthly-mean note — the monthly file this page copies. Same column order. Same definitive flag.
  • SILSO — TableCyclesMiMa.csv — minimum and maximum months, fetched 5 November 2026. Cycle 25’s row is December 2019, smoothed 1.8, and a blank maximum. Durations in the companion text table match the lengths this page computes, from one minimum month to the next. Three smoothed cells in the current series differ from the table by 0.1. The months still match. The cards use the series.
  • SILSO — cycles min/max page — fetched the same day. The HTML table prints a Cycle 25 maximum of October 2024, 160.9. The CSV download does not. The bundled series also reads 160.9 in October 2024, and that month is definitive. This page reports that high-water mark. It does not treat the blank CSV cell as filled, and it does not forecast the rest of the cycle.
  • NOAA SWPC — Sunspots / Solar Cycle — sunspots as cooler magnetic regions, about 7,000 °F against a photosphere of about 10,000 °F. Numbering begins with Cycle 1 in 1755. Cycle 24 began in December 2008, peaked in April 2014, and ended in December 2019, which also marked the beginning of Cycle 25. A new cycle’s leading spots have the opposite polarity and emerge at higher latitudes. WDC-SILSO determines the official daily and monthly numbers. The page points to Clette, Svalgaard, Vaquero, and Cliver 2014, Space Science Reviews 186, 35–103, doi:10.1007/s11214-014-0074-2, for the longer history of the sunspot number. This desk does not restate that paper’s arguments.
  • NASA Science — Sunspots — cooler regions caused by magnetic field lines, often around 6,000 °F against about 10,000 °F. Activity rises and falls roughly every 11 years. The poles flip roughly every 11 years at the height of the cycle. Solar Cycle 25 began in 2019. Flares and coronal mass ejections typically burst from the active regions that carry sunspots.
  • NASA Science — Solar storms and flares — flares and related storms are more common near solar maximum than near solar minimum. An Earth-directed storm can drive a geomagnetic storm and the aurora. The magnetic field and the atmosphere protect people on the ground.
  • Van Allen belts — the trapped-particle regions inside the magnetosphere. A schematic of the cited altitude spans. Not a dose calculator, and not this sunspot chart.
  • Solar wind and CMEs — the wind and the ejections that disturb the shield and the belts. A straight-line time across the fact-sheet semimajor axis. Not a forecast, and not this sunspot chart.
  • NASA — Mysteries of the Sun — the usual sunspot belt between about 35° south and 35° north. Spots form at higher latitudes at the beginning and closer to the equator as activity rises. The butterfly diagram is named there for Edward Maunder. The Maunder Minimum is given as 1645 to 1715, a prolonged stretch of very few sunspots. That stretch is not in the 1749 file.
  • NASA Earth Observatory — Sunspots and the Solar Max — bands of spots form at mid-latitudes and move toward the equator. When they reach the equator, the cycle is at a minimum and new spots begin again at mid-latitudes.
  • NASA Marshall Solar Physics — the solar dynamo — lists the 11-year sunspot cycle, the equatorward drift of the active latitude in the butterfly diagram, and Hale’s polarity law and the 22-year magnetic cycle among the observations a dynamo model has to explain.
  • Norton, Charbonneau, and Passos, Space Science Reviews, 2023 — calls the equatorward progression of active-region emergence Spörer’s law, citing Maunder 1903. The same section says early-cycle regions begin at mid-latitudes, approximately 30°. This page cites that name and that sentence. The disk does not plot the 30° figure. Its start is the 35° belt edge from the NASA booklet, and its end is a classroom 8°.
  • Fusion desk — how the Sun’s core turns hydrogen into helium. This page is about the magnetic cycle at the surface, not the power source.
  • Blackbody desk — the color of a hot surface. A sunspot is darker because it is cooler. This page does not compute that spectrum.
