Dwarf planets
and the Kuiper belt

In 2006 the International Astronomical Union passed Resolution 5A. A planet does three things: it orbits the Sun, it is nearly round, and it has cleared the neighbourhood around its orbit. A dwarf planet orbits the Sun and is nearly round, has not cleared that neighbourhood, and is not a satellite. NASA Science lists five, in order of distance: Ceres, Pluto, Haumea, Makemake, and Eris. This page draws them from above the ecliptic, with Neptune’s orbit as the outer planet and Ceres in the asteroid belt for contrast. The shade is the classical Kuiper belt’s average distance, about 40 to 50 AU. The dashed circles are Neptune’s 3:2 and 2:1, from the same formula as the resonances desk. Pluto’s orbit is the 3:2 case: two laps of Pluto for three of Neptune. The ruler switches between linear and log distance. The snow-line distances, a few AU, are the frost line. This map is the region past Neptune.

Why these are dwarf planets

Orbits the Sun
Resolution 5A, criterion (a), for a planet and for a dwarf planet: the body is in orbit around the Sun. The same resolution’s footnote names eight planets: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune.
Nearly round
Criterion (b): sufficient mass for self-gravity to overcome rigid-body forces so that it assumes a hydrostatic equilibrium, nearly round, shape. Haumea is the awkward case. NASA Science says the fast spin makes it look like a football. The IAU still lists it among the five. The circle on this page is the equatorial diameter NASA prints, not a sphere.
Cleared the neighbourhood
Criterion (c) is the line a planet crosses and a dwarf planet does not. A planet has cleared the neighbourhood around its orbit. A dwarf planet has not. The resolution does not print a formula for “cleared.” This page does not invent one.
Not a satellite
A dwarf planet, clause (d), is not a satellite. Charon orbits Pluto. It is a moon on the fact sheet, not one of the five. NASA Science says Ceres has no moons, Eris has Dysnomia, Haumea has Namaka and Hi'iaka, and Makemake has one provisional moon.
Classical Kuiper belt
NASA Science: all classical KBOs have a similar average distance from the Sun between about 40 and 50 AU. The shade is that sentence, drawn at 40 and 50. The same page puts the belt’s inner edge at Neptune’s orbit, about 30 AU, and the end of the inner main region around 50 AU. Those are a wider description. The shade is the classical sentence.
Pluto’s 3:2
NASA Science: Pluto circles the Sun twice for every three times Neptune goes around, and the objects that share that resonance are the plutinos. On this page the dashed 3:2 is an exact resonance on Neptune’s fact-sheet axis, 39.401489 AU. Pluto’s own J2000 axis is 39.48168677 AU. Close, and not the same number. The resonances desk is that formula, with Jupiter’s gaps as well as this pair. Pluto’s axis is also outside the about 40 to 50 AU classical range. A plutino is a resonant object. This page does not call it a classical KBO.

Where the numbers come from

  • IAU — Result of the 2006 Resolution votes — Resolution 5A. A planet (a) is in orbit around the Sun, (b) has sufficient mass for its self-gravity to overcome rigid body forces so that it assumes a hydrostatic equilibrium (nearly round) shape, and (c) has cleared the neighbourhood around its orbit. A dwarf planet meets (a) and (b), (c) has not cleared the neighbourhood around its orbit, and (d) is not a satellite. Footnote: the eight planets are Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune. Resolution 6A: Pluto is a dwarf planet by that definition and the prototype of a new category of trans-Neptunian objects. The press page names the first members as Ceres, Pluto, and 2003 UB313.
  • NASA Science — Dwarf planets — five dwarf planets, in order of distance: Ceres, Pluto, Haumea, Makemake, and Eris. The IAU says there may be many more, perhaps more than a hundred. NASA’s classroom list is “orbit its host star,” “be mostly round,” and “be big enough that its gravity cleared away any other objects of similar size.” That is NASA’s summary. The criteria quoted above are the resolution.
  • NASA Science — Kuiper Belt facts — classical KBOs have a similar average distance between about 40 and 50 AU. The inner edge begins at Neptune’s orbit, about 30 AU. The inner main region ends around 50 AU. Pluto is in a 3:2 resonance: it circles the Sun twice for every three times Neptune goes around. The same page names the plutinos, and the resonances 1:1, 4:3, 3:2, and 2:1. Eris, Haumea, and Makemake are named among the dwarf planets in the belt.
  • NASA NSSDCA — Pluto Fact Sheet — J2000 semi-major axis 39.48168677 AU, eccentricity 0.24880766, inclination 17.14175°, node 110.30347°, longitude of perihelion 224.06676°. Sidereal orbit period 90,560 days. Equatorial radius 1,188 km. Number of natural satellites 5. The comparison table’s eccentricity 0.2444, inclination 17.16°, and semimajor-axis ratio 39.236 are different lines and are not drawn. Updated 11 January 2024.
  • NASA Science — Pluto facts — equatorial diameter about 2,377 km. Orbit described as 248 years long, from about 30 AU to 49.3 AU, average 39 AU. Five moons: Charon, Nix, Hydra, Kerberos, and Styx. The fact-sheet radius and the J2000 elements are the numbers drawn. These sentences are the rounded page.
