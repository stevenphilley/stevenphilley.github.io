Foucault
the turning plane

A Foucault pendulum’s swing plane turns relative to the floor. The rate is Earth’s sidereal rotation times the sine of the latitude, Ω sin φ. This page uses a mean sidereal day of 86,164.0905 s, which is 23 h 56 m 4.0905 s, or 23.934470 mean solar hours. JPL’s astrodynamic parameters print 86164.09054 s, cited to Seidelmann’s 1992 explanatory supplement. Rounded to four decimals in the seconds, that is 86164.0905. The calculator uses 86164.0905. NASA’s Earth Fact Sheet lists the sidereal rotation period as 23.9345 hours. 23.934470 rounds to 23.9345 at four decimals. Neither printed rounding is substituted. At the pole the period is that one sidereal day, 23.934470 h. At 30° the sine is one half, so the period is twice the unrounded sidereal day, 47.868939 h. The six-decimal hour strings are rounded separately. At the equator sin φ is zero and the plane does not precess. Léon Foucault described the experiment in 1851, showed it at the Paris Observatory in February, and hung a long pendulum in the Panthéon in March. The same sidereal day, as a clock rather than a pendulum, is the sidereal desk. The slow walk of the axis is the precession desk. 37.29° N is the latitude the local sky uses for Campbell.

Educational idealized model, not an engineering design. A real pendulum needs a long wire, a heavy bob, and a drive or a Charron ring to fight damping and elliptical drift. This page leaves those out. The line is the swing plane, seen from above. It is not a measured wire, and the bob is not given a length, so the drawing has no swing period. The arc is the position within the current turn. The readout keeps the full angle. Changing the latitude recomputes the turn after the elapsed time. It does not carry an old angle forward. The latitude buttons are round classroom values, not surveys.

The sine law

What turns
At the pole the swing plane stays fixed against the stars and the floor turns under it. One full circle takes one sidereal day, and viewed from above that turn is clockwise. Elsewhere the floor’s turn relative to the plane is only the part of Earth’s rotation along the local vertical, Ω sin φ. At the equator that part is zero, and the plane does not precess. In the rotating frame the same rate is the Coriolis acceleration on the moving bob. “The Earth turns under a fixed plane” is the polar case. It is not the whole story at other latitudes.
The period
T = 86,164.0905 s / |sin φ|. In mean solar hours that is 23.934470 / |sin φ|. At 90° the result is 23.934470 h, which is 23 h 56 m 4.0905 s. At 30°, sin 30° is one half, so the period is 47.868939 h. The fact sheet’s 23.9345 h is the four-decimal rounding. It is not the divisor.
The two hour rates
A sidereal hour is 1/24 of the sidereal day, so the Earth turns 15° and the plane turns 15° sin φ. A mean solar hour is 3600 s. In that hour the Earth turns 15.04107°, the five-decimal rounding of 360° × 3600 / 86,164.0905. The plane turns that unrounded angle times sin φ. Campbell, 37.29° N, is 9.08774° per sidereal hour and 9.11262° per mean solar hour. Paris, 48.85° N, is 11.29484° and 11.32577°. Sydney, 33.87° S, carries a minus sign: the plane turns the other way.
The sign
North of the equator the plane turns clockwise, viewed from above. South of the equator it turns counter-clockwise. The equator does not precess. A positive rate on this page is the clockwise sense.
What a real pendulum adds
Air and the pivot take energy, so the swing dies unless something drives it. The path also wants to open into an ellipse. A Charron ring, or a drive that replaces only the lost energy, is how a museum pendulum keeps a flat swing. This page does not size a wire, a bob, a ring, or a drive.
