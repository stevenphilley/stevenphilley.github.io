The main asteroid belt is the region between Mars and Jupiter where most known asteroids orbit the Sun. A Kirkwood gap is a dip in that population at a mean-motion resonance with Jupiter. Write p:q when the asteroid completes p orbits while Jupiter completes q. JPL states the 3:1 that way. Kepler’s third law turns the ratio into a distance. This page anchors it to Jupiter’s fact sheet, 5.20336301 AU and 4,332.589 days, the same pair as the resonances desk: a = 5.20336301 × (q/p)2/3. The 3:1 computes 2.501516 AU. The Kepler desk is the one-sun form, P² = a³. Pair this page with Jupiter (the anchor), dwarf planets (Ceres), Lagrange (the Trojan points), and impacts (what a body does if it meets a planet).

Educational schematic. Not a census of the belt and not an ephemeris. The bars are the same height except for a notch one bin wide, 0.025 AU, at each computed Kirkwood axis, so the line can be seen. The period on the card is that period times (a / 5.20336301) to the power 3/2. Named orbits are JPL Small-Body Database solutions and are not replaced by this period. The 3:1 Kirkwood gap. Jupiter’s fact sheet, mean orbital elements (J2000), prints a semimajor axis of 5.20336301 AU and a sidereal orbit period of 4,332.589 days. A 3:1 ratio means the inner body completes 3 orbits while Jupiter completes 1. The semi-major axis is 5.20336301 times (1/3) to the power 2/3, which computes 2.501516 AU. The period is 4,332.589 days times 1/3, which computes 1,444.196 days (3.9539 years of 365.256 days). That is Kepler’s third law with this fact sheet as the anchor: a / a_Jupiter = (P / P_Jupiter) to the power 2/3. JPL’s June 2007 histogram of 156,929 numbered asteroids labels a gap at this resonance. This page marks the computed axis. It does not replot those orbits. The histogram here is a schematic. Bar height is not a count, and the notch is one bin, 0.025 AU, so the line can be seen. That width is not a measured gap.

What the words mean

The anchor
a = 5.20336301 × (q/p) to the power 2/3. P = 4,332.589 days × (a / 5.20336301) to the power 3/2. At a listed ratio that period is 4,332.589 days times q/p. The resonances desk prints the same 3:1, 5:2, 7:3, 2:1, and 3:2.
One-sun year
The Kepler desk uses P² = a³, so the year is a to the power 3/2 when a is in AU. (5.20336301) to that power is 11.8693 years of 365.256 days. The fact-sheet period divided by 365.256 days is 11.8618 years. This page does not use the one-sun year to place the gaps, because the ratio with the fact-sheet period would miss the integer. JPL’s asteroid periods are the orbit solutions. They are not the anchored periods on the card.
Inner, middle, outer
NASA’s Basics of Space Flight describes two compositional belts, from Sloan Digital Sky Survey data: an inner belt centered at 2.8 AU, silicate-rich, and an outer belt centered at 3.2 AU, carbon-rich. Those are centers, not edges. JPL’s orbit classes are different. Inner Main-belt is a below 2.0 au and q above 1.666 au. Main-belt is a above 2.0 au and below 3.2 au, and q above 1.666 au. Outer Main-belt is a above 3.2 au and below 4.6 au. Jupiter Trojan is a above 4.6 au and below 5.5 au, and e below 0.3. JPL’s word “inner” is sunward of 2.0 AU. It is not the stretch between the 4:1 and the 3:1. This page does not rename the chart stretches as a catalog.
Where the four sit
Vesta’s axis is between the 4:1 and the 3:1. Ceres and Pallas are between the 3:1 and the 5:2. Hygiea is between the 7:3 and the 2:1, and still below 3.2 AU, so the semi-major axis is inside JPL’s Main-belt window. All four records are class MBA. NASA’s 2.8 AU center is a sentence about composition. It is not Ceres’s 2.765552595034094 AU, and it is not replaced by it.
Hilda and the Trojans
The 3:2 computes 3.970909 AU. JPL’s elements diagram labels the Hilda group. Ip’s 1976 abstract says the 3:2 coupling of some of these asteroids is stable. The 1:1 is Jupiter’s own axis. NASA Science says Trojans share an orbit and gather at the L4 and L5 points, and that the Jupiter Trojans are thought to be as numerous as the asteroids in the belt. The class window is wider than the line.
Mass and the crossing
NASA Science says the total mass of all the asteroids combined is less than that of Earth’s Moon. The same page estimates the belt holds between 1.1 and 1.9 million asteroids larger than 1 kilometer, and millions of smaller ones. No mass in kilograms is printed here. NASA’s Basics of Space Flight says various spacecraft have crossed the main asteroid belt: Pioneer 10 and Pioneer 11, Voyagers 1 and 2, Ulysses, Galileo (crossed twice), Cassini, New Horizons, and Juno. The same chapter calls the Kirkwood gaps relatively empty areas between the main concentrations. The belt those spacecraft crossed is not a wall of rock.
A near flag
The card says “near” when the period ratio is within 1 percent of a listed p/q. At the exact axis the flag says “on.” The 1 percent is there so a slider step can still light the resonance. It is not a published half-width. A dash is not used: every axis on the slider has a period.

Where the numbers come from

  • NASA NSSDCA — Jupiter Fact Sheet — Jupiter Mean Orbital Elements (J2000), semimajor axis 5.20336301 AU. Sidereal orbit period 4,332.589 days. Earth’s sidereal orbit period on the same sheet is 365.256 days, the year when a day count is divided out.
  • NASA NSSDCA — Notes on the fact sheets — most values come from a list that includes JPL Ephemeris DE430/DE431. The notes say the values are not a single official set. This page uses the J2000 block printed on the Jupiter sheet, the same pair as the resonances desk. It does not mix in an osculating element from another epoch.
  • NASA — Basics of Space Flight, Kepler’s third law — the square of the period is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis. This page uses a / a_Jupiter = (P / P_Jupiter) to the power 2/3.
  • JPL SSD — Asteroid main-belt distribution — histogram of June 2007, 156,929 numbered asteroids. Primary gaps labeled 3:1, 5:2, 7:3, and 2:1. The 3:1 is where the asteroid completes 3 orbits for every 1 of Jupiter. This page does not replot the counts. The 4:1 is computed here and is not a label on that chart.
  • JPL SSD — Asteroid elements distribution — June 2007, 159,366 numbered asteroids. Labeled groups include the Hilda group and the Trojans. Extents follow Gradie, Chapman, and Tedesco 1989, except the Hilda eccentricity limit was raised from 0.3 to 0.35. Two compositional belts, from Sloan Digital Sky Survey data: inner, centered at 2.8 AU, silicate-rich; outer, centered at 3.2 AU, carbon-rich. Spacecraft that have crossed the belt: Pioneer 10 and Pioneer 11, Voyagers 1 and 2, Ulysses, Galileo (crossed twice), Cassini, New Horizons, and Juno.
