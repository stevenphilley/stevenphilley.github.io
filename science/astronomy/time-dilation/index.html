Time dilation
and two rates

A moving clock ticks slow. A clock higher in a gravitational well ticks fast. Both are on a GPS satellite, and they do not cancel. Pick an altitude, or type a speed. The circular speed is √(GM / r). The Lorentz factor is γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²). The velocity term on the card is the exact special-relativistic rate, √(1 − v²/c²) − 1, in microseconds over 86,400 seconds. The gravitational term is the weak-field monopole, GM (1/R − 1/r) / c², against a clock at rest on the equatorial radius. GM = 3.986004418×10¹⁴ m³/s², the TCG-compatible value in IERS Conventions (2010) Table 1.1, which is the value Ashby 2003 prints. R = 6,378,137 m. c = 299,792,458 m/s exactly. The GPS preset uses the IS-GPS-200N radius, 26,559,710 m, and computes −7.21 μs/day from speed, +45.65 μs/day from gravity, and +38.44 μs/day net. NIST quotes 7, 45, and 38. The period of the same circle is the satellites desk. A hovering clock near a black hole is the black holes desk. The plain-language receipt is the gravity desk.

Educational calculator. Simplified weak-field model, not for navigation or engineering. No Earth rotation, no quadrupole, no eccentricity, and no Sagnac effect. The ground clock in the gravitational term is at rest at the equatorial radius, not on the rotating geoid. A day in the microsecond rates is 86,400 SI seconds.

GPS · this page and the citations

Near −7 and +45, and not the same digits.

NIST’s public clock page says GPS satellites fall behind earthbound clocks by 7 microseconds per day because of speed, gain 45 microseconds per day because gravity is weaker, and run 38 microseconds per day fast when the two are combined. Ashby 2003, equation (35), prints 3 GM_E/(2 a c²) + Φ₀/c² = +2.5046×10⁻¹⁰ − 6.9693×10⁻¹⁰ = −4.4647×10⁻¹⁰. He reads the negative sign as the orbiting clock running fast. Over 86,400 seconds that printed offset is +38.58 μs/day. He prints GM_E = 3.986004418×10¹⁴ m³/s² and a₁ = 6.3781370×10⁶ m, and later a nominal semi-major axis a₀ = 2.656175×10⁷ m. With this page’s GM and c, 3 GM/(2 a₀ c²) is 2.5046×10⁻¹⁰, the digits he prints. In his figure the effects cancel at a ≈ 9,545 km. On this monopole the circular balance, r = 3R/2, is 9,567.21 km. This page’s GPS preset is the IS-GPS-200N radius 26,559,710 m. It computes −7.21 μs/day from speed, +45.65 μs/day from the potential difference, and +38.44 μs/day net. At Ashby’s a₀ the same formulas give −7.21 μs/day, +45.65 μs/day, and +38.44 μs/day. His 38.58 μs/day is larger than this net because his reference is the geoid. The IAU value he cites is L_G = 6.969290134×10⁻¹⁰, so Φ₀/c² = −6.969290134×10⁻¹⁰. This page’s −GM/(R c²) is −6.95349×10⁻¹⁰. Rotation and the quadrupole are in his ground clock and not in this one.

What the buttons are

ISS is the lithograph’s about 420 km. NASA’s 2023 station lithograph says an average altitude of about 420 kilometers. The button is that altitude. The card is the circle: 7.657 km/s, velocity −28.18 μs/day, gravity +3.71 μs/day, net −24.47 μs/day. Speed wins. Some NASA pages say about 400 km. This page does not average those sentences into a third altitude.

GPS is a radius, not the words “about 20,200 km.” IS-GPS-200N sets AREF = 26,559,710 m when the ephemeris correction ΔA is zero. gps.gov says approximately 20,200 km and twice a day. The altitude here is AREF minus 6,378,137 m, 20,181.57 km. The popular 20,200 km is not substituted.

The Moon is a distance. Geostationary is a period. The Moon button is a test-particle circle at the Moon Fact Sheet semimajor axis, 384,400 km. Its circular speed on this GM is 1.018 km/s and its period is 27.452 d. The sheet’s mean orbital velocity is 1.022 km/s and its revolution period is 27.3217 days. Earth’s GM alone does not reproduce that month, so those sheet lines are not used. Geostationary on this page is 35,786.07 km, the radius whose period equals the Earth Fact Sheet sidereal rotation, 23.9345 hours, with this page’s GM. The satellites desk prints 35,786.05 km because it uses that sheet’s rounded GM, 0.39860 × 10^6 km³/s², and the same 23.9345 hours.

Literacy · the words on the readout

Lorentz factor
γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²). A moving clock’s proper time is the stay-at-home time divided by γ. One year aboard is γ years at home, at constant speed. At orbital speeds γ − 1 is a few parts in 10¹⁰ or 10¹¹, so the card prints 1 + that difference.
Velocity term
√(1 − v²/c²) − 1, times 86,400 seconds, in microseconds. Negative means slow. Ashby’s GPS bookkeeping uses the second-order piece −v²/(2c²). At the speeds of these orbits the two print the same microseconds. They part company as v becomes an appreciable fraction of c.
Gravitational term
GM (1/R − 1/r) / c². The Newtonian potential is −GM/r. A higher clock is less deep in the well, so this term is positive: fast. R is the equatorial radius, 6,378,137 m. The term is zero at the surface and grows as the radius grows.
Net drift
The gravitational fraction plus the Lorentz fraction, over one day of 86,400 seconds. For GPS the gravity term wins, so the net is fast. For the ISS the velocity term wins, so the net is slow. They balance, on this monopole, at r = 3R/2, which is 9,567.21 km.
What is left out
Earth’s spin, the equatorial bulge, the eccentricity of a real GPS orbit, the Sagnac effect, and the Shapiro delay. Ashby’s Φ₀ is an effective potential on the rotating geoid, L_G = 6.969290134×10⁻¹⁰. This page’s surface is only −GM/(R c²). That is why his printed net, +38.58 μs/day, is 0.14 μs/day faster than the +38.44 this page computes. The factory offset he prints, −4.4647×10⁻¹⁰, is the correction applied so the satellite clock keeps geoid time. This page does not apply it. It reports the drift.

Sources · the constants and the GPS story

Cited, not invented

  • NIST — speed of light in vacuum and BIPM — the metre — c = 299,792,458 m/s exactly. This page divides by that c.
  • IERS Conventions (2010), IERS Technical Note 36 — Table 1.1, geocentric gravitational constant GM⊕ = 3.986004418 × 10¹⁴ m³ s⁻², marked TCG-compatible. The same table’s equatorial radius is 6,378,136.6 m. This page does not use that radius. L_G = 6.969290134 × 10⁻¹⁰ is listed there as a defining constant, d(TT)/d(TCG) = 1 − L_G, from the IAU XXIV General Assembly. The TT-compatible GM differs in the last digit and is not substituted. The difference does not move the microseconds this page prints.
  • Ashby 2003, Living Reviews in Relativity 6, 1 — “Relativity in the Global Positioning System.” Equation (13) prints GM_E = 3.986004418 × 10¹⁴ m³ s⁻² and a₁ = 6.3781370 × 10⁶ m. Equation (35) prints 3 GM_E/(2 a c²) + Φ₀/c² = +2.5046 × 10⁻¹⁰ − 6.9693 × 10⁻¹⁰ = −4.4647 × 10⁻¹⁰, and the negative sign means the orbiting clock runs fast. He cites the IAU value Φ₀/c² = 6.969290134 × 10⁻¹⁰. Later he prints a nominal semi-major axis a₀ = 2.656175 × 10⁷ m and says the effects cancel at a ≈ 9545 km. Open copy: Springer.
  • NIST — Putting Einstein to the Test — GPS satellites “fall behind earthbound clocks by 7 microseconds per day,” weaker gravity “should speed them up by 45 microseconds per day,” and the clocks “run 38 microseconds per day faster.” Those are the integers on that page. This page’s GPS preset is −7.21, +45.65, and +38.44.
  • NASA NSSDCA — Earth Fact Sheet — equatorial radius 6,378.137 km, the radius this page uses. Sidereal rotation period 23.9345 hours, used only to place geostationary. The sheet’s GM, 0.39860 × 10⁶ km³/s², is the rounded figure on the satellites desk. It is not the GM in the arithmetic here.
  • NASA — International Space Station lithograph, 2023 — average altitude of about 420 kilometers. The ISS button is that 420 km. The rates are computed.
  • IS-GPS-200N — AREF = 26,559,710 meters, the nominal semi-major axis when ΔA = 0. The GPS button uses that radius. GPS.gov — Space segment says approximately 20,200 km. That sentence is not the radius.
  • NASA NSSDCA — Moon Fact Sheet — semimajor axis 0.3844 × 10⁶ km. Mean orbital velocity 1.022 km/s. Revolution period 27.3217 days. The button uses the semimajor axis as a radius. The speed and the period on the card are this page’s Earth-only GM. They are not those sheet lines.
