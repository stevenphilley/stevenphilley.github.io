Geologic time

Earth is about 4,567 million years old. Almost all of the animals in the gallery lived in the last 539 of those years. This chart is the International Chronostratigraphic Chart for December 2024: official names, official colours, and the boundary ages in millions of years before present (Ma).

Time is nested. An eon is the largest unit. Eons divide into eras, eras into periods, periods into epochs, and epochs into ages. The same ranks, when you mean the rocks rather than the years, are eonothem, erathem, system, series, and stage. The Phanerozoic, the eon of visible animal life, is a thin slice on a true-scale bar, so the chart can also be drawn with each period the same width.

A boundary is a physical place, not a round number chosen for convenience. For the Phanerozoic, and for the Ediacaran, a unit begins at a Global Boundary Stratotype Section and Point, a GSSP, the “golden spike”: one point in one cliff or core where a fossil, a chemical shift, or a magnetic reversal has been agreed as the base. Older Precambrian units are mostly still defined by a Global Standard Stratigraphic Age, a GSSA, a round number of millions of years. A tilde on a date means the age is approximate. Learn the order

Phanerozoic periods, oldest first: Camels Often Sit Down Carefully. Perhaps Their Joints Creak. Cambrian, Ordovician, Silurian, Devonian, Carboniferous, Permian, Triassic, Jurassic, Cretaceous. Then three more periods: Paleogene, Neogene, Quaternary.

Cenozoic epochs: Pretty Eager Old Men Play Poker Hard. Paleocene, Eocene, Oligocene, Miocene, Pliocene, Pleistocene, Holocene.

Ages and colours follow the International Chronostratigraphic Chart v2024/12 (Cohen, Finney, Gibbard, and Fan, 2013, updated; International Commission on Stratigraphy).
