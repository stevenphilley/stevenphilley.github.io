Primers

Six doorways

Realism vs formalism

A teaching spectrum, not a war with only two armies. Realist impulses emphasize the world’s density — long takes, deep space, location sound, less overt manipulation — so the viewer explores. Formalist impulses emphasize cinema’s constructedness — montage, stylized design, expressive camera — so meaning is actively shaped by form. Most films mix both. Ask: what does this film want me to believe is “given,” and what does it flaunt as made?

Historically, critics associated Bazin with realist long-take/depth preferences and Eisenstein with montage collision. Use the names as doorways, then watch the films.

Auteur theory

Auteur thinking treats a director (sometimes another key craftsperson) as the primary creative author whose themes and style recur across films — even inside a studio system. It began as a critical strategy (Cahiers du Cinéma; popularized in the U.S. by Andrew Sarris): look for signature, not only prestige scripts.

Useful: it trains close attention to pattern. Limits: film is collaborative; focusing only on directors can erase labor, stars, writers, and industrial constraint. Ask “auteur of what?” and “who else authored this frame?”

Genre theory

Beyond marketing labels, genre theory studies how genres work as systems of expectation — iconography, narrative plots, audience rituals, and industrial cycles (rise, peak, parody, revival). Genres are historically sticky but change: the Western of 1939 is not the Western of 1992.

Read a genre film for fulfillment and variation. Hybridity is normal. See the genres desk for contracts and breaks.

Spectatorship

Spectatorship asks how films position viewers — not only “what happens on screen,” but “who does the film think I am, and what does it want me to want?” Theories range from apparatus and gaze models to cognitive approaches (how we infer, feel, predict) and cultural studies (how different audiences read differently).

Practical habit: notice address (direct look at camera), identification cues, and when a film withholds or forces alignment with a character’s knowledge.

Feminist film theory (overview)

A cluster of approaches examining how cinema constructs gender, desire, and power. A widely taught starting point is work on the male gaze (Laura Mulvey’s 1975 essay): classical Hollywood often aligns looking with masculine desire while women are positioned as spectacle. That claim has been debated, revised, and expanded for decades.

Broader feminist film work includes representation and stereotype critique; women’s authorship and industrial access; intersectional analysis (race, class, sexuality, disability); queer readings; and attention to genre (melodrama, horror) as sites of negotiation. Educational takeaway: ask who looks, who is looked at, who acts, and who is framed as “to-be-looked-at.”

Apparatus

Apparatus theory (1970s film theory) analyzes the cinema situation itself — darkened room, projected beam, seated spectators, continuity that sutures you into story space — as an ideological machine that can naturalize a point of view.

Even if you reject strong versions of the theory, the habit remains valuable: the screening setup and the editing grammar are not neutral pipes. Streaming on a phone in daylight is a different apparatus than a 35mm theatrical print — form of exhibition shapes attention.

How to use theory without drowning

Method

  • State the claim in one sentence you could explain to a friend.
  • Name one scene that supports it and one that complicates it.
  • Separate descriptive claims (“this framing isolates her”) from evaluative ones (“this is progressive / regressive”).
  • Cite when you write for class; this desk is orientation, not a citation engine.
