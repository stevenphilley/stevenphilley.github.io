Form

The building blocks of cinematic language: what is in the frame, how the camera looks, how shots join, and how sound works with (or against) the image. Learn these terms, then practice them on the Watch page.

Shot size

How much world in the frame

Shot scale ranges from extreme close-up (a detail) to extreme long shot (a landscape that swallows the figure). Changing scale is a rhetorical move: closer usually means more intimacy or pressure; wider usually means context, isolation, or spectacle. See the interactive gallery on the hub.

Camera angle

Where we look from

  • Eye-level — default “neutral” vantage; still a choice.
  • High angle — looks down; can diminish, survey, or protect.
  • Low angle — looks up; can aggrandize, threaten, or awe.
  • Dutch / canted — horizon tilted; unease, intoxication, fracture.
  • POV — approximates a character’s optical viewpoint (not the same as “we share their feelings”).
  • Over-the-shoulder — anchors dialogue in conversational space.

Angle is not a moral stamp — a low angle on a villain can make them terrifying; on a child it can make the adult world huge. Always ask: power relative to whom?

A push-in (dolly or zoom toward a face) often marks realization. A pull-out can isolate or ironize. Static frames are also a choice — duration becomes the event.

Editing rhythm

Joins, duration, pulse

Average shot length (ASL) is a blunt statistic, but useful: action cinema often cuts faster; contemplative art cinema often holds. Rhythm is also patterned — accelerating cuts toward a climax, then a long hold after impact.

  • Cut — instant join; the default.
  • Dissolve — overlap; time passing, memory, soft transition.
  • Fade — to/from black (or color); chaptering, death, sleep, ending.
  • Wipe / iris — graphic transition; often genre-marked (adventure, silent-era).
  • Match cut — graphic or conceptual continuity across a join.
  • Jump cut — discontinuity inside similar framing; rupture or energy.
  • Cross-cut — parallel actions; suspense, comparison.

Sound

Diegetic · non-diegetic · against the image

Diegetic sound belongs to the story world (dialogue, footsteps, a radio the characters hear). Non-diegetic sound is for us (score, voice-over narrator outside the scene, some sound design flourishes). The border can blur — a song starts on a car radio then swells past what the car could produce.

  • Sync vs asynchronous — sound matched to visible source, or deliberately off.
  • Offscreen sound — expands space beyond the frame; threat or invitation.
  • Sound bridge — audio from next scene starts early (or hangs late).
  • Silence — not empty; often the loudest cue.
  • Voice-over — guide, ironist, liar, or essayist; ask who speaks and why we trust them.

Mise-en-scène checklist

Everything placed before the camera

Mise-en-scène (literally “putting into the scene”) covers setting, lighting, costume/makeup, figure behavior, and composition. When you freeze a frame, work the list:

  • Setting / set dressing — location vs studio; props that characterize.
  • Lighting — high-key (even, few shadows) vs low-key (contrast, pools of dark); motivated practicals vs theatrical.
  • Costume & makeup — class, era, disguise, transformation.
  • Blocking & performance — who dominates space; gesture vs stillness.
  • Composition — balance, depth planes, leading lines, negative space, center vs edge.
  • Color & texture — palette motifs; grain, gloss, grit.
  • Depth of field — what’s sharp; what’s soft and why.
