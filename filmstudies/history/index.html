Film Studies · Eras & movements

History

A concise tour of major eras and movements — dates are approximate, borders are leaky, and many national cinemas deserve their own desks. Cards list signature traits and a couple of widely known titles for orientation (public-domain and culturally canonical references). No film stills; read, then watch with a critical list in hand.

Timeline How to use this

Timeline cards

Silent → classical → art cinema → New Waves → New Hollywood → now

Silent cinema & early narrative

1890s – late 1920s

From actualities and attractions to multi-reel storytelling. Parallel editing, close-ups, and continuity grammar mature (Griffith and peers). National styles diverge: Hollywood industrialization, German Expressionist sets and shadows, Soviet montage as argument, French Impressionist subjectivity. Intertitles carry dialogue; live music and effects accompany exhibition.

Canonical touchstones: A Trip to the Moon (1902); The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari (1920); Battleship Potemkin (1925); Metropolis (1927); Sunrise (1927).

Classical Hollywood

roughly 1927 – early 1960s

Studio system, star contracts, genre cycles, and the continuity style as default. Sound synchronizes (talkies), then deep-focus and long-take options (e.g. Welles) coexist with coverage cutting. Production Code shapes what can be shown. Narrative clarity, goal-oriented protagonists, and invisible craft dominate — even when directors push against the grain.

Canonical touchstones: Citizen Kane (1941); Casablanca (1942); Singin’ in the Rain (1952); many studio Westerns, musicals, and melodramas of the period.

Postwar art cinema

late 1940s – 1960s

Italian Neorealism shoots in streets with nonprofessionals and open endings. Elsewhere, modernist art cinema loosens causality, emphasizes subjectivity and duration (Bergman, Antonioni, Resnais). Ambiguity becomes a feature, not a bug. Festival culture and art-house circuits expand.

Canonical touchstones: Bicycle Thieves (1948); Tokyo Story (1953); The Seventh Seal (1957); Breathless (1960) bridges into New Wave.

New Waves

late 1950s – 1970s (multiple)

French New Wave: jump cuts, location shooting, cinephile play, auteur branding. Parallel “new waves” and new cinemas: Britain, Brazil (Cinema Novo), Japan, Eastern Europe, later New German Cinema. Shared impulse — break studio polish, claim youth and politics, reinvent grammar.

Canonical touchstones: The 400 Blows (1959); Breathless (1960); Persona (1966); examples across national new cinemas rather than one list.

New Hollywood

roughly mid-1960s – early 1980s

U.S. studio crisis meets film-school generation. Antiheroes, fractured tones, European influences, and rating-system freedom after the Code. Blockbusters (mid-70s) begin re-centering spectacle and franchise logic even as personal cinema peaks.

Canonical touchstones: Bonnie and Clyde (1967); Easy Rider (1969); The Godfather (1972); Taxi Driver (1976); Apocalypse Now (1979).

Contemporary & global

1980s – present

Conglomerate studios, tentpoles, and IP; independent and Sundance ecosystems; digital capture and DIY distribution; transnational co-productions. Streaming reshapes windows and serial form. Global art cinema and popular cinemas (Bollywood, Nollywood, East Asian blockbusters, etc.) complicate any single “Hollywood timeline.” Theory and criticism track identity, media convergence, and platform aesthetics.

Canonical touchstones: Widely taught anchors vary by course — use local syllabi; treat “contemporary” as plural, not one style.

How to use this

Study method

  • Pick one era; watch one touchstone; note three formal habits (cutting, camera, performance).
  • Ask what industrial conditions enabled the style (studios, war, censorship, new cameras, festivals).
  • Compare a “classical” scene to a New Wave scene with the form checklist.
  • Remember: movements are labels critics and filmmakers argue over — useful maps, not natural kinds.
