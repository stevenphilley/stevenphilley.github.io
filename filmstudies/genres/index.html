Genres

Genre is a contract with the audience: shared expectations about story world, tone, and payoff. Filmmakers fulfill, bend, or break that contract on purpose. These cards sketch conventions and common breaks — starting points for reading, not cages.

Genre as contract

Expectation · recognition · variation

You do not need a checklist tattooed on your arm. You need to notice when a film trains you to expect a showdown, a laugh, a jump scare, or a wedding — then delivers, delays, or refuses. Industry uses genre to market; critics use it to compare; fans use it to find “more like this.” Hybrid films (rom-com-horror, neo-noir sci-fi) trade on double literacy.

Genre cards

Seven common contracts

Western

Contract

A myth of frontier order: open land, coded violence, and a negotiation between wilderness and civilization.

Conventions

Iconography (horses, guns, dusty towns); clear moral or ambiguous antihero; showdowns; landscape as moral stage; community vs loner.

How films break them

Revisionist westerns muddy heroism; Indigenous and border perspectives reverse the gaze; “anti-westerns” drain glory from the shootout; settings migrate (space western, noir western).

Film noir

Contract

A mood of urban fatalism: crime, desire, and compromised protagonists in a world that feels already lost.

Conventions

Low-key lighting, canted frames, voice-over, flashbacks; femmes fatales (and critiques of that trope); rain-slick streets; moral gray; hard-boiled talk.

How films break them

Neo-noir updates settings and gender politics; color noir; comedy-noir hybrids; films that expose noir’s misogyny while keeping the style.

Melodrama

Contract

Feeling structured as plot: social pressure, family, and desire expressed through excess — music, coincidence, spectacle of emotion.

Conventions

Clear moral stakes; pathos; domestic or romantic crisis; timing of revelation; music that tells you how to feel (and when to resist it).

How films break them

Ironic or detached melodrama; male weepies; political melodrama; films that make excess the subject rather than the tool.

Science fiction

Contract

Speculative what-if grounded in altered science/tech/social systems — wonder and dread about possible worlds.

Conventions

Novum (the new thing); world-building; future/past/alt-present; icons (ships, labs, ruins); questions of humanity, labor, ecology, surveillance.

How films break them

Sci-fi without gadgets (social speculation); hybrid horror-sci-fi; comedies; slow cinema in speculative settings; refusing the “twist tech” explanation.

Horror

Contract

A contract to seek fear, disgust, or dread in a safe frame — then play with how safe that frame is.

Conventions

Threat (monster, curse, other people); suspense vs shock; final girl / survival patterns; taboo and body; atmosphere and offscreen space.

How films break them

Art-horror and elevated horror debates; comedy-horror; refusing catharsis; flipping who the “monster” is; found-footage and digital-native forms.

Comedy

Contract

A promise of laughter and usually a social repair (or a pointed refusal of repair).

Conventions

Timing, incongruity, status games; romantic comedy’s meet-cute and obstacles; parody and satire; physical gag construction; punchline editing.

How films break them

Anti-comedies that withhold laughs; cringe and awkwardness; dark comedy; mixing genres so the laugh catches in the throat.

Documentary

Contract

A claim to address the historical world — with rhetoric, evidence, and ethics — not a guarantee of neutrality.

Conventions

Modes (expository, observational, participatory, reflexive, performative — after Bill Nichols); interviews; archive; voice-over; vérité camera presence.

How films break them

Hybrid docufiction; essay films; mockumentary; acknowledging the camera’s power; refusing tidy thesis structure.
