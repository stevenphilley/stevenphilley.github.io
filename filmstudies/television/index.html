History of Television

A rich but readable arc from spinning disks to streaming apps — invention races, network eras, color and cable, the digital switchover, and platform TV. Dates are approximate; national timelines diverge. Pair with film history when cinema and television share industrial DNA.

Timeline

Mechanical → electronic → broadcast → boom → color → cable → digital → streaming

Mechanical television

1880s – early 1930s

Paul Nipkow’s scanning disk (1884 patent) turns a scene into sequential light samples. Experimenters — Charles Francis Jenkins in the U.S., John Logie Baird in Britain, and others — build electromechanical cameras and receivers: spinning disks, selenium or photocells, neon lamps. Images are low-line (often under 100 lines), dim, and flicker-prone, but they prove “seeing at a distance” is possible. Short-wave and AM experiments carry pictures across cities; demonstration theaters and hobbyist kits appear before electronic systems win.

Landmarks: Nipkow disk concept (1884); Baird televised silhouette / greyscale demos (mid-1920s); Jenkins radiovisor broadcasts; BBC experimental mechanical service before electronic takeover.

Electronic TV — Farnsworth & Zworykin

1920s – 1930s

All-electronic scanning replaces spinning disks. Philo T. Farnsworth demonstrates an image dissector camera tube (1927 landmark) and pursues independent patents. Vladimir K. Zworykin, working with Westinghouse and then RCA, develops the iconoscope and kinescope path that industrial labs scale. Patent fights and cross-licensing shape who can commercialize what; RCA’s manufacturing and broadcast muscle matter as much as tube physics. By the late 1930s, electronic systems deliver sharper, brighter pictures than mechanical TV can sustain.

Landmarks: Farnsworth image dissector demo (1927); Zworykin iconoscope/kinescope lineage; RCA–Farnsworth patent settlement era; 1939 New York World’s Fair RCA TV showcase.

Broadcast beginnings

1930s – mid-1940s

Public services and commercial stations leave the lab. Britain’s BBC launches a regular electronic high-definition service from Alexandra Palace (1936). In the U.S., experimental and limited commercial broadcasts grow under FCC rules; network radio companies (NBC, CBS) treat television as the next distribution layer. WWII freezes consumer set production and slows civilian expansion, while radar and electronics R&D feed postwar TV hardware. Programming is live or film — scarce, scheduled, and local-to-network.

Landmarks: BBC electronic service (1936); U.S. commercial authorization and early network TV; wartime pause; VHF channel plans and standards debates.

Postwar boom

late 1940s – 1950s

Set ownership explodes in the U.S. and other recovering economies. Living-room antennas, network evening schedules, and “must-see” live drama, variety, news, and sports remake leisure. The freeze on new U.S. stations (1948–1952) and the Sixth Report & Order reshape channel allocation, including UHF. Sponsors and agencies invent TV advertising grammar; Hollywood initially resists, then supplies filmed series. Television becomes a mass political medium — conventions, debates, and crisis coverage enter the home.

Landmarks: rapid set penetration; freeze & allocation settlement; golden-age live anthology drama; filmed series and Hollywood crossover; early news spectacle.

Color television

1950s – 1970s (mass adoption)

Compatible color is the engineering prize: color signals that still look correct on black-and-white sets. After competing systems, the U.S. adopts NTSC color (1953). Early colorcasts and expensive receivers keep adoption slow until the mid-1960s, when networks shift primetime to color and set prices fall. Elsewhere, PAL and SECAM become regional standards. Color changes production design, sports presentation, and advertising — “in living color” is both slogan and industrial strategy.

Landmarks: NTSC color standard (1953); network color primetime push (mid-1960s); PAL/SECAM abroad; color as default by the 1970s in many markets.

Cable & satellite

1950s community antenna → 1970s–1990s multichannel

Community antenna TV (CATV) begins as a way to pull distant broadcast signals into valleys and cities with poor reception. Deregulation, microwave, and then satellite delivery turn cable into a programming industry: pay movie channels, news networks, music video, sports packages. Direct-broadcast satellite competes for the dish. Audience fragments; niche channels and 24-hour news rewrite “appointment TV.” Remote controls and VCRs (then DVRs) loosen the schedule’s grip.

Landmarks: CATV origins; HBO and satellite-fed cable; CNN and niche nets; DBS competition; VCR/DVR time-shifting.

Digital transition

1990s – 2010s

Digital compression (MPEG and successors), HDTV, and ATSC/DVB/ISDB standards remake transmission and displays. Broadcasters migrate from analog NTSC/PAL to digital terrestrial; many countries hard-switch analog off in the 2000s–2010s. Widescreen 16:9, multichannel audio, electronic program guides, and flat panels replace CRTs. DVDs, then Blu-ray and early internet video, train audiences to expect on-demand quality outside the linear grid.

Landmarks: HDTV standards fights; DTV mandates and analog switch-offs; plasma/LCD/LED sets; DVD → early web video pipelines.

Streaming & platform TV

late 2000s – present

Broadband, smartphones, and smart TVs turn IP delivery into the default growth path. Subscription video-on-demand, ad-supported free tiers, and hybrid live/VOD apps unbundle cable while rebundling content into platform catalogs. Binge release strategies, algorithmic home screens, and global co-production reshape series form — seasons as drops, seasons as ongoing drops. Linear broadcast and cable persist for news, sports, and habit, but measurement, advertising, and creative deals increasingly follow streaming logic. Media history now includes interface design and recommendation as part of “what television is.”

Landmarks: Netflix/Hulu/Amazon and peers; YouTube and creator TV; cord-cutting; AVOD/FAST channels; global streamer competition and consolidation cycles.

How to use this

Study method

  • Pick one era; note the bottleneck it solved (scanning, patents, spectrum, color compatibility, bandwidth, attention).
  • Ask who controlled distribution — inventors, RCA-style firms, networks, cable MSOs, or platforms — and how that shaped what got made.
  • Compare a live 1950s broadcast mindset to a streaming drop: scarcity of schedule vs abundance of catalog.
  • Cross-read with film history when Hollywood and TV trade talent, formats, and IP.
  • Treat standards (NTSC/PAL, ATSC/DVB) as political settlements as much as engineering wins.
