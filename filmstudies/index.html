Shot scale gallery

Interactive · ECU → ELS · CSS diagrams only

Shot scale is how much of a figure (or world) fills the frame. It is one of the fastest ways directors steer attention and emotion. Click a scale — the diagram and note update. No stills from copyrighted films; the figure is a simple block standing in for a person.

Extreme close-up

Extreme close-up (ECU). Isolates a detail — an eye, a hand on a trigger, a ticking clock. Compresses space and heightens intimacy or dread. Use sparingly; every ECU announces that this detail matters.

Think: a trembling lip before a confession; a key turning in a lock.

Close-up (CU). Face fills most of the frame. We read micro-expression and withhold environment. Classical Hollywood used CU for emotional punctuation; art cinema often holds the CU longer than comfort.

Think: reaction shots in dialogue; a star’s face as landscape.

Medium shot (MS). Roughly waist-up. Balances body language with enough room for gesture and prop interaction. Default for conversation and TV drama; “invisible” until you notice how often it appears.

Think: two characters at a table; an interview setup.

Medium long / full (MLS). Full figure with modest headroom. Shows how a body occupies space and relates to furniture, doorways, other people. Useful for blocking and comedy physicality.

Think: a character entering a room; a dance step.

Long shot (LS). Figure is small in a readable environment. Emphasizes setting as character — the street, the desert, the corridor. Westerns and landscape cinema live here.

Think: a rider on a ridge; someone alone on a city block.

Extreme long shot (ELS). Human almost lost in geography or architecture. Scale dwarfs the individual — awe, exile, or systems bigger than any one person. Establishing shots often start here before cutting in.

Think: a city skyline at dawn; a figure crossing a salt flat.

Continuity vs montage

Two editing philosophies · toggle

Editing is not only “cutting out the boring parts.” Two broad traditions still shape how scenes feel: continuity editing (smooth story space) and montage (collision of images to produce an idea).

Continuity editing aims to keep spatial and temporal orientation clear so you follow action without noticing the cut. Classical Hollywood perfected this “invisible” style.

  • 180-degree rule keeps eyelines and screen direction consistent
  • Match on action hides the join inside a continuous gesture
  • Shot / reverse-shot for dialogue; establishing shot then coverage
  • Eyeline match: we see someone look, then what they see

Montage (especially Soviet montage) treats the cut as a collision. Meaning arises between shots, not only inside them. Juxtaposition can argue, shock, or compress years into seconds.

  • Intellectual montage: images collide to suggest a concept
  • Metric / rhythmic cutting by duration or movement pulse
  • Parallel editing (cross-cutting) builds suspense across spaces
  • Associative montage links unlike images by theme or metaphor

Three-act beat map

One common scaffold · not a law

Many narrative features roughly follow a three-act shape. It is a teaching tool, not a rule — art cinema, episodic TV, and essay films often refuse it. Still useful when you ask: where does the story turn?

Act I

~ first 25%

Setup: world, protagonist, desire or problem. Inciting incident disrupts equilibrium. Ends near a first turning point that commits the character to the main conflict.

Act II

~ middle 50%

Confrontation: obstacles escalate. Midpoint often a false victory or irreversible revelation. Rising complications toward a crisis / low point before the final push.

Act III

~ last 25%

Resolution: climax tests the central dramatic question; falling action and denouement show the new equilibrium (or deliberate lack of one).

Sequence approach (e.g. eight sequences) chops the same journey into shorter dramatic units — handy when Act II feels like a fog. See also Watch for scene-level close reading.
