Local desk · Compare / contrast

1981 Compton and 2026 Campbell — two California homes

Educational essay · Cited public sources · Not a city ranking · Updated Sep 2026

Two incorporated California cities. Different counties, different metros, different decades, different economies — and still both places people call home. Compton in 1981 sat in the Los Angeles Basin, a Black-led inner-ring suburb about ten miles south of downtown L.A. Campbell in 2026 sits in Santa Clara County, a small Silicon Valley city next to San Jose. This page compares what the Census, a city financial report, and careful histories will support. It is not a dunk, not a stereotype tour, and not a claim that either city is a type.

How to read this

The dates are the point. 1981 is the nearest Census year plus one: April 1, 1980 is the count; 1981 is the civic year (a new Compton mayor; Campbell already a built-out orchard-to-suburb city). 2026 is the year this desk is writing. Campbell’s newest official numbers are a mix of the 2020 Census, ACS 2020–2024, Census Bureau July 2024 and July 2025 estimates, and the city’s Annual Comprehensive Financial Report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 2025. Those vintages do not match a single morning. They are named as they are used.

Compton’s 1981 numbers come from the 1980 Census as republished by HUD’s State of the Cities Data Systems (SOCDS) — race, nativity, income, poverty, housing tenure — plus city and historian accounts of who held office and what the place had been. Where two reputable printings disagree by a few dozen people, the page says so instead of picking a favorite in silence.

What this page will not do. It will not invent crime rates, test scores, or “what it felt like.” It will not treat 1980s Compton as a punchline or as the music-video city that national media later sold. N.W.A.’s Straight Outta Compton is a late-1980s record, years after 1981. Historians of Compton start earlier: a suburban dream of single-family houses and factory-adjacent work that became a Black-led city under real fiscal and racial pressure. If a claim cannot be sourced, it is cut.

Two maps, two decades

Compton is an incorporated city in Los Angeles County — “Hub City” in older booster language — on the floor of the Los Angeles Basin, south of downtown and next to the industrial and residential grid that includes Watts, Willowbrook, Lynwood, and South Gate. The city’s own demographic page gives a geographic area of about 10.2 square miles; Census QuickFacts lists 10.03 square miles of land in 2020. It is not downtown Los Angeles. It is an inner-ring suburb that, by 1981, had already lived a generation of racial succession and Black political control.

Campbell is an incorporated General Law city in Santa Clara County, contiguous with San Jose and about fifty miles south of San Francisco. The FY2025 ACFR describes 6.7 square miles, substantially built out, “in the heart of Silicon Valley,” with a mix of residential, office, and commercial retail. Census QuickFacts gives 6.08 square miles of land in 2020. The city’s official history is an orchard-city story: Benjamin Campbell’s 1851 hay-and-grain ranch, the railroad, drying yards, and the Ainsley / Hyde / Payne canneries. Incorporation came in 1952. The Pruneyard land-history desk is the sister page for the orchard-to-center conversion on Campbell and Bascom.

Neither place is a postcard of “California.” Compton 1981 is South L.A. County after white flight and before the album that made the name a global brand. Campbell 2026 is a West Valley small city inside the most expensive tech metro in the country. The shared word is still accurate: both are California municipalities where people sleep, pay rent or a mortgage, send children somewhere, and argue about the council.

Campbell · 2024–2026 vintages

~42–44k

2020 Census 43,959. July 1, 2025 estimate 42,490. City ACFR ~43,281 as of January 1, 2025. ACS 2020–2024 near 42,800.

Compton in the 1980 Census was roughly twice Campbell’s present size, on a larger footprint, in a denser basin. HUD’s 1970 figure for Compton is 78,493 — a modest 1970s gain, not a boomtown spike. Campbell’s 1980 Census count, compiled by the Metropolitan Transportation Commission / Bay Area Census, is 27,067. The orchard city had already become a tract-and-downtown suburb by the year this essay uses for Compton. The later jump to the low-forties is a 1990s–2020s story, not a 1981 one.

Compton, 1980 — race, origin, nativity

HUD SOCDS, using 1980 Census race and Spanish-origin categories, reports Compton as 73.9% Black, non-Hispanic (60,104 people), 21.1% Hispanic of any race (17,137), 2.6% White, non-Hispanic (2,104), and 2.4% other races, non-Hispanic (1,941). Those four shares are designed to add to 100. Hispanic origin is an ethnicity; it is not a fifth race to stack on top of Black and White.

That is a Black-majority city with a large and growing Mexican and Latino population — not a monolith. A CSUN history thesis on Black–Latino relations in Compton (1960–1996) uses the same 1980 Census to say the Black community was still much larger than the Mexican community, and that the 1990 Census would show a sharp relative shift. 1981 sits on the earlier side of that decade. Foreign-born residents were 10,985, or 13.5% of the city (HUD SOCDS). Most people in Compton in 1980 were U.S.-born.

Campbell, 2020–2024 — race, origin, nativity

Census QuickFacts for Campbell (ACS 2020–2024 unless noted) is a different city on every axis that table measures. White alone, not Hispanic or Latino: 44.9%. Asian alone: 25.8%. Hispanic or Latino: 19.5%. Black alone: 1.6%. Two or more races: 13.7%. White alone (including Hispanic): 48.6%. No group is a majority if you use the mutually exclusive 2020 Census race-and-ethnicity file: California Department of Finance’s 2020 redistricting profile for Campbell counts 20,855 non-Hispanic White (47.4%), 10,500 Asian (23.9%), and 8,424 Hispanic or Latino of any race (19.2%) out of 43,959.

Foreign-born persons: 32.5% (QuickFacts; Census Reporter: 13,922 ±945). Language other than English at home, age 5+: 41.1%. Campbell is a small Silicon Valley city with a large immigrant share — still below the San Jose–Sunnyvale–Santa Clara metro’s 40.8% foreign-born (Census Reporter), and more than double the U.S. rate. The 1980 Campbell Census, by contrast, was 90.3% White and 8.3% of Spanish origin (MTC / Bay Area Census). The demographic story of Campbell between 1981 and 2026 is not “unchanged suburb.” It is orchard-era White plurality giving way to a White-plurality, Asian-and-Latino city inside a global labor market.

Do not flatten either table into a moral. Compton 1980 was a Black political majority with a sizable Latino minority. Campbell 2026 is a high-income, high-foreign-born South Bay city. Those are Census facts. They are not rankings of virtue, safety, or authenticity.

Economy and work

Compton 1981 sat in an industrial-and-suburban Los Angeles basin that was about to lose more factory floors. Historian Emily E. Straus, in Death of a Suburban Dream (Penn Press, 2014), frames mid-century Compton as attractive to people who wanted single-family houses, proximity to manufacturing jobs, and a suburb ten miles from downtown — not as a place invented by later albums. The LSE review of Straus is blunt about the outsider cartoon: from afar it is easy to recite racism, white flight, disappearing working-class employment, and drugs. Urbanists closer to Los Angeles, the review says, know Compton was never defined strictly by Black poverty and was a site of successive struggles over who would govern a scarce municipal pie.

This page does not invent a 1981 Compton employment total. What it can say, with dates attached: General Motors closed the South Gate assembly plant in March 1982 — next door, not in Compton — at a cost of about 4,300 jobs (Los Angeles Times, 1985 and 1986). South Gate’s later civic memory still names GM and Firestone as the vanished anchors of that industrial belt (Times, 1995). A 1987 Times story on Compton’s own industrial parks — Xerox, Toyota, Nissan, Northrop, Ralphs Grocery headquarters, furniture, electronics, tires — is six years after 1981, and it is useful as a caution against the idea that Compton had “no economy.” The same story is also a caution against boosterism: city officials complained that many of those jobs were filled by commuters, not residents. Industry on the 91 Freeway is not the same sentence as a resident job.

Campbell 2026’s economy is the South Bay’s. The ACFR does not pretend the city is a chip fab. It describes a built-out residential/office/retail mix whose regional context is Silicon Valley: tech-sector reset after the pandemic boom, venture capital near long-run averages, AI as the latest concentration, and — the sentence the city chose to keep — “persistent wealth and income inequality, a chronic shortage of affordable housing, and stark racial disparities in health, education, and earnings.” Nearly one-third of Silicon Valley households, the ACFR’s regional narrative says, still fall below basic self-sufficiency standards. That is the city’s own finance letter, not a blog.

Census QuickFacts for Campbell (ACS 2020–2024): 68.4% of people 16+ are in the civilian labor force; mean travel time to work is 23.4 minutes; median household income is $145,268 (2024 dollars); per capita income is $85,208. Census Reporter notes that Campbell’s median household income is about 90% of the San Jose–Sunnyvale–Santa Clara metro median ($162,111) — rich by national and California standards, not the richest city in its own valley. Data USA, reading the same ACS vintage, puts about 32.4% of residents as foreign-born and flags a one-year dip in median household income from $147,128. High income here is a metro fact, not a claim that every household is comfortable.

Compton’s 1979 income, from HUD SOCDS in 2009 dollars: median household income $39,763; median family income $42,234; poverty rate 26.4%. Among households, 26.1% sat in the national lowest fifth, 60.4% in the middle three-fifths, 13.5% in the top fifth. That is a working-and-poor city with a real middle, not a cartoon of zero wages. Do not convert those 2009 dollars into 2024 dollars on this page — the conversion is not in the source table. Campbell’s 1979 median household income, in 1979 dollars, was $19,742, with 7.6% of persons below poverty (MTC 1980 Census compilation). Those two income figures are not in the same year’s dollars. The poverty rates are: Compton 26.4% (1979) versus Campbell 7.6% (1979) versus Campbell 5.6% (ACS 2020–2024). The gap that survives the unit problem is poverty and class position, not a fake inflation math.

Housing and cost of living

HUD SOCDS housing counts for Compton in 1980: 22,374 total units (excluding seasonal and migrant), 21,488 occupied, 12,565 owner-occupied — an owner rate of 58.5% — and 886 vacant. A majority-owner, mostly occupied inner-ring suburb. This page does not have a sourced 1980 median house value for Compton and will not invent one. “Affordable relative to Westside Los Angeles” is the historian’s qualitative claim (Straus’s suburban-dream frame), not a Zillow number.

Campbell’s ACS 2020–2024 housing picture is the Silicon Valley one everyone already recites, and the Census actually prints it: owner-occupied rate 51.7%; median value of owner-occupied units $1,617,000; median gross rent $2,737; median selected monthly owner costs with a mortgage $4,000+ (Census top-codes the figure). Census Reporter’s margin on the median value is ±$44,594. Homeownership is lower than Compton’s 1980 rate. That is not a morality play. It is what a $1.6 million median does to who can buy.

The FY2025 ACFR is the city’s own cost-of-living paragraph. Property-tax revenue rose with “steadily increasing housing prices.” The regional letter calls expanding affordable housing one of the most powerful tools available, notes that the pace of new housing approvals slowed, and puts the region’s homeless population above 10,500. Campbell sponsored a West Valley housing fair and an affordable-housing developer roundtable and ran code updates to support housing. The civic anxiety in the 2025 report is not a 1981-style crime brand. It is whether a small city next to San Jose can remain livable when the regional product is wealth and the regional shortage is roofs.

Two different scarcities, then. Compton 1981: a majority-Black homeowner suburb whose tax base and surrounding industrial jobs were under pressure, with a poverty rate already more than double Campbell’s at the same Census. Campbell 2026: a high-income, roughly half-renter city whose median house is more than four times the national median (Data USA / ACS: $1.62M vs $332,700) and whose city government writes “chronic shortage of affordable housing” into the annual financial report. Both are housing stories. They are not the same story.

Schools and youth — high-level only

This site now has a separate South Bay education-quality desk that starts with Campbell Union ESD (K–8). Treat the next sentences here as civic literacy, not as a ranking of Compton Unified or Campbell Union.

Straus’s book is, by design, a school-district history. The publisher’s own summary: Compton is often associated in the public mind with disinvestment, gang violence, and failing schools; before that brand, it was a suburb people chose for houses and jobs; and the fight over the school district became a symbol of the city’s economic and political crises. The argument she wants you to leave with is that the school crisis is “not, at heart, a crisis of education; it is a long-term crisis of development.” That is a historian’s thesis, not a 1981 API score. This page quotes the thesis and does not invent test results.

Campbell’s official history records that Campbell Union High School closed in 1980 and that the campus now serves as a community center. That is civic chronology for the same year as this Compton snapshot: a small city folding a historic high-school plant into civic reuse while the valley’s economy was already shifting from canneries to electronics. It is not a claim about today’s Campbell Union High School District or about whether 1980 students were “better.”

What the ACS will say about adults who live in Campbell now: 93.5% of persons 25+ are high-school graduates or higher; 60.2% hold a bachelor’s degree or higher (QuickFacts / Census Reporter). Those are resident attainment figures. They are not a grade for any school. Compton’s 1980 educational-attainment table is not opened on this page, so it is not quoted.

Youth, carefully. Both cities had and have children. Compton 1980 was a younger, poorer, Black-and-Latino city whose schools were already a political battlefield. Campbell 2026 is an older, richer, more credentialed city (median age 39.9 on Census Reporter; 20.5% under 18 on QuickFacts) whose youth story is tangled with housing cost — who can grow up here, who has to leave. No student is a demographic.

Local identity and civic story

Compton — Black political leadership, then a later brand

By 1981 Compton had been a Black-led city for more than a decade. Douglas Dollarhide, elected mayor in June 1969, was the first African American mayor of Compton (BlackPast). BlackPast, citing the 1970 Census, says African Americans were about 65% of the city by then and that Compton became the first California municipality with a Black majority. Doris A. Davis (BlackPast spells the first name Dorris) defeated him in 1973; secondary accounts treat her as the first Black woman to serve as mayor of a major U.S. city. Lionel Cade held the office through 1981. Walter R. Tucker Jr., a Compton dentist, school-board trustee, and former councilmember, defeated incumbent Cade in 1981 and served until his death in 1990 (Los Angeles Times, 1985 and 1990).

A 1972 New York Times “Talk of Compton” piece already described the city as Black-led — mayor, city manager, schools — and as a place that had been the “Hub” of white south-county suburbs and was no longer called that. The tone of that article is of its era. Use it as evidence that the civic identity of Black political control was national news nine years before 1981, not as this page’s voice.

The later national identity is music. N.W.A. formed in the mid-to-late 1980s; Straight Outta Compton is a late-1980s album (History.com’s “This Day in History” dates the mainstream arrival to 1988). That record, and the 2015 film that borrowed its name, taught a planet a version of Compton. 1981 residents were not living inside that album. They were living in a Black-majority, increasingly Latino, majority-homeowner city with a 26% poverty rate, a new dentist-mayor, and an industrial belt about to shed auto plants. BlackPast’s Dollarhide entry itself slides from 1969 into “by the 1980s… N.W.A.” — a compression this page refuses. The caricature is part of the history. It is not the 1981 Census.

Campbell — orchard, downtown, Pruneyard, small-city Silicon Valley

Campbell’s civic story, in the city’s own words, runs ranch to railroad to cannery to incorporation (1952) to a “progressive community.” Downtown still sits where Campbell Avenue meets the tracks. The official history’s 1980 note — the high school closes, the buildings become a community center — is the same calendar year as Compton’s Census. The Pruneyard, on the old Brynteson pear-and-prune ranch, had already been an open-air center for a decade; in 1980 the U.S. Supreme Court decided Pruneyard Shopping Center v. Robins. That is Campbell’s famous civic case: speech in a privately owned courtyard. The Union Avenue fire station followed in 1982. The land-history desk tells that block. This page only needs the civic moral: Campbell’s identity is orchard memory, a walkable downtown, a mall that became a legal word, and a council-manager government that now writes Silicon Valley housing anxiety into its ACFR.

The FY2025 mission line the city printed: “foster a safe, vibrant, small-town feel where innovation and people thrive.” That is branding, and it is also a tell. The anxieties a 2026 Campbell report will name are safety-as-small-town-feel, housing affordability, and regional inequality — not a national crime myth attached to the city’s name.

What is similar

Both are California places people call home. Not metaphors. Incorporated cities with councils, streets, schools, and a grocery run. The compare is only interesting if that sentence stays true.

Both live inside race-and-class geography they did not invent. Compton 1981 is what happens when a White inner-ring suburb becomes a Black-led city while surrounding cities remain harder to enter — BlackPast’s summary of white flight into towns that “often vowed to limit or exclude Black residents.” Campbell 2026 is what happens when an orchard town sits inside a metro that concentrates tech wealth and still, the ACFR says, widens racial disparities in earnings and housing. Different mechanisms. Same state habit: opportunity and exclusion share a freeway.

Both have a postcard and a lived city. Compton’s postcard, after the late 1980s, is a crime-and-rap brand that historians and local writers keep having to peel back. Campbell’s postcard is Silicon Valley small-town: downtown, the water tower, the Pruneyard courtyard. The lived Campbell in the ACFR includes a housing shortage, a homeless count in the valley above 10,500, and a median house over $1.6 million. The lived Compton in 1980 includes a 58% owner rate, a Latino minority already one-fifth of the city, and industrial employers on the freeway. The gap between image and table is the point of a compare/contrast, not a twist ending.

Both are under suburban-and-city pressure at once. Compton was already being read as “urban” while remaining a suburb of single-family streets (Straus’s refusal of the urban/suburban dichotomy). Campbell is a small city that wants a small-town feel while occupying some of the most expensive residential land in the United States. California does that to places: the legal form is a city; the regional machine is a metro; the daily life is a block.

What is different

Region and industry. Los Angeles County, 1981: basin manufacturing, ports, aerospace and auto within commuting distance, and a city whose own industrial parks still mattered even as the wider belt lost plants. Santa Clara County, 2026: tech-adjacent residential and office land, sales tax and property tax as the city’s fiscal weather, venture capital and AI as the regional story the ACFR tells. One city sat downstream of factories. The other sits downstream of equity compensation and housing scarcity.

Wealth concentration. Campbell’s ACS median household income ($145,268) and 5.6% poverty rate describe a rich small city in a richer metro — and still only about 90% of the metro median. Compton’s 1979 poverty rate (26.4%) and HUD income brackets describe a city with a majority in the national middle and a thick low-income tail. The orders of magnitude are the story. Do not turn them into a sneer at either place.

Political identity. Compton 1981 was one of the most important Black-led cities in the western United States — Dollarhide to Davis to Cade to Tucker — in a county that had not made that easy. Campbell 2026 is a five-member council-manager city that selects a mayor from the dais, a Silicon Valley suburb whose civic pride is downtown, festivals, and “small-town feel.” Those are different answers to the question “who is this city for?”

The fear that got exported. This page will not print a 1981 Compton homicide rate it did not open in a historical series. What it will say: contemporaries and later historians describe rising crime and a damaged reputation in the late 1960s and 1970s (BlackPast’s Dollarhide essay; the 1972 Times piece). The national crime narrative that attached to the word Compton hardened after 1981, especially once a late-1980s album and a generation of news copy treated the city as a genre. Campbell’s 2026 public anxieties, in the documents this desk can cite, are housing cost, regional inequality, and the wish to keep a safe small-city feel. Those are not equivalent fears. Pretending they are is how a compare becomes a smear.

The honest last sentence is small. Two California cities, forty-five years and about 350 miles of coastline apart, both real, both incomplete in any single table. Read the sources. Do not use this page as a tour of other people’s blocks.
